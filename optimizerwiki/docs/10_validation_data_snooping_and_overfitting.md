> [!abstract] Summary
> The chapter establishes why the performance of a strategy measured on historical data is a biased estimate of its future performance, and quantifies the discount to be applied. Given enough trials, a correlated signal will always be found by pure chance: the chapter formalizes this bias through the multiple-testing problem, derives the Harvey and Liu haircut Sharpe ratio, the Bailey and López de Prado deflated Sharpe ratio, and the probability of backtest overfitting of Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado, and Zhu. Two validation criteria follow: an out-of-sample test conducted with caution, and an economic rationale independent of the statistical evidence.

## The Backtest Paradox: Why the Past Deceives

The construction of a portfolio, in the preceding chapters, has been treated as an optimization problem starting from estimated moments. What remains is the question on which the whole exercise hinges: will a strategy that would have worked on past data also work in the future? The answer is far from obvious. The career of William H. Miller, manager of the Legg Mason Value Trust, illustrates the problem. From 1991 to 2005 his fund beat the market every year, an unmatched streak; in 2007–2008 it collapsed by nearly 65%, roughly twice the market decline, and investors who entered in 1991 effectively gave back all the accumulated advantage. The question the episode raises is whether the performance prior to 2007 was skill or luck [BerkDeMarzo2019]. It is not an isolated case: Forbes' Honor Roll of top funds, tracked over a 19-year period, returned 11.2% annually versus 13.1% for the market index, and the superior past performance of these funds did not prove to be a good predictor of their future ability to beat the market [BerkDeMarzo2019].

A backtest is a historical simulation of how a given strategy would have performed in the past. It is a powerful and necessary research tool, but also one that is easily manipulated [Bailey2014]. The structural reason it deceives is that random samples contain patterns: a systematic search over a large space of strategies will eventually identify one that profits from the random configuration in which the data happened to fall. When a set of parameters is optimized to maximize a backtest's performance, the resulting strategy is likely fitted to these randomnesses: this is the phenomenon of backtest overfitting [Bailey2014]. A simple example makes this transparent. Flipping a fair coin ten times may by chance produce a sequence such as $\{+,+,+,+,+,-,-,-,-,-\}$; a researcher might conclude that the optimal rule is to expect "heads" for the first five flips and "tails" for the last five (a typical "seasonal" argument). Flipping the coin ten more times will produce a different sequence, and the rule, designed to profit from a pattern present only in the past, has zero predictive power over the future, however well it appears to have worked [Bailey2014].

Competition among managers means that the signal-to-noise ratio in financial series is low, which increases the probability of "discovering" a chance configuration rather than a real signal [Bailey2014]. Because financial returns have a low signal-to-noise ratio — further lowered by competition — testing a hypothesis must be conducted on large samples, unlike what happens in sciences where signal dominates noise: Newton did not need statistical tests for the theory of gravitation [BaileyEtAl2017]. The situation is compounded by a structural phenomenon. Many financial series exhibit memory effects: variables that have built up strong tension revert to equilibrium violently, "undoing" previous patterns rather than merely "diluting" them. Since backtest overfitting tends to identify the rules that would have profited from the most extreme random patterns, and since, in the presence of memory, such extreme patterns must be undone, overfitting leads not only to a zero expected gain but to the maximization of out-of-sample loss: a circumstance that may explain why so many systematic funds do not deliver what was advertised [Bailey2014].

## Data Snooping and Selection Bias

The size effect — the observation that low-capitalization (or high book-to-market) securities show positive alphas — was documented by Banz in 1981 [Banz1981]. At the time the evidence was not convincing: financial economists had combed through the data looking for securities with positive alphas, and due to estimation error it is always possible to find some; indeed, if one searches hard enough, it is always possible to find something these securities have in common. Many researchers therefore attributed Banz's results to data-snooping bias: the idea that, given a sufficient number of characteristics, it will always be possible to find one that by pure chance turns out to be correlated with the estimation error of average returns [BerkDeMarzo2019]. The same pattern applies to the momentum strategy documented by Jegadeesh and Titman, who, ranking securities by realized returns over the preceding 6–12 months, found that the best performers had positive alphas over the following 3–12 months [JegadeeshTitman1993]. The data-snooping problem is thus intrinsic to empirical research on equity returns, and many well-established empirical anomalies — certain technical trading rules, calendar effects, and the like — are overturned once data-mining biases are accounted for [HarveyLiu2015].

The bias has a root more general than any single backtest. Researchers who conduct multiple tests on the same data tend to publish only those that pass a statistical significance test, hiding the others: since negative results go unreported, the investor is exposed to a biased sample of results. This problem, called selection bias, takes many forms: analysts who do not report the full extent of the experiments conducted (the "file drawer effect"), journals that publish only "positive" outcomes (the "publication bias"), indices that track only the performance of hedge funds that have not failed (the "survivorship bias"), managers who publish only the track record of strategies that have so far been profitable ("self-selection bias" or "backfilling") [Bailey2014]. What these phenomena have in common is that critical information is withheld from the decision-maker, with the effect of a Type I error probability much greater than expected: ignoring the full extent of the trials makes the improbable more likely [Bailey2014].

It is worth recalling the structure of a statistical test. Since every measurement carries a margin of error, choosing a statistical model is a decision under uncertainty. Two errors are distinguished: the Type I error, with probability $\alpha$ (also called the significance level), committed by choosing a strategy that should have been discarded (a "false positive"), and the Type II error, with probability $\beta$, committed by discarding a strategy that should have been chosen (a "false negative"). Standard practice fixes the Type I error probability at a low threshold (e.g. $\alpha = 5\%$) while maximizing the power of the test, defined as $1-\beta$ [Bailey2014]. The crucial point is that a 5% false-positive probability holds only when the test is applied exactly once. Applying it to the same data many times — in modern quantitative research, potentially millions or billions of times — makes the emergence of false positives nearly certain [BaileyEtAl2017]. Whoever selects the best strategy from a large number of alternatives is thus exposed to the "winner's curse" and is led to choose a strategy with an inflated Sharpe ratio; out-of-sample performance tends to disappoint, a phenomenon that in the shrinkage-estimation literature is called regression toward the mean [Bailey2014] (cf. [[08 Errore di stima, outlier e shrinkage]]). The severity of the problem has led some researchers to paraphrase Ioannidis and conclude that "most claimed research findings in financial economics are likely false" [Ioannidis2005], [HarveyLiuZhu2016].

## The Multiple-Testing Problem

When more than one hypothesis is tested, false rejections of the null hypothesis become more likely: a profitable strategy is erroneously "discovered." Methods for multiple testing serve to limit these occurrences and fall into two categories: those that control the family-wise error rate and those that control the false-discovery rate [HarveyLiu2015]. Suppose $M$ hypotheses are tested, with p-values $(p_1, p_2, \dots, p_M)$, of which $R$ are rejected: these $R$ rejections correspond to discoveries, which include both true and false discoveries (the null hypothesis being that there is no skill). Letting $N_f$ be the total number of false discoveries, the family-wise error rate (FWER) is the probability of committing at least one false discovery:
$$\mathrm{FWER} = \mathrm{Pr}(N_f \geq 1).$$
An alternative focuses on the proportion of false rejections. Letting $\mathrm{FDP}$ be the false discovery proportion,
$$\mathrm{FDP} = \begin{cases} N_f/R & \text{if } R > 0, \\ 0 & \text{if } R = 0, \end{cases}$$
the false discovery rate (FDR) is defined as $\mathrm{FDR} = E[\mathrm{FDP}]$. Both generalize the Type I error probability of a single test; procedures that control the FDR allow the number of false discoveries to grow in proportion to the total number of tests, and are therefore more permissive than those that control the FWER [HarveyLiu2015].

Harvey and Liu present three p-value adjustment procedures, following Harvey, Liu, and Zhu. Order the p-values in increasing order, $p_{(1)} \leq p_{(2)} \leq \dots \leq p_{(M)}$. The Bonferroni method corrects each p-value equally, multiplying it by the number of tests:
$$p_{(i)}^{\text{Bonferroni}} = \min[M\, p_{(i)}, 1], \qquad i = 1, \dots, M.$$
The Holm method relies on the ordered sequence and adjusts each p-value as
$$p_{(i)}^{\text{Holm}} = \min\Big[\max_{j \leq i}\{(M-j+1)\, p_{(j)}\}, 1\Big].$$
Both control the FWER, and since $p_{(i)}^{\text{Holm}} \leq p_{(i)}^{\text{Bonferroni}}$, Bonferroni is the stricter procedure: it inflates the original p-values more than Holm does. The third method, due to Benjamini, Hochberg, and Yekutieli (BHY), instead controls the FDR, defining the adjusted p-values sequentially starting from the largest:
$$p_{(i)}^{\text{BHY}} = \begin{cases} p_{(M)} & \text{if } i = M, \\ \min\Big[p_{(i+1)}^{\text{BHY}}, \dfrac{M \cdot c(M)}{i} p_{(i)}\Big] & \text{if } i \leq M-1, \end{cases}$$
with $c(M) = \sum_{j=1}^{M} \frac{1}{j}$, a choice that makes the test valid under an arbitrary dependence structure among the test statistics [BenjaminiYekutieli2001], [HarveyLiu2015]. Bonferroni and Holm guarantee the elimination of all false discoveries regardless of the number of tests: an appropriate goal for a space mission, where the failure of a component has catastrophic consequences, but managers may accept that the number of false discoveries grows with the number of tests. For financial applications it seems reasonable to control the rate of false discoveries rather than their absolute number: for this reason Harvey and Liu recommend the BHY method [HarveyLiu2015], [BenjaminiHochberg1995], [Holm1979].

## The Sharpe Ratio as a t-statistic and Its Haircut

The link between multiple testing and portfolio evaluation runs through the Sharpe ratio, the most widely used performance statistic in finance (cf. [[02 Selezione media-varianza]]). Given a sample of historical returns $(r_1, r_2, \dots, r_T)$ of a zero-cost strategy — for example a long-short strategy $r_t = R_t^L - R_t^S$ — let $\hat{\mu}$ be the sample mean and $\hat{\sigma}$ the sample standard deviation. The t-statistic for testing the null hypothesis that the mean return is zero is
$$t\text{-statistic} = \frac{\hat{\mu}}{\hat{\sigma}/\sqrt{T}},$$
and, under the assumption of i.i.d. normal returns, follows a $t$ distribution with $T-1$ degrees of freedom [HarveyLiu2015]. The Sharpe ratio is defined as $\widehat{SR} = \hat{\mu}/\hat{\sigma}$, so that
$$\widehat{SR} = \frac{t\text{-ratio}}{\sqrt{T}}.$$
For fixed $T$, a higher Sharpe ratio implies a higher t-statistic, hence a higher significance level (lower p-value): this equivalence justifies using the Sharpe ratio as a measure of a strategy's attractiveness [HarveyLiu2015]. For lower-frequency returns, if $\hat{\mu}$ and $\hat{\sigma}$ are monthly mean and volatility, the annual Sharpe ratio is $12\hat{\mu}/(\sqrt{12}\,\hat{\sigma}) = \sqrt{12}\,\hat{\mu}/\hat{\sigma}$ [HarveyLiu2015].

Transform the Sharpe ratio into the t-statistic, and this into the p-value. For a single test,
$$p^S = \mathrm{Pr}(|r| > t\text{-ratio}) = \mathrm{Pr}(|r| > \widehat{SR}\cdot\sqrt{T}),$$
where $r$ is a $t$ variable with $T-1$ degrees of freedom; the focus is on a two-sided alternative since a long-short strategy can be profitable with either a positive or a negative mean [HarveyLiu2015]. But if the researcher has tried $N$ strategies and presents the most profitable one, the p-value of the single test greatly overstates its significance. Assuming $N$ independent tests, under the null hypothesis that none generates nonzero returns, the probability of observing a maximum t-statistic at least as large as the one observed is
$$p^M = \mathrm{Pr}\big(\max\{|r_i|,\, i=1,\dots,N\} > t\text{-ratio}\big) = 1 - \prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{Pr}(|r_i| \leq t\text{-ratio}) = 1 - (1 - p^S)^N.$$
For $N = 1$, $p^M = p^S$, no adjustment; but with $N = 10$ and $p^S = 0{,}05$ one obtains $p^M = 0{,}401$, that is, roughly a 40% probability of finding by chance a strategy with an equally high t-statistic [HarveyLiu2015]. Equating the p-value of a single test to $p^M$ defines the haircut Sharpe ratio $\widehat{HSR}$:
$$p^M = \mathrm{Pr}(|r| > \widehat{HSR}\cdot\sqrt{T}).$$
Since $p^M > p^S$, $\widehat{HSR} < \widehat{SR}$. The proportional discount is $hc = (\widehat{SR} - \widehat{HSR})/\widehat{SR}$ [HarveyLiu2015]. An example: over twenty years of monthly returns ($T = 240$), an annual Sharpe ratio of $0{,}75$ gives $p^S = 0{,}0008$; with $N = 200$ tests one obtains $p^M = 0{,}15$, which implies an adjusted annual Sharpe of $0{,}32$, that is, a discount of about 60% [HarveyLiu2015].

The common practice of discounting the reported Sharpe ratio by 50% is, according to Harvey and Liu, merely a rule of thumb and a serious mistake: the multiple-testing discount is nonlinear. The highest Sharpe ratios are penalized only moderately, while marginal ones are penalized heavily. This makes economic sense: strategies with very high Sharpe ratios are probably real discoveries, while marginal strategies should be discarded because they are the most likely false positives [HarveyLiu2015]. In the example with three strategies (E/P, momentum, and Frazzini and Pedersen's betting-against-beta), with $N = 50$ tests the discount is nearly 50% for the least profitable E/P strategy (annual Sharpe $0{,}43$), but only $7{,}9\%$ for the most profitable BAB strategy (annual Sharpe $0{,}78$): the discount is not uniform across different levels of the Sharpe ratio [HarveyLiu2015], [FrazziniPedersen2014]. In general, for annual Sharpe ratios below $0{,}4$ the discount is almost always greater, sometimes far greater, than 50%, while for Sharpe ratios above $1{,}0$ it is at most 25%: 50% is too lenient for small Sharpes and too harsh for large ones [HarveyLiu2015]. The problem can also be posed in reverse: given a significance level, what is the minimum average return a proposed strategy must exceed? With 240 observations and 10% annual volatility, the minimum monthly average return for a single test is $0{,}365\%$ (about $4{,}4\%$ annually), but under BHY it rises to $0{,}616\%$ (about $7{,}4\%$ annually) [HarveyLiu2015].

## The Deflated Sharpe Ratio

A second approach directly corrects the Sharpe ratio for the entire testing context. Consider $N$ independent backtests associated with a class of strategies (for example, Discretionary Macro), each with an estimated Sharpe ratio $\widehat{SR}_n$, $n = 1, \dots, N$. Assume that the $\widehat{SR}_n$ follow a distribution with mean $E[\{\widehat{SR}_n\}]$ and variance $V[\{\widehat{SR}_n\}]$ — a reasonable assumption, since a "class of strategies" implies a common pattern. The expected maximum over $N$ independent trials is approximated as
$$E[\max\{\widehat{SR}_n\}] \approx E[\{\widehat{SR}_n\}] + \sqrt{V[\{\widehat{SR}_n\}]}\left((1-\gamma)\,Z^{-1}\!\left[1-\tfrac{1}{N}\right] + \gamma\,Z^{-1}\!\left[1-\tfrac{1}{N\,e}\right]\right),$$
where $\gamma \approx 0{,}5772$ is the Euler-Mascheroni constant, $Z$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function, and $e$ is Euler's number [Bailey2014]. The derivation is direct: given a set of normal variables $y_n \sim \mathcal{N}[\mu, \sigma^2]$, the standardization $x_n = (y_n - \mu)/\sigma$ preserves order, so $\max\{y_n\} = \mu + \sigma\max\{x_n\}$, and by linearity of the expectation operator $E[\max\{y_n\}] = \mu + \sigma E[\max\{x_n\}]$; extreme value theory provides the approximation of $E[\max\{x_n\}]$ for large $N$ [Bailey2014]. The consequence is clear-cut: as the number of independent trials $N$ grows, the expected maximum of the Sharpe ratios grows too, even in the total absence of skill ($E[\{\widehat{SR}_n\}] = 0$, $V[\{\widehat{SR}_n\}] > 0$). It is thus unsurprising to obtain good backtests as more candidates are examined: it is a pure consequence of random behavior [Bailey2014].

On this basis Bailey and López de Prado construct the deflated Sharpe ratio (DSR). It relies on the probabilistic Sharpe ratio (PSR), which computes the probability that the true Sharpe ratio exceeds a given threshold, accounting for sample length and the first four moments of the return distribution, so as to correct the inflationary effect of short and non-normal samples [BaileyLopezdePrado2012]. The DSR is a PSR in which the rejection threshold is set equal to the expected maximum under the null hypothesis $H_0: SR = 0$:
$$\widehat{DSR} = \widehat{PSR}(\widehat{SR}_0) = Z\!\left[\frac{(\widehat{SR} - \widehat{SR}_0)\sqrt{T-1}}{\sqrt{1 - \hat{\gamma}_3\,\widehat{SR} + \frac{\hat{\gamma}_4 - 1}{4}\,\widehat{SR}^2}}\right],$$
where the threshold is $\widehat{SR}_0 = \sqrt{V[\{\widehat{SR}_n\}]}\left((1-\gamma)Z^{-1}[1-\tfrac{1}{N}] + \gamma Z^{-1}[1-\tfrac{1}{N e}]\right)$, $T$ is the length of the series, $\hat{\gamma}_3$ the skewness, and $\hat{\gamma}_4$ the kurtosis of the returns of the selected strategy [Bailey2014]. While the standard Sharpe ratio is a function of only two estimates, mean and standard deviation, the DSR discounts the Sharpe ratio by accounting for five additional variables: the non-normality of returns ($\hat{\gamma}_3, \hat{\gamma}_4$), the length of the series $T$, the variance of the tested Sharpe ratios $V[\{\widehat{SR}_n\}]$, and the number of independent trials $N$ [Bailey2014].

A numerical example clarifies how it works. A strategist searches for seasonal patterns in the Treasury market by combining pre- and post-auction periods, maturities, holding periods, and stop-losses; he finds that many configurations yield an annualized Sharpe of $2$, with one in particular reaching $2{,}5$ over five years of daily data. Convinced that a Sharpe of $2{,}5$ is certainly significant, he seeks capital. The informed investor asks him to disclose: the number of independent trials $N = 100$, the variance of the backtests $V[\{\widehat{SR}_n\}] = 1/2$, the length $T = 1250$, and the moments $\hat{\gamma}_3 = -3$, $\hat{\gamma}_4 = 10$. This yields a threshold $\widehat{SR}_0 \approx 0{,}1132$ (non-annualized, with 250 observations per year) and
$$\widehat{DSR} \approx Z\!\left[\frac{\left(\tfrac{2{,}5}{\sqrt{250}} - 0{,}1132\right)\sqrt{1249}}{\sqrt{1 - (-3)\tfrac{2{,}5}{\sqrt{250}} + \tfrac{10-1}{4}\left(\tfrac{2{,}5}{\sqrt{250}}\right)^2}}\right] = 0{,}9004 < 0{,}95,$$
so that there is only a 90% probability that the true Sharpe ratio is greater than zero: below 95%, the investor declines. Had the returns been normal ($\hat{\gamma}_3 = 0$, $\hat{\gamma}_4 = 3$), he would have accepted up to $N = 88$ trials; the example shows that it is critical to account jointly for the two sources of inflation, selection bias and non-normality [Bailey2014].

The practical problem of establishing $N$ remains. The relevant number is that of independent trials, not of total trials $M$: using $M$ in place of $N$ would overestimate $E[\max\{SR_n\}]$, since typically $N < M$ due to correlation among the trials. Letting $\rho$ be the average off-diagonal correlation of the $M \times M$ correlation matrix among the trials,
$$\rho = \frac{2\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{j=i+1}^{M}\rho_{i,j}}{M(M-1)},$$
and since a proper correlation matrix is positive definite, $\rho$ is bounded to $\rho \in \left(-\tfrac{1}{M-1}, 1\right]$. When $\rho \to 1$, $N \to 1$; when $\rho \to 0$, $N \to M$; interpolating between these two extremes yields the implied number of independent trials
$$\bar{N} = \hat{\rho} + (1-\hat{\rho})M.$$
Correlation thus reduces the effective number of independent tests [Bailey2014]. The same role played here by $E[\max\{SR_n\}]$ is played, in the Harvey and Liu framework, by the adjusted p-value threshold; the two methods are complementary, and the DSR can also be computed on the Harvey and Liu threshold [Bailey2014], [HarveyLiu2015].

## The Probability of Backtest Overfitting

The deflated Sharpe ratio and the haircut correct a performance statistic. A third approach, due to Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado, and Zhu, instead directly evaluates the selection process: what is the probability that the configuration that turned out best in-sample is overfit? For overfitting to occur, the configuration offering the maximum in-sample (IS) performance must systematically underperform the other configurations out-of-sample (OOS); typically the reason is that the "optimal" IS strategy is so tied to the noise of the training set that further optimization becomes useless or even harmful to signal extraction [BaileyEtAl2017]. Formally, given a sample space of pairs of IS and OOS samples and $N$ strategies, consider the rank space $\Omega$ consisting of the $N!$ permutations of $(1, 2, \dots, N)$. The selection process is said to overfit if the strategy with optimal IS performance has an expected rank below the OOS median. Defining $\bar{r}_n$ as the OOS rank and $\Omega_n^* = \{f \in \Omega \mid f_n = N\}$ as the event that strategy $n$ is the best IS, the probability of backtest overfitting is
$$PBO = \sum_{n=1}^{N} \mathrm{Prob}\big[\bar{r}_n < N/2 \mid r \in \Omega_n^*\big]\,\mathrm{Prob}[r \in \Omega_n^*]:$$
the probability that the strategy selected as optimal IS falls below the OOS median of all strategies [BaileyEtAl2017]. Here "in-sample" denotes the subset of observations used to select the optimal strategy among the $N$ alternatives, not the period over which the underlying model is estimated: overfitting concerns the selection process, not the calibration of the model, and for this reason it can be defined without knowing the underlying models, in a model-free and nonparametric way [BaileyEtAl2017].

To estimate the PBO the authors propose combinatorially symmetric cross-validation (CSCV). A matrix $M$ is formed by collecting the performance series of the $N$ trials: each column is a vector of profits and losses over $t = 1, \dots, T$ observations, so $M$ is of order $(T \times N)$. It is partitioned by rows into an even number $S$ of disjoint submatrices $M_s$ of equal size, and all their combinations taken in groups of $S/2$ are formed, numbering
$$\binom{S}{S/2} = \prod_{i=0}^{S/2-1}\frac{S-i}{S/2-i}.$$
For each combination $c$ the training set $J$ is formed by joining the $S/2$ submatrices that compose it, and the test set $\bar{J}$ as its complement. The best IS strategy $n^*$ on $J$ is determined and its OOS rank on $\bar{J}$ is measured; the relative rank $\bar{\omega}_c = \bar{r}_{n^*}^c/(N+1) \in (0,1)$ is transformed into the logit $\lambda_c = \ln\frac{\bar{\omega}_c}{1-\bar{\omega}_c}$. High logit values indicate consistency between IS and OOS performance, hence low overfitting. Collecting all $\lambda_c$ yields the distribution of relative frequencies $f(\lambda)$, and the PBO is the mass to the left of zero:
$$\phi = \int_{-\infty}^{0} f(\lambda)\,d\lambda,$$
that is, the frequency with which the optimal IS strategy underperforms the median of the OOS trials [BaileyEtAl2017]. Consistent with the Neyman-Pearson framework, models for which the estimated PBO exceeds $0{,}05$ can be rejected [BaileyEtAl2017]. With $S = 16$, $12\,870$ logits are generated, and for a four-year backtest on daily data $S = 16$ corresponds to quarterly partitions, thus preserving the serial correlation structure: a reasonable value in most cases [BaileyEtAl2017].

The framework also provides a direct reading of performance degradation. For each combination the pair $(R_{n^*}, \bar{R}_{n^*})$ of the IS and OOS Sharpe ratios of the selected strategy is recorded; while $R_{n^*}$ is the maximum among the IS values, $\bar{R}_{n^*}$ is not necessarily the OOS maximum, and in general $\bar{R}_{n^*} < \max\{\bar{R}\}$. Regressing $\bar{R}_{n^*}^c = \alpha + \beta R_{n^*}^c + \varepsilon^c$, the slope $\beta$ turns out negative in most cases: overfit backtests minimize future performance, because the model is so fitted to past noise as to become unfit for future signal, and the more a backtest is overfit, the more memory accumulates against its future performance [BaileyEtAl2017]. A useful statistic is the proportion of combinations with negative performance, $\mathrm{Prob}[\bar{R}_{n^*}^c < 0]$; note that even with $\phi \approx 0$ this probability can be high, in which case OOS performance is poor for reasons other than overfitting [BaileyEtAl2017]. In one example, with IS Sharpe ratios all positive and ranging between $1$ and $3$, about 78% of the OOS Sharpes turn out negative and the PBO is 74%: a high IS Sharpe ratio, by itself, says nothing about the representativeness of the result. A contrasting real case instead shows a loss probability of about 3% and a PBO of $0{,}04\%$, indicating a backtest that is not significantly overfit [BaileyEtAl2017]. A further analysis checks stochastic dominance: if the distribution of OOS Sharpes of the optimal IS configurations does not dominate, not even to second order, that of all configurations, this is a clear sign of overfitting [BaileyEtAl2017].

## Implications for Model Builders

The information these tools require converges on one point: the single most important piece of information, almost always missing from published backtests, is the number of trials attempted. Without it, it is impossible to assess the relevance of a backtest; a backtest for which the researcher has not tracked the extent of the search is worthless, however excellent the reported performance may appear, and investors and referees should demand this information [Bailey2014]. The usual disclaimer that "past performance does not guarantee future results" is too lenient when, in the joint presence of selection bias and overfitting, adverse outcomes are actually quite likely [Bailey2014].

The first validation criterion is an out-of-sample test, conducted however with full awareness of its limitations. The holdout method — splitting the sample into an in-sample subset to estimate the model and an out-of-sample subset to validate its generality, possibly repeated via k-fold cross-validation — does not prevent overfitting: it assesses generality as if a single trial had taken place, ignoring the growth of false positives as the number of trials increases. Applying the holdout a sufficient number of times (twenty times for a 95% confidence level), false positives are no longer improbable: they are expected. Validation techniques are useful against the hypothesis suggested by the data (so-called Type III errors), but they do not control for backtest overfitting [Bailey2014]. The holdout is also inadequate for small samples, and has high estimation variance: the section chosen as holdout might be exactly the one that refutes a valid strategy or supports an invalid one, and different holdouts lead to different conclusions [BaileyEtAl2017].

Even a genuine OOS test has intrinsic limitations, underscored by Harvey and Liu. First, an OOS test may not really be out-of-sample: a researcher tries a strategy, finds it fails, revises it, and tries again hoping it will work; this trial-and-error process is not truly OOS, but the difference is hard to detect for an outside observer. Second, like any statistical test, an OOS test holds only in a probabilistic sense: its success may be due to luck, both in the in-sample selection and in the out-of-sample test. Third, since the researcher has already lived through the data period, there is no true OOS using historical data, especially when the strategy involves economic variables: no one can ignore what has happened in the economy [HarveyLiu2015]. There is also a trade-off between Type I and Type II errors tied to sample splitting: withholding data for OOS increases the probability of missing true discoveries in the shorter in-sample data set [HarveyLiu2015]. A genuine OOS test, on previously unpublished data or on uncorrelated markets, remains the cleanest way to assess a strategy's viability, but for most strategies it is not available [HarveyLiu2015].

The second criterion concerns how many trials to attempt and on what justification. Multiple testing is a useful tool that should not be abused: experiments must be carefully planned to avoid conducting an unnecessarily large number of them, and it is investment theory, not computing power, that should motivate which experiments are worth conducting [Bailey2014]. Each additional trial irretrievably increases the probability of a false positive; one answer to the question of how many trials to attempt comes from optimal stopping theory (the "secretary problem," or the $1/e$ law of optimal choice): from the set of theoretically justifiable configurations one randomly samples a fraction $1/e$ (about 37%), measuring their performance, and then continues drawing configurations one by one until one is found that beats all previous ones [Bailey2014]. Consistently, a p-value for a single test makes sense only if the researcher is strongly motivated by an economic theory and directly constructs empirical proxies to test its implications; it makes no sense when the researcher has explored hundreds or thousands of strategies and presents only the most profitable one [HarveyLiu2015]. The economic rationale of a strategy indeed appears among the evaluation criteria alongside the Sharpe ratio, the significance level, and the drawdown [HarveyLiu2015]. This ties statistical analysis to the rest of the monograph: the empirical evidence of a signal gains credibility only when accompanied by an explanation of the risk that signal compensates for — the same logic by which the positive alphas of size-, book-to-market-, and momentum-based strategies find a reading as premia for risks not captured by the CAPM rather than as risk-free opportunities [BerkDeMarzo2019] (cf. [[05 Modelli fattoriali]], [[09 Selezione dei titoli su fondamentali]]). The economic rationale must therefore be independent of the statistical evidence the strategy generates, and not deduced ex post from the backtest it is meant to validate (cf.
