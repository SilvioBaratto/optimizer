> [!abstract] Summary
> The chapter shows how textual, attention, and behavioral information — financial news, lexical dictionaries, aggregate sentiment indices, Google searches, moods extracted from social media, and large-scale news analytics — are transformed into quantitative signals for the cross-section and time series of equity returns. The connecting thread is the empirically testable distinction between the sentiment hypothesis, which predicts transitory price pressure followed by reversal, and the information hypothesis, which predicts a permanent impact. It closes with the aggregation of the many correlated and noisy signals via machine learning methods for measuring the risk premium.

## Sentiment, Information, and the Two Testable Hypotheses

In classical asset pricing theory, investor sentiment plays no role in the cross-section of prices, realized returns, or expected returns [BakerWurgler2006]. For sentiment to matter, two joint ingredients are needed: on one hand, investors whose demand is driven by beliefs not justified by the facts, and on the other, limits to arbitrage that prevent rational operators from promptly correcting mispricing [DeLongEtAl1990; ShleiferVishny1997]. Consistently, sentiment can be defined as "a belief about future cash flows and investment risks that is not justified by the facts at hand" [BakerWurgler2007].

This framework provides a diagnostic criterion that organizes the entire empirical program of the chapter. The sentiment theory predicts that short-horizon returns induced by a signal will be reversed in the long run; the information theory instead predicts that they will persist indefinitely [Tetlock2007]. The temporal signature of a signal — pressure followed by reversal, or lasting impact — thus becomes the test that determines whether a given textual, attention, or behavioral datum conveys noise or news. The sections that follow repeatedly apply this criterion: to media pessimism and retail attention, which turn out to be transitory; to large-scale processed news and underreaction to bad news, which turn out to be more lasting.

The same criterion guides the construction of the signals. Since sentiment acts where arbitrage is costly and valuation is difficult, its effects are expected to concentrate on speculative securities that are hard to value and to arbitrage, and attention signals are expected to produce temporary pressure on securities held by individual investors. The chapter's operational question is not only "does the text predict returns?", but "through which channel, with what sign, and for how long?"

## From Text to Number: Content Analysis and Dictionaries

Turning text into a number requires classifying words into categories and counting them. The methodological principle is that "content analysis stands or falls by its categories" [Berelson1952]: the choice of vocabulary determines the quality of the signal. A first approach applies a predefined psycholinguistic dictionary. The media pessimism measure is constructed by applying principal components analysis to the counts of the 77 categories of the General Inquirer (Harvard IV-4 dictionary) detected in a daily Wall Street Journal column; the first factor, interpreted as pessimism, loads on the Negative, Weak, Fail, and Fall categories [Tetlock2007]. In this way an entire day's text is synthesized into a single indicator projected onto the dimension of maximum joint variance of the counts.

A dictionary built for a different domain, however, systematically misclassifies in a financial setting. Over a large sample of 10-K annual reports, it is observed that 73.8% of occurrences of words classified as negative by the Harvard H4N list are not actually negative in a financial context: terms such as "tax," "cost," "capital," "board," "liability," "foreign," and "vice" express neutral or technical content [LoughranMcDonald2011]. Hence the construction of domain-specific dictionaries: a list of financially negative-tone words (Fin-Neg, 2,337 words) and auxiliary lists for positive tone, uncertainty, litigious language, and strong and weak modal words. The lesson is that the specificity of the categories precedes any statistical sophistication.

A second choice concerns the weighting of terms in the bag-of-words model. Proportional weighting assigns each word a weight equal to its relative frequency; but the frequency distribution is strongly unequal and a few common words dominate the counts [Zipf1949]. tf.idf weighting corrects this imbalance by combining the term's frequency in the document with its rarity in the corpus:
$$w_{i,j} = \begin{cases} \dfrac{1+\log(tf_{i,j})}{1+\log(a_j)}\cdot \log\dfrac{N}{df_i} & \text{if } tf_{i,j}\ge 1\\[2mm] 0 & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases}$$
where $tf_{i,j}$ is the number of occurrences of term $i$ in document $j$, $a_j$ the average term frequency in document $j$, $N$ the number of documents, and $df_i$ the number of documents containing term $i$ [LoughranMcDonald2011]. The first factor normalizes the term's frequency relative to the document's length; the second, the idf factor, depresses ubiquitous words and boosts rare, specific ones. As will be seen, tf.idf weighting changes the significance of the textual signals, confirming that the measure is not neutral with respect to the result.

## Media Sentiment and Short-Term Predictability

The media pessimism measure is inserted into a vector autoregressive model that, estimated by least squares, is equivalent to a Granger causality test [Granger1969]:
$$Dow_t = \alpha_1 + \sum_{i=1}^{5}\beta_{1i}\,Dow_{t-i} + \sum_{i=1}^{5}\gamma_{1i}\,Pess_{t-i} + \sum_{i=1}^{5}\delta_{1i}\,Vlm_{t-i} + \lambda_1\,Exog_{t-1} + \varepsilon_{1t},$$
where $Dow_t$ is the index return, $Pess_t$ pessimism, $Vlm_t$ volume, and $Exog$ a set of calendar and macroeconomic controls [Tetlock2007]. A one-standard-deviation increase in pessimism is followed by downward pressure of about $-8{,}1$ basis points on the next day's return; over days 2 to 5 a reversal of about $6{,}8$ basis points is recorded, so that the cumulative effect, about $-1{,}3$ basis points, is not significantly different from zero. The signal thus produces temporary price pressure that is entirely reabsorbed, the signature predicted by sentiment theory and noise-trading models [DeLongEtAl1990].

Two collateral results reinforce this interpretation. First, unusually high or low values of pessimism — that is, large deviations in absolute value — precede high trading volume, consistent with the idea that a departure from the normal tone triggers trading. Second, pessimism predicts a negative return of the SMB size factor, signaling that the downward pressure is concentrated on small-cap securities, typically held by individual investors [Tetlock2007]. The trading strategy based on the signal generates a gross return on the order of 7% per year, but transaction costs erode its profitability, making the signal more relevant as scientific evidence than as a trading opportunity.

At the individual security level, the proportion of financially negative-tone words in a 10-K predicts negative abnormal returns around the filing date, with a t-statistic of $-2{,}64$, net of controls for size, book-to-market, momentum, and other characteristics [LoughranMcDonald2011]. Here weighting matters decisively: the Fin-Neg list is significant already under proportional weighting, while the Harvard H4N list is significant only when tf.idf weighting is adopted. The tone measure thus depends jointly on the choice of dictionary and the weighting scheme, and the sign of the signal — bad news anticipating low returns — is consistent with that observed at the index level.

## The Aggregate Sentiment Index and the Cross-Section

A complementary approach builds a top-down sentiment index, aggregating market indicators rather than text. Six variables are combined: the closed-end fund discount (CEFD), share turnover (TURN), the number of IPOs (NIPO), the first-day return of IPOs (RIPO), the equity share in new issues (S), and the dividend premium (PDND). The closed-end fund discount has been interpreted as a sentiment measure since the literature on the related puzzle [LeeShleiferThaler1991]. Extracting the first principal component, with each indicator lagged when it leads the others, gives
$$SENT_t = -0{,}241\,CEFD_t + 0{,}242\,TURN_{t-1} + 0{,}253\,NIPO_t + 0{,}257\,RIPO_{t-1} + 0{,}112\,S_t - 0{,}283\,PDND_{t-1},$$
which explains about 49% of the variance; orthogonalizing the proxies first with respect to industrial production, consumption, employment, and recessions yields the index $SENT^{\perp}$, purged of the macroeconomic component, which explains about 53% [BakerWurgler2006].

The effects on the cross-section are formalized by a conditional characteristics model,
$$E_{t-1}[R_{it}] = a + a_1\,T_{t-1} + b_1'\,x_{i,t-1} + b_2'\,T_{t-1}\,x_{i,t-1},$$
where $T_{t-1}$ is beginning-of-period sentiment and $x_{i,t-1}$ a vector of firm characteristics: the interaction term $b_2'$ captures the dependence of cross-sectional premia on sentiment [BakerWurgler2006]. Empirical evidence, obtained both with double sorts and with predictive regressions on long-short portfolios of the type
$$R_{X_{it}=High,t} - R_{X_{it}=Low,t} = c + d\,SENTIMENT_{t-1} + \beta\,RMRF_t + s\,SMB_t + h\,HML_t + m\,UMD_t + u_{it},$$
shows that when sentiment is high, future returns are relatively low for securities that are young, small, volatile, unprofitable, non-dividend-paying, high-growth, and distressed — that is, hard to value and to arbitrage — and that these patterns attenuate or reverse when sentiment is low [BakerWurgler2006]. For several growth and distress variables the conditional relationship takes a U-shape: it is the securities with extreme values, not intermediate ones, that react to sentiment. Consistently, the size effect [Banz1981] emerges only in low-sentiment periods, and the predictive coefficient of sentiment on SMB is about $-0{,}40$ per point (one standard deviation) of the index.

Two checks rule out that these effects are compensation for classical systematic risk. On one hand $SENT^{\perp}$ is orthogonal to macroeconomic conditions; on the other, estimating time-varying market betas, the composite coefficient that should reconcile the results with a conditional CAPM turns out, when significant, to have typically the wrong sign [BakerWurgler2006]. On the expectations side, returns around earnings announcements tend to be lower after periods of high sentiment — the majority of estimated coefficients are negative — consistent with a correction of expectational errors, in the spirit of the results on value stocks [BakerWurgler2006]. Complementarily, stocks' sentiment betas increase with their degree of speculativeness, and when sentiment is high the most speculative stocks earn low future returns [BakerWurgler2007].

## Attention as a Direct Signal: Google Searches

Sentiment measures tone; attention instead measures how much a stock is the object of investor interest, and is a distinct channel. Google Search Volume (Search Volume Index, SVI) provides a direct, revealed measure of retail investor attention, as an alternative to indirect proxies such as turnover, news coverage, or advertising [DaEngelbergGao2011]. The measure is deseasonalized and detrended by constructing abnormal search volume,
$$ASVI_t = \log(SVI_t) - \log\!\big[\text{Med}(SVI_{t-1},\dots,SVI_{t-8})\big],$$
that is, the difference between the log of current volume and the log of its median over the preceding eight weeks [DaEngelbergGao2011]. That attention is conceptually distinct from sentiment is confirmed by the fact that the correlation between SVI and sentiment extracted from news is only on the order of 1.4–2.3%.

The economic mechanism is that of attention-induced price pressure: individual investors, facing the enormous number of securities, tend to buy those that attract their attention, generating temporary net buying and thus overvaluation that is reabsorbed over time [BarberOdean2008]. The data confirm this signature: a one-standard-deviation increase in ASVI is associated with an aggregate positive return of over 30 basis points over the following two weeks (about 18.7 basis points in the first week and 14.9 in the second), followed by a reversal within the year; the effect is stronger for small stocks more heavily traded by retail investors [DaEngelbergGao2011]. In the case of IPOs, high pre-listing attention precedes a higher first-day return — on the order of 16.98% versus 10.90% — and long-run underperformance, consistent with temporary overvaluation fueled by attention. Retail attention thus falls, like media pessimism, on the transitory pole of the sentiment-information dichotomy, but its measure isolates a specific, direct behavioral channel.

## Collective Moods from Social Media

Social media allow the measurement not only of tone polarity, but of more articulated collective emotional states. From 9.85 million tweets collected during 2008, two measures of public mood are extracted: a bipolar positive/negative indicator (OpinionFinder) and a multidimensional profile (GPOMS) that decomposes mood into six dimensions — Calm, Alert, Sure, Vital, Kind, Happy [BollenMaoZeng2011]. Each mood series is standardized relative to a local window,
$$Z_{X_t} = \frac{X_t - \bar{x}(X_{t\pm k})}{\sigma(X_{t\pm k})},$$
where $\bar{x}$ and $\sigma$ are the mean and standard deviation computed over a window of width $\pm k$ around date $t$ [BollenMaoZeng2011].

Predictive power is assessed with Granger causality tests, comparing an index-return model based on its own lags alone,
$$D_t = \alpha + \sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_i\,D_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t,$$
with one that adds the lags of a mood dimension,
$$D_t = \alpha + \sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_i\,D_{t-i} + \sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_i\,X_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t.$$
The result is selective: only the Calm dimension Granger-causes the return of the Dow Jones index, with lags of 2 to 6 days and significance levels below 5%, while neither the bipolar indicator nor the other GPOMS dimensions show predictive power [BollenMaoZeng2011]. Feeding the Calm dimension as an input to a self-organizing fuzzy neural network (SOFNN), the accuracy in predicting the index's direction rises to about 87.6% with a reduced mean percentage error. Once again the theme of categories emerges: it is not generic polarity that matters, but a specific emotional dimension, reaffirming that the choice of what is measured is an integral part of the signal.

## Large-Scale News Analytics: News Versus Sentiment

At industrial scale, the sentiment-information dichotomy can be subjected to a direct test. An archive of 900,754 Reuters articles, linked to firm identifiers over the period 2003–2010, is classified by a sentiment engine based on a three-layer neural network trained on a sample of 3,000 triple-annotated articles, which returns for each news item the probabilities of being positive, negative, or neutral [HestonSinha2016]. The central result concerns the aggregation horizon. If sentiment is measured day by day, predictability lasts only one or two days — post-publication returns are about 0.17% on day 1 and 0.04% on day 2 — in line with earlier literature [Tetlock2007; LoughranMcDonald2011]. If instead news is aggregated on a weekly basis, predictability extends up to 13 weeks, that is, an entire quarter, with a decile spread in the announcement week of 3.75%. The longer duration indicates that the neural network extracts permanent information not yet incorporated into prices, rather than mere transitory sentiment or a liquidity effect.

An essential methodological step is controlling for the very existence of the news. Firms with news have different returns from firms without news: like "the dog that didn't bark," the mere fact that an article is published or not contains information [FangPeress2009]. Neutral news outperforms the absence of news, and this positive premium contradicts the adage that no news is good news [CampbellHentschel1992]. To isolate the effect of tone, a Fama-MacBeth cross-sectional regression is adopted,
$$r_{i,t} = \alpha_{k,t} + \gamma_{k,t}\,\mathbf{1}_{news,\,t-k} + \beta_{k,t}\,Positive_{i,t-k} + \delta_{k,t}\,Negative_{i,t-k} + \epsilon_{i,t},$$
where $\mathbf{1}_{news}$ isolates the publication effect, distinguishing it from the effects of positive and negative sentiment for each lag $k$ from 0 to 13 [HestonSinha2016; FamaMacBeth1973].

A clear asymmetry emerges. Positive sentiment has a strong contemporaneous effect but is incorporated into prices within about a week, with lagged coefficients close to zero; negative sentiment, instead, predicts low returns for the entire quarter, with significant coefficients at lags 1 to 6 and at lag 10 [HestonSinha2016]. This slow reaction to bad news is consistent with short-selling constraints and with the idea that bad news travels slowly [HongLimStein2000]. Much of the delayed reaction is concentrated around subsequent earnings announcements: spreads in the announcement week reach 5.57% (t-statistic 2.7) and remain positive afterward (1.83%, t-statistic 2.1), consistent with post-earnings-announcement drift [FosterOlsenShevlin1984; BernardThomas1990]. Earnings announcements thus act as a price-discovery channel for information not immediately incorporated at publication.

## Aggregating the Signals: Machine Learning in Asset Pricing

The signals described so far — textual tone, attention, mood, news — are numerous, noisy, and mutually correlated: exactly the setting in which machine learning methods offer an advantage. Measuring the risk premium is, at bottom, a prediction problem, since the premium is the conditional expectation of the future excess return:
$$r_{i,t+1} = E_t(r_{i,t+1}) + \epsilon_{i,t+1}, \qquad E_t(r_{i,t+1}) = g^{\star}(z_{i,t}),$$
where $g^{\star}(\cdot)$ is a flexible function of the predictor vector $z_{i,t}$, held identical over time and across securities [GuKellyXiu2020]. The set of predictors is large: 94 firm characteristics interacted with eight macroeconomic variables plus a constant, and 74 sector indicators, for a total of $94\times(8+1)+74 = 920$ base signals across nearly 30,000 stocks between 1957 and 2016.

The canon of methods is compared on this set. OLS regression with all predictors fails, producing an out-of-sample $R^2$ of $-3{,}46\%$ monthly due to over-parameterization; regularization saves it. The elastic net penalizes the objective function with
$$\phi(\theta;\lambda,\rho) = \lambda(1-\rho)\sum_{j=1}^{P}|\theta_j| + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda\rho\sum_{j=1}^{P}\theta_j^2,$$
combining selection (lasso, $\rho=0$) and shrinkage (ridge, $\rho=1$); dimension reduction via principal components (PCR) and partial least squares (PLS) brings the out-of-sample $R^2$ to about 0.26–0.27% [GuKellyXiu2020; KellyPruitt2013]. The mean squared loss can be made robust against the fat tails of returns with the Huber function,
$$H(x;\xi) = \begin{cases} x^2 & |x|\le \xi\\ 2\xi|x| - \xi^2 & |x| > \xi, \end{cases}$$
and the hyperparameters are chosen on a validation sample distinct from the estimation and test samples. Performance is assessed with an out-of-sample $R^2$ measured against a value of zero, not against the historical mean of returns:
$$R^2_{oos} = 1 - \frac{\sum_{(i,t)}(r_{i,t+1} - \hat{r}_{i,t+1})^2}{\sum_{(i,t)} r_{i,t+1}^2}.$$

Non-linear methods dominate. Regression trees, random forests, and boosting achieve monthly $R^2$ on the order of 0.33–0.34%, and neural networks reach 0.33–0.40%, with a multi-layer architecture defined recursively by $x_k^{(l)} = \text{ReLU}\big(x^{(l-1)\prime}\theta_k^{(l-1)}\big)$ [GuKellyXiu2020]. Notably, "shallow" learning prevails: the three-layer network (NN3) achieves the maximum, while deeper networks do not improve, owing to data scarcity and the low signal-to-noise ratio typical of the problem. That the gains are not an artifact of micro-cap illiquidity is confirmed by the fact that the non-linear methods excel even on large, liquid stocks. The source of the advantage is the ability to capture non-linearities and, above all, interactions among predictors, as shown by the marginal associations and interactions: for example, the short-term reversal effect is linear among small stocks but concave among large ones, and the size effect is stronger when aggregate valuations are low (high book-to-market) and equity issuance is low.

On the economic side, the gains are large. Using the relation between predictability and the Sharpe ratio of an active investor,
$$SR^{\ast} = \sqrt{\frac{SR^2 + R^2}{1-R^2}},$$
a market-timing strategy on the S&P 500 index based on the neural network's forecasts raises the annualized Sharpe ratio from 0.51 to 0.77 [GuKellyXiu2020; CampbellThompson2008]. A value-weighted long-short decile spread, sorted on the network's predictions, achieves an annualized Sharpe ratio of 1.35 (2.45 equal-weighted), against 0.61 and 0.83 for the three-characteristic linear benchmark model, with alphas significant relative to a five-factor model augmented by momentum. Finally, all methods agree on a narrow set of dominant signals: price trends — short-term reversal, momentum, industry momentum — followed by liquidity measures (market capitalization, dollar volume, Amihud illiquidity, bid-ask spread) and volatility measures (total and idiosyncratic volatility, beta). Machine learning thus resolves the multiple-comparisons and collinearity problem afflicting the "factor zoo" [HarveyLiuZhu2016], but provides measurement, not mechanism: the identified associations do not by themselves identify the underlying equilibrium relationships.

## Measurement, Arbitrage, and Persistence: An Overall Picture

The signals examined are arranged along the continuum traced by the sentiment-information dichotomy. Media pessimism [Tetlock2007] and retail attention revealed by Google searches [DaEngelbergGao2011] produce temporary price pressure followed by reversal, the signature of sentiment; large-scale news processed by a neural network [HestonSinha2016] and the persistent underreaction to bad news instead show lasting impact, the signature of information. The same conceptual framework, developed from the limits to arbitrage [ShleiferVishny1997] and from noise-trading models [DeLongEtAl1990], explains why sentiment effects concentrate on securities that are hard to value and to arbitrage [BakerWurgler2006] and why the reaction to bad news is slowed by short-selling constraints [HongLimStein2000].

The recurring methodological theme is that measurement is not neutral. Categories determine the signal [Berelson1952], both in the choice of the pessimism factor [Tetlock2007] and in the selection of the relevant emotional dimension [BollenMaoZeng2011]; dictionaries must be domain-specific [LoughranMcDonald2011]; term weighting changes significance; controlling for attention and for publication is necessary so as not to confound the news effect with the tone effect [HestonSinha2016]; orthogonalization with respect to macroeconomic variables isolates the properly sentiment-related component [BakerWurgler2006]. Each of these choices is as much a part of the signal as the model that uses it.

Machine learning represents the frontier of aggregation: it absorbs hundreds of heterogeneous, correlated signals into a single risk-premium forecasting model, with substantial statistical and economic gains that are robust to the choice of horizon [GuKellyXiu2020]. Yet even this synthesis confirms that the dominant signals remain price trends, liquidity, and volatility, within which sentiment, attention, and text enter as incremental, conditioning information. The root of the problem is that risk premia are difficult to measure because market efficiency ensures that return variation is dominated by unpredictable news: alternative data and aggregation methods improve the measurement of the premium, reducing approximation and estimation error, but leave open the question of the underlying economic mechanisms.
