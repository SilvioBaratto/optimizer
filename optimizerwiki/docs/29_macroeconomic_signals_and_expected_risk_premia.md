> [!abstract] Summary
> The chapter establishes which macroeconomic and financial variables predict the expected risk premia of stocks and bonds and how they must be validated before being used. It shows that the slope of the yield curve predicts recessions and real growth, that credit spreads purged of expected loss isolate a cyclical excess bond premium, that scaling exposure inversely with expected variance expands the mean-variance frontier, and that a few dynamic factors extracted from many series predict bond premia in a counter-cyclical way. It further establishes the mandatory reality check: most equity predictors are weak and unstable out of sample, and it is theoretical constraints on the sign of coefficients, on the non-negativity of the premium, and on valuation anchors that make them exploitable. These conditional expected returns are the input that the chapter [[30 Dalle previsioni condizionate ai pesi]] translates into weights.

## Conditional Expected Returns: Why and How to Look for Them

The mean-variance construction of the chapter [[02 Selezione media-varianza]] takes as data the vector of expected returns and the covariance matrix. Up to this point these moments have been treated as constants or estimated from sample averages; the literature on return predictability instead argues that the expected risk premium varies systematically over time, linked to the business cycle, and that certain observable variables — the shape of the yield curve, credit spreads, asset variance, broad macroeconomic aggregates — capture part of this variation. A *conditional* expected return $E_t[r_{t+1}]$ is a function of the information available at $t$; if this function is non-trivial, an investor can tilt the allocation toward assets when their expected premium is high and reduce it when it is low.

The standard testing ground is the predictive regression
$$ r_{t+1} = \alpha + \beta\, x_t + \varepsilon_{t+1}, $$
where $r_{t+1}$ is the asset's excess return (equity premium, bond excess return) and $x_t$ is the predictor known at $t$; the null hypothesis of no predictability is $\beta = 0$ [NeelyRapachTuZhou2014]. As argued in [[10 Validazione, data snooping e overfitting]], in-sample significance is not enough: the decisive criterion is out-of-sample performance, measured by the out-of-sample $R^2$ statistic
$$ R^2_{OS} = 1 - \frac{\sum_{t}\big(r_{t+1}-\hat r_{t+1}\big)^2}{\sum_{t}\big(r_{t+1}-\bar r_{t+1}\big)^2}, $$
in which $\hat r_{t+1}$ is the model's forecast with parameters estimated recursively on data up to $t$, and $\bar r_{t+1}=\tfrac{1}{t}\sum_{s=1}^{t} r_s$ is the historical mean, a very stringent benchmark [CampbellThompson2008; NeelyRapachTuZhou2014]. A value $R^2_{OS}>0$ means that the predictor has lower mean squared error than the historical mean; a negative value — frequent — means that the investor would have done better to ignore the signal. Since estimating an additional coefficient introduces noise, under the null the expected value of $R^2_{OS}$ is slightly negative, so that even a null $R^2_{OS}$ is already weak evidence in favor of predictability [CampbellThompson2008].

This chapter examines macroeconomic signals one by one, for stocks and bonds, and closes with the reality check on their out-of-sample fragility. The resulting conditional expected returns are the input for the chapter [[30 Dalle previsioni condizionate ai pesi]], which derives the mechanics of the weights from them; the same indicators measure the latent regimes treated in [[21 Regimi di mercato e formazione delle view]].

## The Slope of the Yield Curve and the Probability of Recession

The most robust macroeconomic predictor is the slope of the term structure, that is, the *spread* between a long-term and a short-term rate,
$$ SPREAD_t = R^{L}_t - R^{S}_t, $$
defined by [EstrellaMishkin1998] as the yield on the ten-year Treasury note minus the yield on the three-month bill (quarterly averages, with no information lag). A flat or inverted curve ($SPREAD_t \le 0$) tends to anticipate a real slowdown.

The link with the probability of recession is formalized with a probit model. Let $R_{t+k}$ be an indicator variable equal to $1$ if the economy — according to the official business-cycle dating — is in recession $k$ quarters after $t$ and $0$ otherwise. The model sets
$$ \Pr\!\big(R_{t+k}=1 \,\big|\, X_t\big) = F\!\big(\alpha_0 + \alpha_1\, X_t\big), $$
where $F(\cdot)=\Phi(\cdot)$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function and $X_t = SPREAD_t$; the parameters are estimated by maximum likelihood. The reference four-quarter estimate, on quarterly data from 1960 to 1995, is
$$ \Pr\!\big(R_{t+4}=1\big) = F\!\big(-0{,}66 - 0{,}81\, SPREAD_t\big), $$
with pseudo-$R^2 = 0{,}296$ and a strongly significant coefficient on the spread ($t$-statistic $\approx -4{,}99$) [EstrellaMishkin1998]. The negative coefficient implies that a lower spread raises the estimated probability of recession: an average spread of $0{,}76$ percentage points corresponds to a probability of $10\%$, a spread of $-0{,}82$ to a probability of $50\%$, a spread of $-2{,}40$ to a probability of $90\%$. The goodness of the model is assessed with the pseudo-$R^2$ built on the log-likelihood,
$$ \text{pseudo-}R^2 = 1 - \Big(\tfrac{\log L_u}{\log L_c}\Big)^{-\frac{2}{n}\log L_c}, $$
where $L_u$ is the likelihood of the estimated model and $L_c$ that of the constant-only model, and above all with the ability to signal recessions out of sample, with recursive estimation on a growing window.

The authors compare the spread with a set of other leading indicators: equity indices, nominal and real monetary aggregates, individual interest rates, the commercial paper–Treasury spread, real indicators, and composite indices of leading indicators. No indicator dominates at all horizons, but the slope of the curve is the clear choice beyond two quarters and up to about four to six quarters ahead; at very short horizons (one to two quarters) equity indices and composite leading-indicator indices turn out to be competitive or superior. Out of sample the superiority of the spread grows with the horizon, and several predictors that are strong in-sample — such as the commercial paper–Treasury spread — exhibit negative out-of-sample pseudo-$R^2$: a reminder of the divergence between the two assessments. The only family of variables that consistently increases the predictive power of the spread beyond the first quarter is equity indices; adding a third regressor generally worsens performance, hence the lesson of parsimony. The recession probability thus obtained is a direct input for the regime-view formation of the chapter [[21 Regimi di mercato e formazione delle view]].

## The Term Structure as a Predictor of Real Activity

The yield curve does not only predict the binary "recession" event, but future real growth in continuous form. [EstrellaHardouvelis1991] show that the slope — the same spread between the ten-year yield and the three-month rate, $SPREAD_t = R^L_t - R^S_t$ — anticipates the cumulative real growth of output and its components. The cumulative dependent variable is the annualized percentage change of real output from $t$ to $t+k$,
$$ Y_{t,\,t+k} \equiv \frac{400}{k}\,\big[\log(y_{t+k}/y_t)\big], $$
and alongside it the marginal change from $t+k-j$ to $t+k$ is considered, $Y_{t+k-j,\,t+k}\equiv \frac{400}{j}\log(y_{t+k}/y_{t+k-j})$; cumulative growth is the average of consecutive marginal changes. The baseline specification regresses cumulative growth on the current spread,
$$ Y_{t,\,t+k} = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1\, SPREAD_t + \sum_i \beta_i X_{it} + \epsilon_t, $$
estimated on quarterly data from 1955 to 1988; since overlapping horizons generate moving-average errors, standard errors are corrected using the Newey–West method.

The estimation produces positive and significant $\alpha_1$ coefficients: a steeper curve announces higher future real growth. At four quarters the coefficients are $\alpha_0 = 1{,}70$ and $\alpha_1 = 1{,}30$, so that a current spread of 100 basis points predicts annual real growth of about $1{,}70\% + 1{,}30\times 1\% = 3\%$. The cumulative predictive power extends for about four years, the marginal power for about six to seven quarters; in-sample accuracy is highest at five to seven quarters, where the spread alone explains more than a third of the future variation in output ($\bar R^2 \approx 0{,}38$ at $k=5,6$). The signal holds for all private demand components — consumption (nondurables and services), consumer durables, and investment, with larger coefficients for durables and investment — but **not** for government spending, whose coefficient is small and not significant at any horizon.

The authors subject the spread to an incremental information-content test: they insert it into regressions that already control for the real federal funds rate, the leading indicators index, lagged real growth, and lagged inflation. The spread retains a significant coefficient up to about three years, a sign that it conveys information about future real activity that these variables do not replicate and that is not reducible to current monetary policy alone; it also beats survey forecasts, both in-sample and out-of-sample. Even in probit form, with the recession indicator $X_t$ regressed on the spread lagged four quarters,
$$ \Pr\!\big(X_t=1 \mid SPREAD_{t-4}\big) = F\!\big(-0{,}56 - 0{,}78\, SPREAD_{t-4}\big), \qquad \text{pseudo-}R^2 = 0{,}297, $$
adding the four supplementary variables raises the pseudo-$R^2$ only to $0{,}321$, while they alone reach barely $0{,}205$: the spread dominates. For a multi-asset investor the slope is thus a parsimonious leading indicator of the phase of the cycle, complementary to the recession probit signal.

## Credit Spreads, Expected Loss, and the Excess Bond Premium

Credit spreads — the difference between the yield of a corporate bond and that of a government security of the same maturity — are classic cyclical predictors, but crude: they mix compensation for expected default loss with a residual reflecting risk appetite. [GilchristZakrajsek2012] construct an accurate spread and decompose it into the two parts.

For each senior unsecured bond $k$ of non-financial issuer $i$ they avoid maturity mismatch by constructing a **synthetic** government security that exactly replicates the cash flows $\{C(s)\}$ of the corporate security: given the price $P_{it}[k]=\sum_s C(s)\,D(t_s)$ with discount function $D(t)=e^{-r_t t}$, they discount the same cash flows with the Treasury zero-coupon curve, obtaining the yield $y^f_t[k]$. The credit spread on the individual bond is
$$ S_{it}[k] = y_{it}[k] - y^{f}_{t}[k], $$
and the GZ index is the arithmetic average across all outstanding bonds, $S^{GZ}_t = \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_i\sum_k S_{it}[k]$. Default risk is measured with a structural, Merton-inspired distance-to-default [Merton1974], in which equity is a call option on firm value $V$; with an annual horizon,
$$ DD_{it} = \frac{\ln\!\big(V_{it}/D_{it}\big) + \big(\mu_{V,it} - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}_{V,it}\big)}{\sigma_{V,it}}, $$
where $V$ and its volatility $\sigma_V$ are derived iteratively from the value and volatility of equity, and $D$ (the default point) is set equal to current liabilities plus half of long-term liabilities; the implied default probability is $\Phi(-DD)$.

The spread (in logarithm, due to strong asymmetry) is projected on default risk and on issue characteristics,
$$ \ln S_{it}[k] = \beta\, DD_{it} + \gamma'\, Z_{it}[k] + \epsilon_{it}[k], $$
with $Z$ the bond's characteristics (duration, amount, coupon, age, callability indicator) plus sector and rating fixed effects. The predicted spread for the individual bond, with the log-normal correction, is $\hat S_{it}[k]=\exp\!\big[\hat\beta\, DD_{it}+\hat\gamma' Z_{it}[k]+\hat\sigma^2/2\big]$, and its average $\hat S^{GZ}_t$ is the expected-loss component. The aggregate residual defines the *excess bond premium*,
$$ EBP_t = S^{GZ}_t - \hat S^{GZ}_t, $$
that is, the part of the average spread not justified by measured default risk; the regression has high $R^2$ (adjusted $R^2 \approx 0{,}65$–$0{,}70$).

The authors show that the excess bond premium is strongly cyclical and predicts future economic activity — consumption, business fixed investment, industrial production, employment — with coefficients systematically larger in absolute value than those of the expected-loss component: the information in spreads resides largely in the non-default part. In the sub-period from the mid-1980s onward, essentially **all** of the predictive content of the GZ spread for output growth is attributable to the $EBP$. In a VAR, an unexpected one-standard-deviation increase in the $EBP$ (about 20 basis points) causes prolonged declines in consumption, investment, and output, and a cumulative equity market decline on the order of $7\%$. The interpretation is that the $EBP$ measures the effective risk-bearing capacity of financial intermediaries: when their capital deteriorates they become more risk-averse, the $EBP$ rises, and the supply of credit contracts, consistent with the co-variation of the $EBP$ with the tightening of lending standards and with broker-dealer CDS spreads. It is therefore a risk-premium signal common to corporate bonds and equities, and a natural ingredient for the systemic-risk reading of the chapter [[28 Turbulence, absorption ratio e rischio sistemico]].

## Volatility as a Timing Signal

A signal of a different nature arises not from the level but from the variance of assets. [MoreiraMuir2017] propose managing exposure to a risk factor in inverse proportion to its expected variance. The motivation comes from mean-variance choice: the optimal weight on the risky portfolio is proportional to the risk-return trade-off, $w^{*}_t \propto E_t[f_{t+1}]/\hat\sigma^{2}_t(f)$; since volatility is highly variable and persistent but does not predict return, the ratio is approximated by treating the numerator as nearly constant. Given a factor with excess return $f_{t+1}$, the *volatility-managed* portfolio is
$$ f^{\sigma}_{t+1} = \frac{c}{\hat\sigma^{2}_t(f)}\, f_{t+1}, $$
where the constant $c$ is chosen so that the managed strategy and the original one have the same unconditional variance (it does not affect the Sharpe ratio), and expected variance is estimated from the realized variance of daily returns over the preceding month, $\hat\sigma^{2}_t(f) = RV^{2}_t = \sum_{d} f^{2}_{t,d}$, without parameter estimation and in real time. The rule is mechanical: it raises exposure after calm months, reduces it after turbulent months.

Evaluation proceeds by regressing the managed strategy on the original factor,
$$ f^{\sigma}_{t+1} = \alpha + \beta\, f_{t+1} + \varepsilon_{t+1}. $$
The authors find positive and significant intercepts $\alpha$ for the market factor (about $4{,}9\%$ annually, with $\beta \approx 0{,}6$) and for a broad set of factors — value, momentum (the largest $\alpha$), profitability, investment, currency carry — with the exception of the size factor. An $\alpha>0$ means that the managed strategy expands the mean-variance frontier; its magnitude is measured by the *appraisal ratio* $\alpha/\sigma(\varepsilon)$, which for the market is about $0{,}33$ annually. Combining the managed strategy with the original one takes the Sharpe ratio from $S_{old}$ to
$$ S_{new} = \sqrt{S_{old}^{2} + \big(\alpha/\sigma_\varepsilon\big)^{2}}, $$
an increase of about $25\%$ for the market, and the utility gain for a mean-variance investor,
$$ \Delta U_{MV} = \frac{S_{new}^{2} - S_{old}^{2}}{S_{old}^{2}}, $$
reaches about $65\%$ for someone who can trade only the market, roughly twice what the expected-return predictability literature obtains from timing the premium.

The reason the result is surprising is that it rests on an asymmetry: sorting next month's returns by the previous month's realized volatility reveals a weak relation between past volatility and average return but a strong relation between past volatility and current variance. Because the ratio $E_t[f]/\sigma^2_t$ worsens when variance rises without the expected return compensating, reducing exposure in months of high expected variance lowers risk more than it sacrifices return, and raises the Sharpe ratio; the $\alpha$ precisely measures the negative co-variation between the price of risk and variance. The strategy reduces exposure during recessions and after crashes — the opposite of what risk-based explanations would predict — and survives realistic transaction costs. This timing signal complements the level predictors and connects with the dynamic covariance estimation of the chapter [[18 Stima dinamica della covarianza - EWMA e GARCH multivariato]].

## Compressing Many Series into a Few Dynamic Factors

A macroeconomic dashboard contains dozens or hundreds of series — output, employment, prices, monetary aggregates, orders — too many to enter directly into a predictive regression without exhausting the degrees of freedom. [LudvigsonNg2009] solve the problem by compressing a panel of $132$ macroeconomic series into a few common dynamic factors. Given a matrix of standardized series $x_{it}$, they posit the factor structure
$$ x_{it} = \lambda_i'\, f_t + e_{it}, $$
where $f_t$ is a reduced vector ($r \ll N$) of common latent factors, $\lambda_i$ the loadings, and $e_{it}$ the idiosyncratic components with limited cross-sectional correlation. The factors $\hat f_t$ are estimated by principal components, in the spirit of diffusion indices [StockWatson2002]; information criteria indicate $r = 8$ factors, of which the first explains $17{,}7\%$ of the panel's variance and the eight together about $50\%$.

The factors then enter a regression of the $n$-year excess bond return $rx^{(n)}_{t+1}$,
$$ rx^{(n)}_{t+1} = \alpha'\, \hat F_t + \beta'\, Z_t + \epsilon_{t+1}, $$
where $Z_t$ contains the Cochrane–Piazzesi forward-rate factor $CP_t$, a linear combination of five forward spreads [CochranePiazzesi2005]. The composition of $\hat F_t$ is selected by minimizing the BIC criterion over a restricted set of specifications, also allowing non-linear terms: the preferred subset is $(\hat F_{1t}, \hat F_{1t}^{3}, \hat F_{2t}, \hat F_{3t}, \hat F_{4t}, \hat F_{8t})$, where $\hat F_{1t}^3$ is the cube of the first factor; when $CP_t$ is included, the factor $\hat F_{2t}$ — highly correlated with rate spreads — drops out, because its information is already contained in $CP_t$. Summarizing the predictive factors into a single linear index $F5_t$, obtained as the fitted value of the regression of the average excess return on the factors, the first factor — a "real factor" that loads on employment and production — has the largest coefficient in absolute value and is the single most important one, followed by the interest-rate factor and the equity-market factor, while the inflation factors turn out to be relatively less important.

The results show that the macro factors possess information content about bond premia that adds to that of $CP_t$: alone they explain between $21\%$ and $26\%$ of the one-year variation in excess returns, and together with $CP_t$ they bring the $\bar R^2$ to about $44\%$ for the two-year bond, against $31\%$ for $CP_t$ alone. The predictive information is **not** in the yield curve, which is ruled out by related unrestricted term-structure models. Above all, the risk premia thus estimated are strongly counter-cyclical: the real factor correlates over $90\%$ with industrial production growth, and since the return forecast is high when it is low, premia turn out to be high in recessions and low in expansions; when macro information is ignored, the same premia appear nearly acyclical. The result holds out of sample, with mean squared error equal to $79\%$–$93\%$ of that of the constant-expected-return benchmark. This is an example of the factor models of the chapter [[05 Modelli fattoriali]] applied not to returns but to their predictors.

## Combining Many Forecasts: Macro and Technical Predictors

If many predictors are each weak, an effective strategy is to combine their forecasts rather than choose one. [NeelyRapachTuZhou2014] apply this idea to forecasting the equity premium by joining two families of signals: classic macro-financial variables and technical indicators. The macro base is a set of fourteen variables — valuation ratios (dividend-price, dividend yield, earnings-price, payout, book-to-market), premium volatility, net equity issuance, the short rate, the long yield, the long return, the term spread, the default yield and default return spread, inflation. The technical indicators are $14$ mechanical trend rules: six moving averages, which generate the binary signal
$$ S_{i,t} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \text{MA}_{s,t}\ge \text{MA}_{l,t}\\ 0 & \text{if } \text{MA}_{s,t}< \text{MA}_{l,t}\end{cases}, \qquad \text{MA}_{j,t}=\frac{1}{j}\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}P_{t-i}, $$
two momentum rules ($S_{i,t}=1$ if $P_t \ge P_{t-m}$), and six on-balance-volume rules, in which the signed volume $OBV_t=\sum_k VOL_k D_k$ (with $D_k=\pm 1$ depending on the sign of the price change) is filtered by the same moving averages.

To combine the many signals without over-parameterizing, the authors extract principal components from the sets of predictors and use them as regressors in diffusion-index form,
$$ r_{t+1} = \alpha + \sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_k\, \hat F^{j}_{k,t} + \varepsilon_{t+1}, \qquad j \in \{\text{ECON},\ \text{TECH},\ \text{ALL}\}, $$
where $\hat F^{\text{ECON}}$ are the first three components of the $14$ macro predictors, $\hat F^{\text{TECH}}$ the first component of the $14$ technical ones (which loads nearly uniformly on all of them, hence a "consensus" average), and $\hat F^{\text{ALL}}$ the first four components of the union of the $28$ predictors. The key result is in-sample additivity: the $R^2$ of PC-ALL ($2{,}02\%$) equals the sum of PC-ECON ($1{,}18\%$) and PC-TECH ($0{,}84\%$), a sign that macro and technical predictors capture **complementary** information. Out of sample PC-ALL reaches an $R^2_{OS}$ of $1{,}79\%$, higher than that of either family in isolation and than simple combinations, with significance confirmed also by a reality check against data mining.

Predictability is strongly counter-cyclical: technical indicators better capture the decline of the premium near cycle peaks, macro variables the rebound near troughs, and PC-ALL, combining them, has an $R^2_{OS}$ of $11{,}24\%$ in recessions against negative values in expansions. The signal translates into gains for a mean-variance investor who sets the equity weight at $w_t = \frac{1}{\gamma}\,\hat r_{t+1}/\hat\sigma^2_{t+1}$: the certainty-equivalent gain of PC-ALL is about $4{,}9\%$ annually (over $4\%$ net of costs), with the highest Sharpe ratio. That the combination of forecasts beats the single model and the historical mean in a stable and business-cycle-linked way is a general result of the forecast-combination literature [RapachStraussZhou2010]. The techniques for fusing heterogeneous signals used here connect to the alternative-data signals of the chapter [[25 Segnali da dati alternativi e sentiment]].

## The Reality Check: Out-of-Sample Fragility and Theoretical Constraints

The preceding survey must be read in light of a stern warning. [WelchGoyal2008] conduct a systematic examination of equity premium predictors — valuation ratios, rates, spreads, net issuance, equity variance, the investment-capital ratio, the consumption-wealth ratio $cay$ — comparing their in-sample and out-of-sample performance using the regression $\text{Equity Premium}(t)=\gamma_0+\gamma_1 x(t-1)+\epsilon(t)$. Their central diagnostic tool is the cumulative difference in squared forecast errors between the historical-mean model (the null) and the predictive model (the alternative),
$$ \Delta_t = \sum_{s\le t}\big(r_{s}-\bar r_{s-1}\big)^2 - \sum_{s\le t}\big(r_{s}-\hat r_{s}\big)^2, $$
tracked over time: an upward stretch indicates the predictor beats the mean, a downward stretch the opposite. The conclusion is that for most variables $\Delta_t$ does not grow stably; in-sample predictability does not survive out of sample, coefficients are unstable, and much of the apparent success is concentrated in a few episodes — in particular the 1973–1975 oil shock — only to vanish afterward. In many cases the out-of-sample $\bar R^2$ is negative: the historical mean is unbeatable, and the models predict well at the start of the sample and poorly at the end, the opposite of a simple lack of power. The formal statistics used include the out-of-sample $R^2$ and McCracken's MSE-F. This is the mandatory reality check before turning a signal into a position, and it reinforces the message of [[10 Validazione, data snooping e overfitting]] and of [[08 Errore di stima, outlier e shrinkage]].

[CampbellThompson2008] respond that predictors become useful if theoretically motivated restrictions are imposed, instead of estimating them freely. The key restrictions are of two types. The first are sign and non-negativity constraints: if the coefficient $\hat\beta_t$ has the opposite sign to the one expected it is set to zero (the forecast reverts to the historical mean), and if the premium forecast turns out negative it is truncated,
$$ \hat r_t^{\,restr} = \max\!\big(0,\ \hat\alpha_t + \hat\beta_t\, x_t\big). $$
The second is anchoring to a steady-state valuation: starting from Gordon's growth identity $D/P = R - G$ [Gordon1962] and the relation $G = (1 - D/E)\,ROE$ between growth and accounting profitability, the expected return is built from the current valuation ratio by anchoring it to historical average payout and $ROE$, for example from the earnings yield
$$ \hat R_{EP} = \Big(\frac{D}{E}\Big)\frac{E}{P} + \Big(1 - \frac{D}{E}\Big) ROE, $$
and in the most restrictive version the intercept is fixed at zero and the slope at one, without using the estimated covariance between returns and valuation ratios [FamaFrench2002]. With these restrictions many predictors beat the historical mean out of sample: the restrictions "never hurt and almost always improve" performance, and the fixed-coefficient specification improves every monthly regression, with small but positive $R^2_{OS}$ (on the order of fractions of a percentage point per month).

The authors finally show that small $R^2_{OS}$ values are nonetheless economically relevant. For a mean-variance investor who observes the predictor, the expected return gain relative to one who does not observe it is $\frac{1}{\gamma}\frac{R^2}{1-R^2}(1+S^2)$, and the **proportional** increase in expected portfolio return is
$$ \Big(\frac{R^2}{1-R^2}\Big)\Big(\frac{1+S^2}{S^2}\Big), $$
where $S$ is the unconditional Sharpe ratio: the right yardstick for judging $R^2$ is thus the **squared Sharpe ratio** $S^2$. With a monthly equity Sharpe ratio of about $0{,}11$ ($S^2 \approx 1{,}2\%$), a monthly $R^2_{OS}$ close to $0{,}43\%$ produces a proportional increase in portfolio return of about a third ($0{,}43/1{,}2 \approx 36\%$), economically significant. The joint lesson of [WelchGoyal2008] and [CampbellThompson2008] is that macroeconomic signals must be used with constraints — coefficient signs, non-negative expected premium, valuation anchors — that stabilize their estimation and make them exploitable. It is precisely these conditional, constrained expected returns that the chapter [[30 Dalle previsioni condizionate ai pesi]] translates into portfolio weights.
