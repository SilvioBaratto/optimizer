> [!abstract] Summary
> The chapter establishes that the optimal factor-timing portfolio coincides with the stochastic discount factor and that market-neutral equity factors are strongly and robustly predictable, with substantial gains relative to a static exposure. It also presents the skeptical position, however: real predictability remains hard to monetize net of costs and estimation error, extrapolating past performance over multi-year horizons is a trap, and relative valuation is the most consistent predictor, though not the only one. The verdict is balanced: timing is possible at the margin, but chasing recent performance is dangerous, while discipline is to be sought in valuation and in the absence of near-arbitrage.

## From Factor Cyclicality to the Timing Question

Factor performance is strongly time-varying. Small-cap and value stocks performed disappointingly in the second half of the 1990s, while technology growth stocks reached stratospheric valuations; both styles were then rewarded after the tech bubble burst, through the middle of the 2000s, until value fell out of favor again from 2007; high-quality and low-volatility stocks were the best factors in the last five years of the sample, despite having performed poorly in the last bull market, which ended in 2008 [BenderEtAl2017]. The measure of this cyclicality — the rolling three-year excess returns of the factor indices over the market — shows wide swings and alternations of sign across size, value, momentum, quality and low volatility.

Why do factor returns vary over time? The simplest answer rests on an anthropic principle: without this temporal variation in the payoff, the underlying phenomenon would quickly be arbitraged away, and the factors would not exist at all [BenderEtAl2017]. To understand the currents that stir realized returns, [BenderEtAl2017] decomposes the premium of each factor into three ingredients: (1) compensation for risk exposure; (2) the return that originates from the irrationality of market participants; (3) the effects of market frictions. Each of these has its own dynamics and its own drivers: as the level of (and tolerance for) a specific source of risk, the degree to which markets over- or under-react, and the intensity with which frictions distort price discovery vary, the premium moves accordingly. A limit of diversification follows: it tends to disappoint precisely when it would be needed most, in environments driven by top-down forces in which correlations between factors rise. On the positive side, the richness of these dynamics offers the opportunity to improve on a static factor allocation.

The observation that signals work differently across market states, each source defining the state in its own way while a single shared regime remains a hypothesis (see [Chapter 26: Market States and the Cross-Section](26_market_states_and_cross_section.md)), thus leads to a normative question: is it possible to *time* factor exposure, improving the performance of a fixed-weight basket? The question is open and draws reactions ranging from complete futility to unbridled enthusiasm [BenderEtAl2017]. There is also a decisive observation: even the manager who adopts fixed nominal weights is in fact "timing" the factors, because — as correlations fluctuate around their long-run averages — effective weights and exposures swing by far from negligible amounts, and does so in a less informed way than an explicit forecasting approach could achieve [BenderEtAl2017]. Similarly, [ArnottBeckKalesnik2016] observes that we are all market and factor timers: whoever selects strategies, funds or managers on the basis of past performance is already timing factors, but does so in the self-defeating way of performance chasing.

## The Optimal Factor-Timing Portfolio Is the Stochastic Discount Factor

The economic importance of factor timing emerges once one recognizes that the optimal factor-timing portfolio is equivalent to the stochastic discount factor (SDF): if timing matters for the optimal portfolio, it must be taken into account in estimating the SDF [HaddadKozakSantosh2020]. Factor timing unifies two ideas: aggregate returns are predictable over time [Shiller1981], creating room for market timing; factors beyond the market are sources of risk premia in the cross-section [FamaFrench1993], the basis of factor investing.

Start from the minimum-variance SDF in the span of the $`N`$ excess returns $`R_{t+1}`$ [HansenJagannathan1991]:

$$m_{t+1} = 1 - b_t'\left(R_{t+1} - \mathbb{E}_t[R_{t+1}]\right),$$

which satisfies the fundamental relation $`0 = \mathbb{E}_t[m_{t+1}R_{t+1}]`$ when $`b_t = \Sigma_{R,t}^{-1}\mathbb{E}_t[R_{t+1}]`$, with $`\Sigma_{R,t}`$ the conditional covariance matrix of the returns, assumed invertible. The contribution of [HaddadKozakSantosh2020] is to restrict the behavior of the loading $`b_t`$. Cross-sectional heterogeneity in the risk prices $`b_t`$ is assumed to be captured by $`K`$ observable characteristics $`C_t`$, with $`K \ll N`$ (Assumption 1):

$$\underbrace{b_t}_{N\times 1} = \underbrace{C_t}_{N\times K}\,\underbrace{\delta_t}_{K\times 1},$$

where $`\delta_t`$ is a vector of possibly time-varying coefficients. Substituting gives an alternative representation of the SDF in terms of characteristic-managed factor portfolios $`F_{t+1} = C_t' R_{t+1}`$:

$$m_{t+1} = 1 - \delta_t'\left(F_{t+1} - \mathbb{E}_t[F_{t+1}]\right).$$

If a characteristic is market capitalization, the corresponding factor is the market return; if it is a $`\{-1,0,1\}`$ indicator on the extreme quantiles, the factor is a Fama-French-style sort-based portfolio. In this reading $`\delta_t`$ are the time-varying risk prices on the factors.

A conditional factor model follows from this structure (Lemma 1). For an excess return, $`\mathbb{E}_t[R_{j,t+1}] = -\text{cov}_t(m_{t+1}, R_{j,t+1}) = \text{cov}_t(R_{j,t+1}, F_{t+1}')\,\delta_t = \beta_{jt}'\Sigma_{F,t}\,\delta_t`$; applying the same relation to the factors yields $`\mathbb{E}_t[F_{t+1}] = \Sigma_{F,t}\,\delta_t`$, that is $`\delta_t' = \mathbb{E}_t(F_{t+1})'\Sigma_{F,t}^{-1}`$, and therefore

$$\mathbb{E}_t[R_{j,t+1}] = \beta_{jt}'\Sigma_{F,t}\,\delta_t = \beta_{jt}'\,\mathbb{E}_t[F_{t+1}].$$

This relation shows that the model generates variation in risk premia even in a homoskedastic setting, in which $`\Sigma_{F,t}`$ and the $`\beta_{jt}`$ are constant: it is $`\delta_t`$ that governs how the SDF loads on the factors, changing their price of risk. Because the factors fully capture the sources of risk, optimal portfolios are built from these few factors (mutual fund theorem); factor timing is the dynamic counterpart of the theorem, with the return of the conditional maximum-Sharpe-ratio portfolio

$$R_{t+1}^{\text{opt}} = \mathbb{E}_t[F_{t+1}]'\Sigma_{F,t}^{-1}F_{t+1}.$$

Because the SDF loadings equal the weights of the maximum-Sharpe-ratio portfolio, estimating the SDF and solving the factor-timing problem are the same exercise [HaddadKozakSantosh2020].

## Near-Arbitrage and Dimension Reduction of the Problem

Determining the value of factor timing empirically is difficult because it requires measuring the predictability of many returns, opening the door to spurious results. The proliferation of proposed factors — what [Cochrane2011] calls the "factor zoo" — does not alter the theoretical performance of a model (the SDF would simply have a zero loading on the irrelevant factors), but it increases the probability of estimating spurious predictability in finite samples [HaddadKozakSantosh2020]. Economic restrictions are needed.

The connection between timing benefits, the SDF and predictability is illustrated by a decomposition. If returns are conditionally uncorrelated and homoskedastic, with constant conditional variances $`\sigma_i^2`$, the average maximum conditional squared Sharpe ratio can be written

$$\mathbb{E}\left(\mathrm{SR}_t^2\right) = \mathbb{E}\left[\text{var}_t(m_{t+1})\right] = \sum_i \frac{\mathbb{E}[R_{i,t+1}]^2}{\sigma_i^2} + \sum_i \left(\frac{R_i^2}{1-R_i^2}\right),$$

where $`R_i^2 = 1 - \sigma_i^2/\text{var}(r_{i,t+1})`$ is the maximum predictive R² of return $`i`$. The first equality, which holds without these two conditions, shows that the average maximum squared Sharpe ratio coincides with the expected variance of the minimum-variance SDF. The first term is the unconditional part, reminiscent of static Sharpe ratios; the second, increasing in the $`R_i^2`$, encodes predictability and is the incremental benefit of timing the factors.

The second restriction is the absence of near-arbitrage (Assumption 2): average conditional squared Sharpe ratios are bounded above by a constant. A bound on the maximum squared Sharpe ratio is immediately equivalent to an upper bound on the variance of the SDF [HansenJagannathan1991], in the tradition of the bound originally proposed by [Ross1976] for the APT; near-arbitrage opportunities would be eliminated in equilibrium [KozakNagelSantosh2018]. The restriction admits two readings: it corresponds to the average certainty equivalent of a mean-variance investor who uses conditional information optimally (with risk aversion $`\gamma`$, this is $`\mathrm{SR}_t^2/2\gamma`$); and since the SDF has a constant conditional mean, the bound is also a limit on its unconditional variance.

Applying the decomposition to the principal-component (PC) portfolios of the factors, with $`\lambda_i`$ the $`i`$-th eigenvalue of the constant conditional covariance of the factors (so that the PCs are conditionally uncorrelated and homoskedastic),

$$\mathbb{E}\left(\mathrm{SR}_t^2\right) = \sum_{i=1}^K \frac{\mathbb{E}[PC_{i,t+1}]^2}{\lambda_i} + \sum_{i=1}^K \left(\frac{R_i^2}{1-R_i^2}\right),$$

and the total degree of predictability, invariant to rotation, is

$$R_{\text{total}}^2 \equiv \frac{\text{tr}\left[\text{cov}\left(\mathbb{E}_t[F_{t+1}]\right)\right]}{\text{tr}\left[\text{cov}\left(F_{t+1}\right)\right]} = \sum_{i=1}^K \left(\frac{R_i^2}{1-R_i^2}\right)\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda}, \qquad \lambda = \sum_{i=1}^K \frac{\lambda_i}{1-R_i^2} \approx \sum_{i=1}^K \lambda_i,$$

where $`\lambda`$ is the total unconditional variance $`\text{tr}\left[\text{cov}\left(F_{t+1}\right)\right]`$. In terms of the unconditional variances $`\lambda_i^u`$ of the PCs (the eigenvalues of the unconditional covariance when the PCs are extracted from it, as in the empirical section below), the same quantity is $`R_{\text{total}}^2 = \sum_i R_i^2\lambda_i^u / \sum_i \lambda_i^u`$.

Combining the two relations, one concludes that small PCs cannot contribute significantly to predictability, otherwise they would generate implausibly high Sharpe ratios: investors could diversify across many independent, predictable sources. This is the dynamic counterpart of the static reasoning of [KozakNagelSantosh2018]. It follows (Proposition 1) that the SDF, and hence the optimal timing portfolio, is approximated by a combination of the dominant components $`Z_{t+1}`$:

$$m_{t+1} \approx 1 - \mathbb{E}_t[Z_{t+1}]'\Sigma_{Z,t}^{-1}\left(Z_{t+1} - \mathbb{E}_t[Z_{t+1}]\right), \qquad R_{t+1}^{\text{opt}} \approx \mathbb{E}_t[Z_{t+1}]'\Sigma_{Z,t}^{-1}Z_{t+1}.$$

The approach is a regularization of the left-hand side of the predictability problem — *which factors are predictable?* — rather than of the right-hand side — *which variables are useful predictors?* [HaddadKozakSantosh2020].

## The Robust Predictability of the Factor Cross-Section

The empirical implementation of [HaddadKozakSantosh2020] starts from fifty "anomaly" portfolios built from the CRSP-COMPUSTAT universe, sorting stocks into ten value-weighted portfolios for each characteristic; the long-short anomalies are the difference between decile 10 and decile 1, with monthly returns from January 1974 to December 2017. Before the PCs are extracted, each anomaly is market-adjusted, subtracting $`\beta`$ times the market return from its return and $`\beta`$ times the market book-to-market ratio from its own, and rescaled to equal variance, with the $`\beta`$s and variances estimated on the first half of the sample only. These anomalies exhibit a moderately strong factor structure: the first PC explains 25.8% of the variance, and the first five components about 60%. A simple calculation based on plausible priors (a monthly total R² of around 75 basis points, as in predicting the market, and an annual bound on the squared Sharpe ratio equal to 1) limits the number of PCs: taken literally it admits at most four, since with five the harmonic mean of their variance shares (8.7%) falls below 9%, the ratio of the R² prior to the monthly squared-Sharpe bound, and [HaddadKozakSantosh2020] use five; since the adjusted portfolios are market-neutral, the market is added, studying $`Z_{t+1} = (R_{mkt,t+1}, PC_{1,t+1}, \dots, PC_{5,t+1})`$.

As the predictor of each component one uses its net book-to-market ratio, built by combining the anomalies' $`bm`$ according to the portfolio weights, $`bm_{i,t} = q_i'\,bm_t^F`$ [HaddadKozakSantosh2020]. Portfolio-level valuations are stable even when composition changes, because expected returns depend on the fixed combination of "types" (value versus growth, large versus small). The results of six predictive regressions (Table 2) are clear-cut for two components. PC1 and PC4 are unambiguously predictable from their own $`bm`$, with large estimated coefficients and t-statistics around 4 (4.31 and 3.74), and both in-sample and out-of-sample R² around 4% for PC1 and 3.5% for PC4 — about four times the market's out-of-sample R² of 1%, where the out-of-sample R² is $`1 - \text{var}(r - \hat{r})/\text{var}(r)`$ for forecasts $`\hat{r}`$ estimated on the first half of the sample and evaluated on the second. The market, consistent with the literature, is not significant (t of 1.24) and suffers from a substantial Stambaugh bias. PC2 and PC3 are only marginally predictable (t of 1.81 and 2.01, bootstrap p-values of 0.10 and 0.07), significant at 5% only in the placebo test on the out-of-sample R²; PC5 does not appear predictable. The total R² is 1.03% in-sample and 0.93% out-of-sample. At the level of individual anomalies, about half have an out-of-sample R² above 1%.

This robust predictability contributes to the debate on whether anomalies are true opportunities or data-mining artifacts: some anomalies with low unconditional Sharpe ratios (size, sales growth) are nonetheless highly predictable, a sign of "important" deviations from the CAPM in which the premium, absent on average, is sometimes positive and sometimes negative for the same strategy [HaddadKozakSantosh2020]. The decisive ingredient is left-hand-side dimension reduction: predicting each anomaly with its own $`bm`$ generates only half of the predictability, while estimating all 50 anomalies on the 50 valuations without regularization produces an out-of-sample R² of $`-134\%`$. Robustness is confirmed by varying the number of PCs, the portfolio construction and the horizon, and with related methods such as the three-pass regression filter of [KellyPruitt2013], which is more robust when applied to the dominant components than to each portfolio. By contrast, alternative predictors such as sentiment produce moderate out-of-sample predictability, but far below the in-sample one, and "factor momentum" — past performance as a predictor, here an indicator equal to one when the portfolio's average monthly return over the past year is positive — fails out of sample on these fifty anomalies (R² of $`-0.49\%`$ and $`-0.08\%`$), although [HaddadKozakSantosh2020] note that in earlier work own past performance significantly predicts the returns of fifteen anomalies with a positive sign.

## Timing Gains and the Properties of the SDF

The strong predictability translates into substantial investment benefits. For intuition, with a single asset with time-varying expected return $`\mu_t`$ and constant variance $`\sigma^2`$, the mean-variance investor holds a position proportional to $`\mu_t/\sigma^2`$, generating a certainty equivalent proportional to $`\left(\mathbb{E}[\mu_t]^2 + \text{var}[\mu_t]\right)/\sigma^2`$; the term $`\text{var}[\mu_t]/\sigma^2`$ is the gain from exploiting the variation in expected returns, which a static portfolio with weight $`\mathbb{E}[\mu_t]/\sigma^2`$ does not obtain [HaddadKozakSantosh2020].

Building the optimal portfolio with weights $`\omega_t = \Sigma_Z^{-1}\mathbb{E}_t[Z_{t+1}]`$, where every strategy assumes a constant (homoskedastic) conditional covariance $`\Sigma_Z`$ estimated from the forecast residuals, [HaddadKozakSantosh2020] measures the gains (Table 6) by comparing five variants: factor investing (conditional means replaced by unconditional ones), market timing (only the market timed), factor timing (full), anomaly timing and pure anomaly timing (zero weight on the market, zero average exposure to every factor). Pure anomaly timing reaches a Sharpe ratio of 0.71 in-sample and 0.77 out-of-sample, despite taking no static bets. Judged by the information ratio — a conservative measure, a lower bound because it adopts the perspective of an uninformed investor — factor timing, anomaly timing and pure anomaly timing substantially extend the opportunity set, with information ratios rising to 0.42, 0.60 and 0.59 out-of-sample. In terms of expected utility — an ex-ante, model-based quantity that can be estimated only on the full sample, so that its out-of-sample support is the information ratios above, and that is scaled with $`\gamma = 1/2`$, so that it equals the average squared conditional Sharpe ratio $`\mathbb{E}\left(\mathrm{SR}_t^2\right)`$ — the gains are large: almost a doubling, from 1.66 to 2.96, of which 1.26 comes from pure anomaly timing alone; adding market timing to factor investing increases utility by only 0.03.

These benefits have implications for the nature of the SDF. Its conditional variance is $`\text{var}_t(m_{t+1}) = \omega_t'\Sigma_Z\,\omega_t`$. Including factor timing takes the average variance of the SDF from 1.67 to 2.96 (Table 7) — the utility gain above seen from the side of the SDF, since $`\mathbb{E}\left[\text{var}_t(m_{t+1})\right] = \mathbb{E}\left(\mathrm{SR}_t^2\right)`$, the static starting point being printed as 1.66 in Table 6 and 1.67 in Table 7 — a large value relative to estimates that ignore predictability and well above that of standard models (annualized variance of 0.85 in the long-run risk model, between 0 and about 1.2 in the habit model). The SDF is also strongly heteroskedastic, with a standard deviation of the variance of 2.17 and fluctuations between levels close to 0.8 and values of up to 12; the variation occurs mainly at business-cycle frequency but does not always coincide with recessions. The SDF loadings on the factors show interesting dynamics: size and value are procyclical, momentum countercyclical. Finally, the question of speed: with less frequent rebalancing the Sharpe ratio holds up — that of pure anomaly timing even rises from 0.71 to 0.79 with annual rebalancing — although its expected utility falls from 1.26 to 0.81, about 36% lower, while monthly turnover falls from 41% to 8%; this indicates that high-frequency variations in the predictors are not the main force behind the results and that the strategies could be implementable by real investors, although direct measures of transaction costs would be needed for a firm conclusion [HaddadKozakSantosh2020].

## Valuation-Based Timing: Buying Factors on Sale

A distinct tradition approaches timing from the side of relative valuation. [ArnottBeckKalesnik2016], summarizing the two earlier articles of its series, distinguishes between two components of past performance: *structural alpha*, earned net of any impact of rising valuations, and *revaluation alpha*, the part due to rising multiples — non-recurring and as likely to reverse as to persist. Rising valuations create an illusion of alpha and encourage performance chasing. Relative valuations, by contrast, predict future returns robustly and globally, according to the second article of the series: in US data from 1967 to 2016, with valuations measured against their full-sample median, the relation between aggregate valuation and the subsequent five-year return has a negative slope, with in-sample correlations of $`-0.19`$ for the value factor (blend) and $`-0.31`$ for the equal-weighted smart beta strategy, computed on overlapping windows that contain fewer than ten independent five-year periods; similar strategies and factors can sit at very different levels of relative valuation.

On this basis [ArnottBeckKalesnik2016] contrasts a *trend chaser* — who each year invests in the three (of eight) factors or strategies with the best past performance over 1, 3, 5 and 10 years — with a *contrarian* who buys the three worst performers on the same blend of past returns. Performance chasing destroys value: for smart beta the value-add falls to 1.2% against 1.5% for equal weight, and for factors the Sharpe ratio collapses to 0.14 against 0.52. The contrarian instead beats the trend chaser, and beats equal weight on return: a value-add of 2.2% against 1.5% for smart beta and a return of 3.3% against 2.4% for factors, but for factors with a lower Sharpe ratio than equal weight, 0.39 against 0.52, because of lower diversification. The mechanism is that winning often means becoming expensive: the correlation between the past 10-year average return and the ending valuation is 0.54 for smart beta and 0.45 for factors. In a separate test that ranks on valuation instead of past returns, the difference between the three least expensive and the three most expensive factors in the United States is 7.2% per year (t of 3.62), with a four-factor alpha of 7.7% (t of 4.05): the result is not explained by exposure to value. In international markets the difference is far smaller and not significant.

This is not "value investing on steroids": the tilts are based on which factors are cheap relative to their *own* histories, not on amplifying the exposure to value. The contrarian approach moves toward value when value is cheap, and toward growth-oriented factors such as momentum and profitability when these are the ones on sale [ArnottBeckKalesnik2016]. Moderation, however, is crucial: aggressive bets (concentrated on the single cheapest factor) can severely erode Sharpe ratios, because the loss of diversification increases portfolio risk; an analysis with "diversification tilted toward the cheapest" confirms that the value of a timing signal is limited when applied with breadth, and that it is prudent to use it with moderation [ArnottBeckKalesnik2016]. Finally, the very proliferation of products is a form of data mining: after publication or launch, returns degrade — the average excess return of factors falls from 5.8% to 2.4% and that of smart beta from 1.8% to 1.4%, so that about 22% of smart beta alpha and over half of factor alpha has evaporated, an estimate of very poor precision by the authors' own account. They give three reasons: in-sample or publication bias, arbitrage once the idea is published and, very likely, revaluation alpha in the pre-publication returns, because academics discover factors when they are expensive, depressing their future prospects.

## The Failure of Extrapolation

The natural complement is the question of what does *not* predict future returns. [ArnottBeckKalesnik2017] challenges two entrenched assumptions: that past performance is the best estimate of future performance, and that factors have constant risk premia. The central result, on eight US factors with the models calibrated on 1967–1990 and compared on 1991–2011, is that a factor's past performance is *negatively* correlated with its subsequent five-year performance, not significantly for the trailing five years and significantly for the full history since inception: history is worse than useless.

The authors compare six alpha-forecasting models, evaluated on quality (MSE), correlation with subsequent returns and realism of levels. Model 1 (return over the last five years) provides the worst forecast and has a correlation of $`-0.18`$ (t of $`-1.48`$, not significant) with future performance. Model 2 (return since the start of the sample) improves accuracy thanks to a long history, but its correlation remains negative, $`-0.39`$ (t of $`-2.85`$): selecting factors on past performance, regardless of the length of the sample, leads in the wrong direction. The valuation-dependent Models 3–6, by contrast, have positive correlations with future performance; in accuracy (MSE), however, only Models 4–6 beat Model 0 (zero alpha), while the error of Model 3 remains slightly above that of the zero-alpha assumption. Model 6, calibrated on the full sample with look-ahead — clairvoyance — has a correlation of 0.54 (t of 8.05) and reduces the error by 39% relative to Model 2, setting the upper limit of what a valuation model can achieve. Model 4 (valuation-dependent with shrunk parameters, without look-ahead) has a correlation of 0.36 and reduces the error by 25%, about two-thirds of clairvoyance; Model 3 reduces it by 11%, and Model 5, which halves the forecasts of Model 4, by 29%, at the cost of less realistic forecast magnitudes.

Among the models compared, only valuation, then, has predictive power — not with precision, nor with short-term timing efficacy, but enough to add material value [ArnottBeckKalesnik2017]. Applying Model 4 to end-2016 valuations, the alpha forecasts are negative in all markets for low beta and size (expensive relative to their own historical averages), and positive for value and — after a change in composition — for momentum, which has become cheap again. Two caveats accompany the forecasts. First, costs matter: momentum and low-volatility strategies pay the heaviest tolls, and "trading costs can dwarf fees"; the forecasts should be considered net of costs. Second, estimation uncertainty: the model parameters are estimated on finite data, and the estimation error of the return forecasts is, in most cases, larger than the alpha forecast itself [ArnottBeckKalesnik2017]. Chasing recent performance is therefore doubly dangerous: it is inadequate and it leads in the opposite direction.

## The Taxonomy of Predictors and the Traps of Timing

[BenderEtAl2017] organizes timing signals into five main categories (Exhibit 2): Financial Conditions (corporate credit spreads, TED spread, money-supply growth), Economic/Macroeconomic Cycle (GDP growth, capacity ratio, consumer confidence), Sentiment/Risk Sentiment (VIX, ISM PMI), Valuation (CAPE, dividend yield, earnings yield, book-to-price) and Trend/Momentum/Persistence (past performance from 1 month to 5 years). The cardinal point is that different predictors are relevant at different horizons: in factor timing the horizon is critical.

The investment logic varies by category. Valuation and Momentum have direct intuitions: extending the mean reversion of [CampbellShiller1998] to factors, a wide valuation spread signals higher future returns (the information coefficient of value moves with the valuation spread, Exhibit 4), while momentum extends to factors the persistence documented by [JegadeeshTitman1993]. Sentiment and macroeconomic variables instead reflect the changing price of risk: the defensive factors (Low Volatility, Quality) are perceived as less risky because of their consistently low beta, while value and momentum alternate between risky and safe [BenderEtAl2017]. The empirical evidence (Exhibits 8–11) confirms that predictability is concentrated in a subset of signals at longer horizons: TED and term spreads are reasonably strong predictors beyond six months; VIX and sentiment have strong relations at one year and beyond; valuation metrics are strong at long horizons; past performance is significant from as early as three months, strongest at one year, with mean reversion emerging at 3–5 years. Few signals are strong at the one-month horizon. As an example, the correlation between the term spread and the profitability factor goes from $`-0.18`$ at one month to $`-0.46`$ at one year.

Against the promise [BenderEtAl2017] sets the traps, identifying three challenges in building a timing model. (1) *Time-varying causal links*: the most important. Value's beta to the market changes sign over time (Exhibit 13); thus the same widening of credit spreads precedes good value performance in 2000–2001 but bad performance in 2008–2009, probably, as the authors suspect, because value was low beta in the first period and high beta in the second. (2) *Hindsight-based cherry-picking and data mining*: identifying signals that have worked historically is not the same as choosing signals that will work. In an exercise that imagines standing in 1990, of the predictors significant in 1972–1989 very few remain so in 1990–2010: at the quarterly horizon, of the 18 significant for size 1 is confirmed, of the 3 for value none, of the 10 for investment none, of the 9 for momentum 1 (Exhibit 14). Academic research also tends to cluster on the signals that appear predictive. (3) *Data revisions*: macroeconomic series such as GDP and unemployment are often corrected after the first estimate, so backtests may not reflect the information actually available at the time of the forecast. To overcome these challenges [BenderEtAl2017] proposes two routes: a parsimonious model on a few well-vetted signals with theoretical intuition (Valuation and Trend are good candidates) — in the example, a scheme proposed by Shapiro and Thomas (2014) and extended by [ArnottBeckKalesnik2017], a factor is removed for three years if it exceeds its own historical spread by more than one standard deviation, earning 11.28% per year against 9.14% for the static allocation and 7.57% for the index; or a multi-signal panel-regression model that integrates many indicators and their interactions, which in a hypothetical backtest (1997–2015, with the estimation design not stated) improves the information ratio from 0.88 to 1.17. Timing is therefore possible at the margin, for an investor with a sufficiently long horizon and a model calibrated to the horizon.

## The Skeptical Counterpoint

[AsnessEtAl2017] addresses two questions central to the factor investor: how expensive are the most popular factors today, and is it worth timing exposure to them? At the heart of the analysis is the *value spread*: the cheapness or expensiveness of a factor is estimated as the difference between the valuation of the assets with the greatest exposure to that factor and that of the assets with the least exposure. The measure is not unique: choosing one value metric (P/B, P/E) rather than another, or using percentiles instead of z-scores, changes the readings, and a factor that looks expensive on one specification can look entirely ordinary on another.

On the first question the answer is surprisingly benign: the HML and UMD factors in the US large-cap universe are not markedly more expensive than their historical averages going back to 1968; the BAB factor, which represents the defensive style, was more expensive over 2007–2016 than its long-run average, but stayed well below two standard deviations and nowhere near the valuation extremes observed in 2000 [AsnessEtAl2017]. None of the factors is at extreme levels.

On the second question, contrarian timing on valuation is "deceptively difficult". The initial correlations between the value spread and subsequent factor returns look "modestly promising" — moderately positive for value, weaker for momentum and low-beta — but once hypothetical contrarian trading strategies are simulated (which vary the weight of each style between 50% and 150% of the strategic weight according to the out-of-sample value spread, with z-scores truncated at $`\pm 2`$ standard deviations), the initial promise evaporates [AsnessEtAl2017]. There is a big difference between observing a link between initial valuations and returns and finding that a strategy based on it will be equally successful. Timing is, moreover, harder for factors than for markets because of turnover: one tries to predict the future using today's portfolio constituents, but three months later the portfolio can be very different.

The distinction between a single-factor and a multi-factor portfolio is decisive. Value timing is more successful when applied to single factors than to multi-style portfolios that already include value. The reason is that value timing is highly correlated with the ordinary value factor: it adds a value exposure that benefits a single-factor portfolio, but brings little benefit to a portfolio that already contains value [AsnessEtAl2017]. The multi-factor portfolio is a higher bar to clear, since diversification across the three factors already offers a higher Sharpe ratio; indeed, in a portfolio that already includes value, adding timing can lead to lower risk-adjusted returns. The practical conclusion is clear-cut: the result does not mean that investors should start value-timing their single-factor exposures, and if you want value, it is better simply to add a strategic allocation to the value factor. The authors do not, however, close the door on value timing entirely: there could be useful areas, such as a tactical signal that uses value and momentum jointly, or moments when valuations reach extremes not observed at present [AsnessEtAl2017].

## The Balanced Verdict

The four lines of evidence form a coherent picture despite their tensions. There is real predictability in the cross-section of factors: [HaddadKozakSantosh2020] documents it robustly, disciplined by the absence of near-arbitrage, with substantial expected-utility gains and the conclusion that the optimal timing portfolio is the stochastic discount factor; and [ArnottBeckKalesnik2016], summarizing its series, reports that relative valuation predicts the future returns of factors and strategies robustly and globally.

At the same time this predictability is hard to monetize net of costs and estimation error. [ArnottBeckKalesnik2017] warns that transaction costs — very heavy on momentum and low-volatility — must be subtracted, and that the estimation error of the forecasts often exceeds the forecast itself. [AsnessEtAl2017] finds that, when trading strategies are simulated, the promise of the correlations evaporates, and that in a multi-style portfolio already holding value, timing is a weak addition for the long-term investor. [ArnottBeckKalesnik2016] shows that applying the signal with breadth limits its value and that aggressive bets erode Sharpe ratios through the loss of diversification. And [BenderEtAl2017] documents the traps of fragility: time-varying causal links, cherry-picking and data mining, data revisions.

Two conclusions are shared. First, valuation is the most consistent predictor, though not the only one: it is the only variable that generates positive correlations and error reductions in the comparison of valuation and past-return models [ArnottBeckKalesnik2017], it survives among the few strong signals at long horizons [BenderEtAl2017], and — albeit modestly, since once traded its promise evaporates, a weakness [HaddadKozakSantosh2020] attribute to forecasting each factor separately — it informs the value spread [AsnessEtAl2017]; [BenderEtAl2017] also find past performance strongest at about one year and TED and term spreads strong beyond six months, and [HaddadKozakSantosh2020] find some out-of-sample predictability in sentiment. Second, extrapolating past performance over horizons of several years is dangerous: past performance is negatively correlated with the following five years, significantly so for the full history since inception [ArnottBeckKalesnik2017], and most predictor-factor relations selected with hindsight do not survive out of sample [BenderEtAl2017]; at horizons of about three to twelve months, by contrast, persistence is positive [BenderEtAl2017]. The "factor momentum" indicator of [HaddadKozakSantosh2020] fails out of sample in their disciplined setting, a result specific to that design. The debate remains open — from skepticism to the conviction that factor timing is one more resource — but there is agreement that chasing recent performance is the wrong way, and that discipline is to be sought in valuation, in model parsimony, in consistency with the horizon and in the no-near-arbitrage constraint.
