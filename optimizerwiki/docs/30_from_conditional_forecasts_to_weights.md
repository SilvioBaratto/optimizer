> [!abstract] Summary
> The chapter establishes how a return forecast conditioned on macroeconomic variables translates into optimal portfolio weights. It distinguishes the two-stage plug-in approach — modeling the conditional moments and then optimizing — from the approach that models the weights directly: variable selection via a state index, parametric policies as a function of characteristics, and the expansion of the asset space. It shows how each route closes the pipeline that brings macro signals into the convex optimizer, and how the choice among them is governed by the trade-off with estimation error.

## Macro Conditioning and the Weights Problem

The chapter [[29 Segnali macroeconomici e premi al rischio attesi]] documents that means, variances, covariances, and higher-order moments of stock and bond returns are time-varying and partially predictable from economic variables [AitSahaliaBrandt2001]. This chapter's problem is to translate that predictability into portfolio weights: given a forecast conditioned on macroeconomic information, how are the optimal allocations obtained to hand over to the convex optimizer of [[14 Ottimizzazione convessa - coni, dualità e KKT]].

Consider an investor who at time $t$ chooses the weights $x_t$ to assign to $N$ risky assets and a risk-free asset in order to maximize expected utility of wealth at a future date $t+\tau$ [Brandt2010]. With $z_t$ a $K$-dimensional vector of observable state variables and $y_t\equiv[r_t,z_t]$ a first-order Markov process, the problem is
$$V(\tau,W_t,z_t)=\max_{\{x_s\}_{s=t}^{t+\tau-1}}\mathrm{E}_t\big[u(W_{t+\tau})\big],$$
subject to the budget constraint $W_{s+1}=W_s\,(x_s'r_{s+1}+R_s^f)$. The function $V$, called the value function, measures the quality of the investment opportunities available. Rewriting the problem as a one-period choice with state-dependent utility yields the Bellman equation
$$V(\tau,W_t,z_t)=\max_{x_t}\mathrm{E}_t\big[V\big(\tau-1,W_t(x_t'r_{t+1}+R_t^f),z_{t+1}\big)\big],$$
with first-order conditions $\mathrm{E}_t[V_2(\tau-1,\cdot,z_{t+1})\,r_{t+1}]=0$, which form a system of non-linear equations generally solvable only numerically [Brandt2010]. The case $\tau=1$ is the static single-period choice; for $\tau>1$ the choice is dynamic and multi-period.

With constant relative risk aversion (CRRA) utility $u(W_{t+\tau})=W_{t+\tau}^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$, homotheticity allows normalizing $W_t=1$ and the value function depends only on the horizon and the state variables: the Bellman equation reduces to
$$\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\,\psi(\tau,z_t)=\max_{x_t}\mathrm{E}_t\bigg[\frac{(x_t'r_{t+1}+R_t^f)^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\,\psi(\tau-1,z_{t+1})\bigg].$$
If the excess returns $r_{t+1}$ are contemporaneously independent of the innovations of the state variables $z_{t+1}$, the conditional expectation factorizes into the product of two expectations and the first-order conditions of the multi-period problem coincide with those of the single-period problem. If instead returns are not independent of the innovations of the states, the dynamic choice differs from the myopic one: the difference is the *hedging demand*, with which the investor hedges against changes in investment opportunities [Brandt2010].

The continuous-time formulation makes this decomposition explicit. If asset prices and states evolve as correlated Itô processes, the solution of the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation is
$$x_t^\star=\underbrace{-\frac{V_2}{W_t V_{22}}\,(\Sigma_t^p)^{-1}\mu_t^p}_{\text{myopic demand}}\;\underbrace{-\frac{V_2}{W_t V_{22}}\frac{V_{23}}{V_2}\,(\Sigma_t^p)^{-1}D_t^p\rho_t'D_t^{z\prime}}_{\text{hedging demand}},$$
where the first term is the myopic choice — a fraction $1/\gamma_t$, with $\gamma_t\equiv -W_tV_{22}/V_2$, of the tangent portfolio of the instantaneous mean-variance frontier — and the second depends on the projection of state innovations onto return innovations [Merton1971]. The myopic demand is the *market timing* channel: the response of the allocation to conditional moments. The hedging demand is the intertemporal channel, absent when $\tau=1$ or when the states do not predict changes in future opportunities.

Obtaining weights from a conditional forecast admits two fundamentally different strategies. The first, traditional, is a two-stage procedure: in the first, the conditional moments of returns are modeled and estimated; in the second, the problem is solved for the optimal weights. The second skips the first stage and models or estimates the optimal weights directly. The following sections develop both.

## The Plug-In Approach: From Estimated Moments to Weights

In the plug-in approach, the solution of the expected-utility maximization is a map from the preference parameters $\phi$ (for example $\gamma$), the state vector $z_t$, and the parameters of the data-generating process $\theta$ to the optimal weights:
$$x_t^\star=x(\phi,z_t,\theta).$$
Given $Y_T\equiv\{y_t\}_{t=0}^T$ one obtains a consistent estimate $\hat\theta$ and "plugs it in" to the map to obtain $\hat x_t^\star=x(\phi,z_t,\hat\theta)$ [Brandt2010]. If $\hat\theta$ is consistent with asymptotic distribution $\sqrt T(\hat\theta-\theta)\to N[0,V_\theta]$ and the map is sufficiently regular, the delta method gives
$$\sqrt T(\hat x_t^\star-x_t^\star)\to N[0,\,x_3(\cdot)V_\theta\,x_3(\cdot)'].$$

In the mean-variance problem $\max_x\ \mathrm{E}[r_{p,t+1}]-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\mathrm{var}[r_{p,t+1}]$ with i.i.d. excess returns, the optimal weights are $x^\star=(1/\gamma)\Sigma^{-1}\mu$ and are estimated with the sample analogs
$$\hat\mu=\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T r_{t+1},\qquad \hat\Sigma=\frac{1}{T-N-2}\sum_{t=1}^T (r_{t+1}-\hat\mu)(r_{t+1}-\hat\mu)',$$
where the unusual degrees of freedom make $\hat\Sigma^{-1}$ unbiased under normality, so that $\mathrm{E}[\hat x^\star]=(1/\gamma)\mathrm{E}[\hat\Sigma^{-1}]\mathrm{E}[\hat\mu]=(1/\gamma)\Sigma^{-1}\mu$ [Brandt2010]. Britten-Jones shows that the plug-in weights of the tangent portfolio $\hat x^\star_{tgc}=\hat\Sigma^{-1}\hat\mu/(\iota'\hat\Sigma^{-1}\hat\mu)$ are obtained from the coefficients $\hat b$ of an OLS regression of a vector of ones on excess returns without an intercept, $1=b\,r_{t+1}+u_{t+1}$, so that the standard theory of $\hat b$ allows inference on the weights via $t$- and $F$-tests [BrittenJones1999].

The imprecision of the plug-in estimate is, however, severe. For a single risky asset, expanding $\hat x^\star=(1/\gamma)\hat\mu/\hat\sigma^2$ around $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ gives
$$\mathrm{var}[\hat x^\star]=\frac{1}{\gamma^2}\Big(\frac{\mu}{\sigma^2}\Big)^2\Big(\frac{\mathrm{var}[\hat\mu]}{\mu^2}+\frac{\mathrm{var}[\hat\sigma^2]}{\sigma^4}\Big):$$
the imprecision is scaled by the size of the optimal weight and depends on the imprecision of the risk premium and of volatility, each relative to its own magnitude [Brandt2010]. With ten years of monthly data, $\mu=6\%$, $\sigma=15\%$, $\gamma=5$, the standard error of $\hat x^\star$ is 14% against a true weight of 53.3%; under a GARCH process the variance of the sample variance inflates enormously and the standard error can rise above 100%, showing that all moments of returns contribute to the imprecision of the weights. In finite samples, as the number of assets increases precision deteriorates drastically because the distinct elements of the covariance matrix grow quadratically; Jobson-Korkie's simulations show plug-in frontiers that are extremely volatile and on average far below the true one, with extreme and unstable weights [JobsonKorkie1980]. Michaud describes mean-variance optimizers as "error maximizers," because they assign large positive (negative) weights to securities with large positive (negative) estimation errors in the risk premium [Michaud1989]; Chopra and Ziemba quantify the relative weight of errors in the different moments [ChopraZiemba1993].

An economic measure of the error is the certainty-equivalent loss. Letting $\mathrm{CE}=x^{\star\prime}\mu-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}x^{\star\prime}\Sigma x^\star$ be the certainty equivalent of the true weights and $\hat{\mathrm{CE}}$ that of the plug-in weights evaluated at the true moments,
$$\mathrm{CE}-\mathrm{E}[\hat{\mathrm{CE}}]\simeq \frac{\gamma}{2}\,\mathrm{tr}\big[\mathrm{cov}[\hat x^\star]\,\Sigma\big].$$
Since consistency implies that the two portfolios generate on average the same return, the loss arises from *parameter uncertainty*, penalized by utility in the same way as the intrinsic uncertainty of returns; in the single-asset example the loss is an order of magnitude smaller than the standard error of the weight, consistent with the fact that first-order deviations from the optimal rules have second-order consequences [Cochrane1989]. Kan and Zhou search for variants $\hat w^\star=c\times\hat\Sigma^{-1}\hat\mu$ with a constant $c$ that minimizes the economic loss [KanZhou2004].

Motivated by these properties, there are three complementary ways to improve plug-in estimates [Brandt2010]. The first is *shrinkage*: the James-Stein estimate $\mu_s=\delta\mu_0+(1-\delta)\bar\mu$ shrinks sample means toward a common value, reducing extreme errors at the cost of small biases, with optimal factor $\delta^\star=\min[1,\,(N-2)/T\,/\,(\bar\mu-\mu_0)'\Sigma^{-1}(\bar\mu-\mu_0)]$ [JamesStein1961; Jorion1986]. Shrinkage also applies to the covariance, $\hat\Sigma_s=\delta\hat S+(1-\delta)\hat\Sigma$, with targets such as the identity matrix, that of a factor model, or that of equal correlations; shrinkage toward a positive-definite target additionally guarantees a positive-definite estimate even when $N>T$ [LedoitWolf2003]. The second way is imposing a factor structure, which reduces dimensionality: with a single-factor model $\Sigma=\sigma_m^2\beta\beta'+\Sigma_\epsilon$ [Sharpe1963], and more generally $\Sigma=B\Sigma_f B'+\Sigma_\epsilon$; the factors can come from theory, from empirical work (macroeconomic, industry, or characteristic-based factors), or be extracted from returns [ChenRollRoss1986; FamaFrench1993], as in [[05 Modelli fattoriali]]. The third way is constraints on the weights, which truncate extreme positions; Jagannathan and Ma show that short-sale constraints and position limits are equivalent to a form of shrinkage of the covariance matrix,
$$\tilde\Sigma=\Sigma+(\delta\iota'+\iota\delta')-(\lambda\iota'+\iota'\lambda),$$
where $\lambda$ and $\delta$ are the constraint multipliers, explaining why constraints help even when not theoretically justified [JagannathanMa2003]. Green and Hollifield warn, however, that extreme weights do not necessarily imply an undiversified portfolio [GreenHollifield1992]. These remedies are the content of [[08 Errore di stima, outlier e shrinkage]].

## The Decision-Theoretic Approach: Subjective Distributions and Economic Beliefs

The second traditional econometric approach is decision theory: the econometrician takes on the role of the investor and chooses optimal weights with respect to their own subjective belief about the distribution of returns [Brandt2010]. In the presence of statistical uncertainty about the parameters, or about the parameterization of the process itself, this subjective distribution can differ markedly from the plug-in of point estimates, and so can the weights that result from it.

Write the myopic maximization with i.i.d. returns as
$$\max_{x_t}\int u(x_t'r_{t+1}+R^f)\,p(r_{t+1}\mid\theta)\,dr_{t+1}.$$
If the investor knows the parametric form but not the true $\theta$, the problem is not well posed. There are at least three ways to proceed. The first is to naively use the estimates in place of the true parameters, analogous to the plug-in estimate. The second considers the worst-case values of the parameters within a pre-specified set, obtaining robust rather than optimal weights (robust control), as in [[15 Ottimizzazione robusta e insiemi di incertezza]]. The third eliminates the dependence on the unknown parameters by replacing the true distribution with a subjective distribution that depends only on the observed data and prior beliefs; by incorporating all available information, this approach is, for many, the most attractive [Brandt2010].

Zellner and Chetty, Klein and Bawa, and Brown were among the first to advocate subjective distributions in portfolio choice. Given $Y_T$ and a prior belief $p_0(\theta)$, Bayes' theorem gives the posterior distribution $p(\theta\mid Y_T)\propto p(Y_T\mid\theta)\,p_0(\theta)$, with which the parameter is *integrated out* to obtain the subjective predictive distribution
$$p(r_{t+1}\mid Y_T)=\int p(r_{t+1}\mid\theta)\,p(\theta\mid Y_T)\,d\theta,$$
which replaces the true distribution in the maximization [Brandt2010]. In the mean-variance problem with non-informative priors, the posterior mean of the weights coincides with the frequentist estimate up to the degrees of freedom, but the variability of the priors relative to the information in the data automatically determines how much the estimates are shrunk toward the target, giving a Bayesian interpretation of shrinkage.

Economic beliefs enter as informative priors. For return predictability, the regression $r_{t+1}=a+b\,z_t+\varepsilon_{t+1}$ produces very noisy forecasts ($R^2$ around 1%); Kandel and Stambaugh and Connor recommend a prior centered on market efficiency ($b=0$), yielding a posterior mean $\hat b_{\mathrm{Bayes}}=[T\hat\sigma_z^2/\hat\sigma_\varepsilon^2/(T\hat\sigma_z^2/\hat\sigma_\varepsilon^2+1/\sigma_b^2)]\,\hat b_{ols}$ [KandelStambaugh1996]. Connor reformulates the shrinkage in terms of the expected degree of predictability $\rho\simeq\mathrm{E}[R^2]$,
$$\hat b_{\mathrm{Bayes}}=\Big[\frac{T}{T+1/\rho}\Big]\,\hat b_{ols},$$
which, with an expected $R^2$ of 1% and five to ten years of data, shrinks the OLS coefficient by about half toward zero [Connor1997]. For beliefs from an equilibrium model, Pastor centers a prior on the cross-sectional intercepts $\alpha$, $p(\alpha)=\mathrm{MVN}[0,\sigma_\alpha I]$, whose dispersion measures the strength of belief in the CAPM; the posterior shrinks the intercepts toward zero, $\mathrm{E}[\alpha\mid Y_T]=(1-\delta)\hat\alpha_{ols}$, while simultaneously adjusting the betas [Pastor2000]. Finally, the idea of averaging over parameter values extends to *model uncertainty*: with a finite set of models $M_j$, the posterior probabilities $p(M_j\mid Y_T)$ allow the construction of a predictive distribution averaged over models, $p(r_{t+1}\mid Y_T)=\sum_j p(r_{t+1}\mid Y_T,M_j)\,p(M_j\mid Y_T)$, whose non-central moments are themselves averaged over models and usable as subjective mean-variance inputs [Avramov2002]. This uncertainty contributes to the subjective variance of returns as much as, or more than, parameter uncertainty.

## Modeling Weights Directly

The traditional approach, whether plug-in or decision-theoretic, is by its nature a two-stage procedure: first the data-generating process is modeled and inference is drawn on it, then the problem is solved for the weights. An alternative line of research skips the first stage and infers directly on the optimal weights [Brandt2010].

Beyond the fact that optimal weights are the ultimate object of interest, there are three reasons to focus on them directly. The first is that modeling returns is the weak point of the traditional approach: there is wide disagreement on how to model returns, and the empirical relations documented between state variables and return moments are usually tenuous, with a risk of serious misspecification and estimation error, subsequently amplified by the optimizer. The intuition is that optimal weights are easier to model and estimate than conditional distributions. The second, related, reason is dimensionality reduction: in an unconditional mean-variance problem with 500 assets, modeling returns involves over 125,000 parameters, but the final result is only 500 optimal weights; focusing on the weights reduces the room for misspecification and estimation error. The third is that inferring on the weights lends itself naturally to a loss function based on expected utility, avoiding the inconsistent practice of estimating the return model with quadratic loss and then switching to expected utility for the weights [Brandt2010].

Three methods fall within this family. In the *nonparametric* method, optimal weights are characterized by the conditional Euler conditions $\mathrm{E}_t[u'(x_t'r_{t+1}+R_t^f)r_{t+1}]=0$; conditional expectations are replaced with nonparametric regressions and one solves for the weights that zero out their sample analogs. For a realization $z_t=z$,
$$\hat x(z)=\Big\{x:\ \frac{1}{Th_T^K}\sum_{t=1}^T\omega\Big(\frac{z_t-z}{h_T}\Big)u'(x'r_{t+1}+R_t^f)\,r_{t+1}=0\Big\},$$
with $\omega(\cdot)$ a kernel function and $h_T$ a bandwidth that tends to zero as $T$ grows [Brandt1999]. This estimator is consistent and asymptotically Gaussian, but suffers from the curse of dimensionality: in practice no more than two predictors can be used with postwar monthly or quarterly data [Brandt2010]. The other two methods — parameterizing the weights as a function of the states, and expanding the asset space — are the subject of the following sections, and address precisely the dimensionality limit of the nonparametric method.

## Variable Selection via a State Index

The question of which predictors to follow is not resolved by forecasting the individual moments separately. The difficulty is that the moments (or functions thereof) relevant to the choice are *endogenous* to the investor's preferences [AitSahaliaBrandt2001]. Even in the mean-variance case, where the optimal allocation is proportional to the ratio of the conditional mean to the conditional variance, one wants variables that predict that *ratio* well, not the two moments separately: choosing variables that best predict mean and variance separately is counterproductive, since a variable may have a positive effect on both (good for the mean, bad for the variance) and it is unclear how to weigh their relative importance. Moreover, different objective functions emphasize different aspects of the distribution: a mean-variance investor wants to forecast means and variances, a loss-averse one cares about the left tail; since different moments are predicted by different variables, and since at the optimum different investors hold different portfolios, they may choose different predictors. This suggests selecting variables to predict *optimal weights directly*, rather than first the characteristics of the distribution and then their implications [AitSahaliaBrandt2001].

The one-period investor maximizes $\max_{\alpha_t}\mathrm{E}[v(W_t(\alpha_t'R_{t+1}))\mid Z_t]$ subject to the constraint $\alpha_t'\iota=1$, and the solution is the map $\alpha_t\equiv\alpha(Z_t)$ from the state vector to the weights (the portfolio policy). With mean-variance preferences $\mathrm{E}[v\mid Z_t]=\mathrm{E}[W_{t+1}\mid Z_t]-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\mathrm{Var}[W_{t+1}\mid Z_t]$ the policy is
$$\alpha_t=\Sigma_t^{-1}\iota\,\frac{\gamma W_t-\iota'\Sigma_t^{-1}\mu_t}{\gamma W_t\,\iota'\Sigma_t^{-1}\iota}+\frac{\Sigma_t^{-1}\mu_t}{\gamma W_t},$$
with $\Sigma_t=\mathrm{Var}[R_{t+1}\mid Z_t]$ and $\mu_t=\mathrm{E}[R_{t+1}\mid Z_t]$: even with this simple preference there is no direct link between the predictability of individual moments and the policy, and the policy can be highly non-linear and non-monotonic. In the presence of the risk-free rate, the choice reduces to a fraction of the tangent portfolio,
$$\alpha_t^{tgc}=\frac{1}{\gamma W_t}\,\frac{\mathrm{E}[r_{t+1}^{tgc}\mid Z_t]}{\mathrm{Var}[r_{t+1}^{tgc}]},$$
that is, it is proportional to the conditional mean-variance ratio of the tangent portfolio, a descriptive statistic of the conditional distribution [AitSahaliaBrandt2001].

To circumvent the curse of dimensionality of the fully nonparametric estimator, a *semiparametric* approach is adopted: it is *assumed* that the optimal weights depend on the predictors $Z_t$ only through a single linear index $Z_t'\beta$, leaving the dependence on that index completely free. The problem becomes
$$\max_{\alpha_t}\mathrm{E}[v(W_t(\alpha_t'R_{t+1}))\mid Z_t'\beta],\qquad \alpha_t\equiv\alpha(Z_t'\beta;\beta).$$
Collapsing the state vector into a univariate index allows kernel regression to be implemented in dimension one, with a parametric $\sqrt T$ convergence rate independent of the number of predictors; the index also offers a univariate summary of the current state of time-varying investment opportunities [AitSahaliaBrandt2001]. The idea borrows from index regressions [PowellStockStoker1989]. The coefficients $\beta$ are estimated from the conditional Euler conditions of the unrestricted problem: substituting the index restriction into the first-order conditions yields the moment conditions $\mathrm{E}[m_{t+1}(\beta)\mid Z_t]\equiv \mathrm{E}[v'(W_t(\alpha(Z_t'\beta;\beta)'R_{t+1}))R_{t+1}\mid Z_t]=0$, which, multiplied by instruments $g(Z_t)$ and taken in unconditional expectation, give a standard GMM problem
$$\min_\beta\ \mathrm{E}[m_{t+1}(\beta)\otimes g(Z_t)]'\,W\,\mathrm{E}[m_{t+1}(\beta)\otimes g(Z_t)]$$
[Hansen1982]. Alternatively, under the assumption that the form of the index is suboptimal, $\beta$ is chosen by maximizing unconditional expected utility
$$\max_\beta\,\mathrm{E}[v(W_t(\alpha(Z_t'\beta;\beta)'R_{t+1}))],$$
that is, minimizing the expected utility loss due to collapsing information into an index; empirically this loss turns out to be negligible in most cases. The relative weights $\hat\beta$ and their statistical significance indicate which variables, and in what combination, are relevant to the choice [AitSahaliaBrandt2001].

Applying the approach to four popular predictors — the credit spread (default spread, the yield difference between Moody's Baa and Aaa corporate bonds), the log dividend yield of the S&P 500 index, the term spread (the difference between the yields of 10-year and 1-year Treasury securities), and an index momentum/trend variable — sharp regularities emerge. The term spread is by far the most important predictor of expected stock and bond returns; the credit spread positively predicts variances and covariance; the log dividend yield positively predicts expected stock returns at the annual horizon [AitSahaliaBrandt2001]. In portfolio choice with a risk-free rate, the term spread is the dominant contributor to the index for both the mean-variance and the CRRA investor, with the credit spread or the dividend yield as the second predictor depending on the horizon; the index achieves a *compromise* between predicting means and variances. CRRA preferences (with $v(W)=W^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$ for $\gamma>1$ and $v(W)=\ln W$ for $\gamma=1$) produce indices similar to the mean-variance ones, with slightly more weight on the term premium and less on the dividend yield; if the no-short-sale constraints are relaxed, the CRRA indices with a risk-free rate are identical across different levels of risk aversion, a sign that preferences for higher-order moments are not enough to induce CRRA investors with different $\gamma$ to follow different variables [AitSahaliaBrandt2001].

The applications go on to characterize market timing, horizon effects, and hedging demands. *Market timing* is the response of the optimal allocation to changes in the index: all the investors considered engage in market timing, except the very risk-averse ones. There is more market timing at long horizons than short ones, because long-horizon returns are more predictable (due to the slow mean reversion of the term premium and the dividend yield) and because short-horizon investors hold more cash; less risk-averse investors do more timing. *Hedging demands* arise in the multi-period choice with intermediate rebalancing, when returns are contemporaneously correlated with changes in investment opportunities; they are measured as $\alpha_n(Z_t'\beta_n;\beta_n)-\alpha_1(Z_t'\beta_1;\beta_1)$, the deviation of the $n$-horizon choice from the one-period choice, solved recursively backward. Since the main state variable is endogenously determined to be the term spread — and returns are not particularly correlated with its changes — hedging demands turn out to be surprisingly small, indeed *negative*: long-term investors allocate less wealth to risky assets than short-term ones. Long-horizon investors, moreover, concentrate even more on the term spread [AitSahaliaBrandt2001].

## Parametric Portfolio Policies

The simplest way to directly estimate optimal weights is to parameterize them as a function of observable quantities — state variables and/or firm characteristics — and solve for the parameters that maximize expected utility [Brandt2010]. This method, developed for large-scale cross-sectional applications, avoids estimating the covariance matrix [BrandtSantaClaraValkanov2009].

Suppose that at each date $t$ there are $N_t$ securities in the investable universe, each with excess return $r_{i,t+1}$ and a vector of characteristics $y_{i,t}$ observed at $t$ — for example the market beta, market capitalization, the book-to-market ratio, past twelve-month return. The problem is to choose the weights $x_{i,t}$ that maximize expected utility of the portfolio return $r_{p,t+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_t}x_{i,t}r_{i,t+1}$. The optimal weights are parameterized as a function of the characteristics:
$$x_{i,t}=\bar x_{i,t}+\frac{1}{N_t}\,\theta'\hat y_{i,t},$$
where $\bar x_{i,t}$ is the weight of security $i$ in a benchmark portfolio, $\theta$ is a vector of coefficients to be estimated, and $\hat y_{i,t}$ are the characteristics standardized cross-sectionally to zero mean and unit standard deviation at each date [BrandtSantaClaraValkanov2009]. This parameterization captures the idea of active management relative to a benchmark: the intercept is the benchmark weight, and the term $\theta'\hat y_{i,t}$ represents the optimal deviations. Standardization serves two purposes — it makes the cross-sectional distribution of the characteristics stationary, and it implies that the cross-sectional mean of $\theta'\hat y_{i,t}$ is zero, so that the deviations sum to zero and the optimal weights always sum to one. The factor $1/N_t$ is a normalization that allows the weight function to be applied to an arbitrary number of securities.

The crucial aspect is that the coefficients $\theta$ do not vary either across assets or over time. Coefficients constant across assets imply that the policy cares only about the characteristics of the securities, not about the securities themselves: the characteristics fully describe the security for investment purposes. Coefficients constant over time mean that the coefficients that maximize conditional expected utility at each date are the same across all dates, and therefore also maximize unconditional expected utility. This allows $\theta$ to be estimated by maximizing the sample analog of unconditional expected utility:
$$\max_\theta\ \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}u(r_{p,t+1})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}u\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{N_t}\Big(\bar x_{i,t}+\frac{1}{N_t}\theta'\hat y_{i,t}\Big)r_{i,t+1}\Big),$$
for a pre-specified utility function (mean-variance, quadratic, or CRRA) [BrandtSantaClaraValkanov2009]. Since $\theta$ solves an optimization problem with a small number of parameters, the standard errors of $\hat\theta$ and of the weights follow standard GMM theory.

The method has three practical advantages [BrandtSantaClaraValkanov2009]. First, it optimizes portfolios with a very large number of securities, as long as the size of the parameter vector remains small. Second, and related, the optimal weights are less exposed to error maximization and overfitting, because the entire portfolio is optimized by choosing few parameters; the optimized weights turn out to be much less extreme than those of a standard plug-in approach, and stable both in-sample and out-of-sample. Third, the method implicitly accounts for the dependence of expected returns, variances, covariances, and higher-order moments on the characteristics, insofar as cross-sectional differences in these moments affect the portfolio's expected utility. The method extends with parameterizations that constrain the weights to be non-negative and with non-linear parameterizations that allow for interactions between characteristics (for example small stocks with high momentum) [BrandtSantaClaraValkanov2009].

Finally, the idea of parameterizing cross-sectional weights combines naturally with parametric market timing: to let the impact of characteristics vary over time as a function of macroeconomic predictors $z_t$, one sets
$$x_{i,t}=\bar x_{i,t}+\frac{1}{N_t}\,\theta'(z_t\otimes\hat y_{i,t}),$$
with $\otimes$ the Kronecker product: the problem is rewritten as a parameterized cross-sectional choice over an expanded asset space with naively managed portfolios [BrandtSantaClaraValkanov2009]. This is the connection to the following section. The use of cross-sectional characteristics such as size, book-to-market, and momentum links the method to [[26 Market states e la sezione trasversale]] and to [[13 Selezione e costruzione quantitativa]].

## Expanding the Asset Space

The third direct method transforms the conditioned dynamic problem into a static Markowitz problem on an expanded set of assets. It is shown that solving a conditioned problem with parameterized weights $x_t=\theta z_t$ is equivalent to solving an *unconditional* problem on an expanded asset space that includes naively managed portfolios with excess returns $z_t$ for the excess return of each base asset; this makes implementing the dynamic choice no more difficult than the standard Markowitz problem [BrandtSantaClara2006].

Consider the one-period mean-variance problem, assuming that the optimal weights are linear functions of $K$ state variables $z_t$ (which generally include a constant):
$$x_t=\theta z_t,$$
with $\theta$ an $N\times K$ matrix of coefficients. The conditioned problem is $\max_\theta\mathrm{E}_t[(\theta z_t)'r_{t+1}]-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\mathrm{E}_t[((\theta z_t)'r_{t+1})^2]$ (the quadratic term is the *non-central* second moment, as in [BrandtSantaClara2006]). Using the linear algebra identity
$$(\theta z_t)'r_{t+1}=z_t'\theta'r_{t+1}=\mathrm{vec}(\theta)'(z_t\otimes r_{t+1}),$$
where $\mathrm{vec}(\theta)$ stacks the columns of $\theta$, one defines
$$\tilde x=\mathrm{vec}(\theta),\qquad \tilde r_{t+1}=z_t\otimes r_{t+1}.$$
The conditioned problem becomes $\max_{\tilde x}\mathrm{E}_t[\tilde x'\tilde r_{t+1}]-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\mathrm{E}_t[(\tilde x'\tilde r_{t+1})^2]$. Since the same $\tilde x$ maximizes the conditional mean-variance trade-off at all dates (hence without a time index), it also maximizes the *unconditional* trade-off
$$\max_{\tilde x}\ \mathrm{E}[\tilde x'\tilde r_{t+1}]-\frac{\gamma}{2}\mathrm{E}[(\tilde x'\tilde r_{t+1})^2],$$
which is simply the problem of finding the unconditional optimal mean-variance weights $\tilde x$ for the expanded set of $N\times K$ assets with returns $\tilde r_{t+1}$ [BrandtSantaClara2006]. These expanded assets are interpreted as *conditional* (or managed) portfolios: each invests in a single base asset an amount proportional to the value of a state variable. Given the unconditional solution
$$\tilde x^\star=\frac{1}{\gamma}\,\mathrm{E}[\tilde r_{t+1}\tilde r_{t+1}']^{-1}\,\mathrm{E}[\tilde r_{t+1}],$$
the conditional weight invested in each base asset at each date $t$ is recovered by summing the products of the elements of $\tilde x^\star$ and $z_t$. The critical property is that the solution depends only on the *unconditional* moments of the expanded set: it requires no assumption about the conditional joint distribution of returns and states, nor about how conditional moments depend on the states or how the states evolve [BrandtSantaClara2006].

The idea extends to the multi-period case with *timing* portfolios. In a two-period problem, the compounded excess return decomposes (neglecting the compounding term, of two orders of magnitude smaller) into a choice between two intertemporal portfolios, one that holds the risky asset only in the first period and the other only in the second. For an $H$-period problem, the set of timing portfolios is constructed as
$$\tilde r_{t\to t+H}=\Big\{\prod_{\substack{i=0\\ i\neq j}}^{H-1}R^f_{t+i}\,r_{t+j+1}\Big\}_{j=0}^{H-1},$$
where each term represents a portfolio that invests in the risky assets in period $t+j$ and in the risk-free rate in the others, and the static solution $\tilde x^\star=\tfrac{1}{\gamma}\mathrm{E}[\tilde r_{t\to t+H}\tilde r_{t\to t+H}']^{-1}\mathrm{E}[\tilde r_{t\to t+H}]$ is obtained [BrandtSantaClara2006]. The ideas of conditional and timing portfolios can be combined by replacing the risky returns $r_{t+j+1}$ with the returns of conditional portfolios $z_{t+j}\otimes r_{t+j+1}$; the resulting optimal weights give the optimal allocations to the conditional portfolios at each date. The assumption that the weights are linear in the states is innocuous, since $z_t$ can include non-linear transformations of more basic variables, and linear weights are interpreted as general functions $g(y_t)$ approximable by a polynomial expansion [BrandtSantaClara2006].

The appeal of the method is its simplicity: all the statistical techniques designed for the static mean-variance problem apply directly to the single- and multi-period market-timing problems. Macro conditioning thus becomes a simple set of timing portfolios — for example more stocks when the term spread is high — and the problem is solved with the static optimizer of [[02 Selezione media-varianza]]. This construction is akin to the factor timing of [[27 Factor timing e rotazione dei fattori]].

## The Methodological Map and the Trade-offs

The entire path from conditional moments to optimal weights is organized along a fundamental axis [Brandt2010]. On one side, the traditional two-stage approach: modeling the distribution of returns — by plugging in point estimates (plug-in) or by forming a subjective belief (decision-theoretic/Bayesian) — and then solving for the weights. On the other, the approach that skips the first stage and infers directly on the weights, with nonparametric methods (kernel regressions on conditional Euler conditions), semiparametric methods (the state index $Z_t'\beta$), and parametric methods (weights as a linear function of states and characteristics).

The criterion for choosing along this axis is the trade-off with estimation error. The plug-in method is unbiased or consistent but extremely imprecise in finite samples: its imprecision is scaled by the size of the weights, worsens quadratically with the number of assets, and produces extreme and unstable weights, with the optimizer acting as an "error maximizer" [JobsonKorkie1980; Michaud1989]. Remedies within the first stage — shrinkage, factor models, constraints — reduce variability at the cost of bias, and are interpretable in a unified way, since both Bayesian shrinkage and constraints are equivalent to contractions [JagannathanMa2003]. The decision-theoretic approach integrates out parameter and model uncertainty, recognizing that this contributes to the subjective variance of returns as much as intrinsic uncertainty [Avramov2002].

The approach that models the weights directly attacks the error at its root, through dimensionality reduction: where modeling the returns of 500 assets requires over 125,000 parameters, the final result is only 500 weights, and focusing on them reduces the room for misspecification and estimation error [Brandt2010]. Variable selection via the index $Z_t'\beta$ avoids the curse of dimensionality by collapsing the state vector into a univariate index with $\sqrt T$ convergence, and the utility loss due to the collapse turns out empirically negligible [AitSahaliaBrandt2001]. Parametric policies reduce the entire choice to a few coefficients $\theta$ constant across assets and over time, estimated by maximizing sample average utility without estimating the covariance matrix, yielding weights that are less extreme and stable both in-sample and out-of-sample [BrandtSantaClaraValkanov2009]. Expanding the asset space reduces the conditioned dynamic choice to the static Markowitz problem on managed portfolios, depending only on unconditional moments [BrandtSantaClara2006].

A common trait of the direct methods is the consistency between the estimation loss function and the economic objective: inferring on the weights lends itself to a loss based on expected utility, avoiding the inconsistency of estimating the return model with quadratic loss and then switching to expected utility in the optimization [Brandt2010]. With this, the map closes: the macroeconomic signals of [[29 Segnali macroeconomici e premi al rischio attesi]] — the slope of the term structure, the credit spread, the dividend yield — enter as the state vector $z_t$ or as the index $Z_t'\beta$; through one of the routes described they become optimal weights, in the form of a timing allocation, a deviation from the benchmark, or the solution of a convex problem; and constrained, non-extreme weights are precisely what the convex optimizer of [[14 Ottimizzazione convessa - coni, dualità e KKT]] is equipped to produce. This is the pipeline that carries raw data through to weights.
