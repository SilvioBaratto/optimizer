> [!abstract] Summary
> The chapter positions stress testing as an ex-ante, forward-looking complement to risk measures based on historical data, reconstructing the governance and scenario-design principles codified by the Basel Committee and their justification as it emerged from the financial crisis. On the methodological side it establishes the systematic scenario selection of Breuer, Jandačka, Rheinberger, and Summer: plausibility measured via the Mahalanobis distance, the search for Maximum Loss within a given plausibility ellipsoid, and the identification of the key risk factors that makes a scenario useful. The result is a dashboard in which the stress test integrates Value-at-Risk, Conditional Value-at-Risk, and drawdown measures, filling their blind spots at the extremes.

## Beyond Risk Under Normal Conditions

The risk measures of the preceding chapters — Value-at-Risk and coherent measures from [[04 Misure di rischio coerenti]], Conditional Value-at-Risk from [[16 Ottimizzazione del CVaR - la formulazione di Rockafellar-Uryasev]], drawdown-based measures from [[20 Misure di rischio da drawdown]] — describe a portfolio's behavior under ordinary conditions, estimating risk from historically observed statistical relationships. Stress testing addresses the complementary question: what happens at the extremes, when large shocks materialize.

A stress test is commonly described as the evaluation of a bank's financial position under a severe but plausible scenario, to support decisions [BCBS2009]. The term designates not only the mechanics of applying individual tests, but the entire context within which they are developed, evaluated, and used in decision-making. Stress testing is a risk management tool that complements other approaches and measures, and plays a particularly important role in: providing forward-looking risk assessments; overcoming the limitations of models and historical data; supporting internal and external communication; feeding capital and liquidity planning procedures; informing the definition of the bank's risk tolerance; and facilitating the development of mitigation or contingency plans across a range of stressed conditions [BCBS2009].

Its importance is greatest after long periods of benign economic and financial conditions, when fading memory of downturns can induce complacency and risk underestimation; and it is central during expansion periods, when innovation generates new products that grow rapidly and for which historical loss data are scarce or absent [BCBS2009]. The Basel II regulatory framework anchors stress testing on a regulatory basis: Pillar 1 requires banks using the Internal Models Approach for market risk to have a rigorous stress-testing program, and banks adopting internal ratings-based approaches for credit risk must conduct credit risk stress tests to assess the robustness of their internal capital assessments [BCBS2009], [BCBS2005].

## The Weaknesses Revealed by the Crisis

The depth and duration of the financial crisis led many banks and supervisory authorities to question whether pre-crisis stress-testing practices were adequate. Not only did the crisis prove, in many respects, more severe than indicated by banks' stress test results, but it was possibly aggravated by weaknesses in the practices themselves [BCBS2009]. The Basel Committee identifies four areas of weakness.

*Use and integration in risk governance.* Board and senior management involvement is critical to ensuring the appropriate use of stress testing in risk governance and capital planning. At many banks stress testing was conducted as an exercise isolated from the risk function, with little interaction with business areas, so much so that the latter often considered the analysis not credible; at others it was a mechanical exercise. Many banks also lacked an overarching stress-testing program, instead conducting separate tests for individual risks or portfolios, with limited firm-wide integration, reducing the ability to identify correlated tail exposures and risk concentrations [BCBS2009].

*Methodologies.* Most risk management models, including stress tests, use historical statistical relationships to assess risk, assuming that risk is driven by a known and constant statistical process. The crisis revealed serious flaws in such an approach: after a long period of stability, retrospective historical information indicated benign conditions, so the models captured neither the possibility of severe shocks nor the buildup of vulnerabilities in the system; and historical relationships, such as correlations, proved unreliable once events began to unfold. Extreme reactions, by definition, occur rarely and carry little weight in models based on historical data, making them difficult to model quantitatively [BCBS2009].

*Scenario selection.* Most bank stress tests were not designed to capture the extreme market events actually experienced. Scenarios tended to reflect mild shocks, assumed shorter durations, and underestimated correlations across positions, risk types, and markets arising from systemic interactions and feedback effects. Before the crisis, 'severe' scenarios typically produced losses no larger than a quarter's earnings, and often much smaller. At many banks it was difficult to obtain senior management buy-in for more severe scenarios: scenarios considered extreme or innovative were often judged implausible by the board and senior management [BCBS2009].

*Specific risks and products.* Not covered in sufficient detail were, among others: the behavior of complex structured products under stressed liquidity conditions; pipeline or securitization risk; basis risk in hedging strategies; counterparty credit risk; contingent risks; funding liquidity risk. In particular, stress tests of structured products failed to recognize that the risk dynamics of such instruments differ from those of similarly rated cash instruments, such as bonds [BCBS2009].

## Principles for a Stress-Testing Program

Based on this assessment, the Basel Committee formulates principles for banks and supervisory authorities, applied proportionately to the size and complexity of the activity [BCBS2009]. The principles cover governance, use, methodology and scenario design, and specific areas.

*Governance and integration.* Stress testing must be an integral part of the bank's overall governance and risk management culture and must be actionable, with results influencing decision-making at the appropriate management level, including the strategic decisions of the board and senior management. The board bears ultimate responsibility for the program; senior management is responsible for its implementation, management, and oversight. A program must be documented by written policies and procedures, and rest on an infrastructure sufficiently robust and flexible to accommodate diverse and changing tests at the appropriate level of granularity [BCBS2009].

*Complementary risk perspective.* A program must promote risk identification and control and provide a complementary and independent perspective relative to other tools such as Value-at-Risk and economic capital. Since stress testing allows simulating shocks that have not yet occurred, it should be used to assess the robustness of models against possible changes in the economic and financial environment, challenging the projected risk characteristics of new products and simulating scenarios in which the statistical relationships embedded in the models break down. The outcomes of stress tests for a given portfolio can in particular provide indications about the validity of statistical models at high confidence intervals, such as those used to determine VaR [BCBS2009]. Stress testing must be an integral part of the Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP) and feed into capital and liquidity planning processes.

*Methodology and scenario selection.* Stress tests must cover a range of risks and business areas, integrating them meaningfully on a firm-wide basis to provide a complete picture of risk, taking into account the interrelationships among risk factors. Impact is typically assessed against one or more measures: asset values; accounting profit/loss; economic profit/loss; regulatory capital or risk-weighted assets; economic capital requirements; liquidity and funding gaps [BCBS2009]. Programs must cover a range of scenarios, including forward-looking ones, and aim to account for systemic interactions and feedback effects. The Committee notes that ex-ante estimation of the probabilities of stress events is problematic, since the statistical relationships used to derive them tend to break down under stressed conditions: hence the importance of giving appropriate weight to expert judgment in defining relevant scenarios with a forward-looking perspective. Stress tests must present a range of severities, including events capable of generating the greatest harm in terms of loss size or reputational damage [BCBS2009].

*Specific areas.* The effectiveness of risk mitigation techniques — hedging, netting, use of collateral — must be systematically challenged and assessed under stressed conditions, in which markets may not function fully and several institutions may simultaneously pursue similar mitigation strategies. The program must explicitly cover complex and bespoke products such as securitized exposures, considering the underlying assets, their exposure to systematic market factors, relevant contractual arrangements, embedded triggers, and the impact of leverage; it must cover pipeline and warehousing risks; capture the effect of reputational risk by integrating risks from off-balance-sheet vehicles; and strengthen the approach toward highly leveraged counterparties, assessing potential wrong-way risk [BCBS2009].

## The Problem of Hand-Picked Scenarios

The quality of a stress test depends crucially on the definition of the scenarios. Defining stress scenarios is a mental experiment, a counterfactual exercise in which the risk manager tries to imagine which adverse or even catastrophic events could hit their portfolio [BreuerEtAl2009]. Such a mental experiment is exposed to two major pitfalls: considering implausible scenarios and neglecting plausible ones.

Thinking in terms of scenarios requires imagining situations perhaps not yet occurred but possible in the future. The bias toward historical experience can lead to ignoring plausible but harmful scenarios that have not yet occurred historically, creating a dangerous blind spot. At the opposite extreme, if the tester's imagination assigns excessive weight to highly implausible scenarios, management faces an awkward decision: should one react to alarming results from highly implausible scenarios? [BreuerEtAl2009].

Breuer, Jandačka, Rheinberger, and Summer give a precise operational definition to the three requirements the Basel Committee specifies for stress tests: the *plausibility* of scenarios, their *severity*, and their *usefulness* in suggesting risk-mitigating actions [BreuerEtAl2009], [BCBS2005]. The basic idea is to define a suitable plausibility region in terms of the distribution of risk factors and to systematically search for the scenario of maximum portfolio loss over this region. The method sits within a classical quantitative risk management framework: a portfolio of financial instruments is given, and the value of each position at a given future horizon depends on $n$ systematic risk factors $\boldsymbol{r} = (r_1,\dots,r_n)$ and $m$ idiosyncratic risk factors $\epsilon_1,\dots,\epsilon_m$. Uncertainty about the realization of the risk factors is described by a distribution estimated from historical data; plausibility is captured by specifying how far one moves into the tails of that distribution [BreuerEtAl2009]. In their formulation, the distribution of the systematic factors $\boldsymbol{r}$ is restricted to the class of elliptical distributions, of which $\text{Cov}$ and $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ denote the covariance matrix and expected value; the standard distributions of classical risk management belong to this class [FangEtAl1987]. The distribution of the idiosyncratic factors can be arbitrary.

## Plausibility: the Mahalanobis Distance

In a stress test one imagines extreme realizations of one or more systematic risk factors. How should the plausibility of this mental experiment be quantified? An intuitive approach compares the extreme realization of a factor with its mean: the farther one moves from the mean value, the less plausible the scenario becomes. Distance should be measured in standard deviations; for multivariate movements, plausibility must also depend on the correlations, since a movement consistent with the correlations is more plausible than one that contradicts them [BreuerEtAl2009].

The statistical concept that formalizes these ideas is the Mahalanobis distance [Mahalanobis1936], given by
$$\text{Maha}(\boldsymbol{r}) := \sqrt{(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{T} \cdot \text{Cov}^{-1} \cdot (\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{\mu})}.$$
It is simply the distance of the test point $\boldsymbol{r}$ from the center of mass $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ divided by the width of the ellipsoid in the direction of the test point. Intuitively, $\text{Maha}(\boldsymbol{r})$ is interpreted as the number of standard deviations of the multivariate movement from $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ to $\boldsymbol{r}$; it accounts for the correlation structure and the standard deviations of the risk factors [BreuerEtAl2009].

Contrary to earlier literature, plausibility is defined directly in terms of $\text{Maha}(\boldsymbol{r})$: a high Maha value implies low plausibility of the scenario. Earlier works defined plausibility in terms of the *probability mass* of the ellipsoid of all scenarios with equal or lower Maha [Studer1997]. This creates the problem of the *dimensional dependence of the maximum loss*: if plausibility were defined via the ellipsoid containing a fixed probability mass, the maximum loss would depend on the number of systematic risk factors, which is to some extent arbitrary. An example illustrates the problem: given a bond portfolio with risk factors consisting of two yield curves in ten foreign currencies, a risk manager who models the curve with seven maturity buckets uses 150 factors, another who uses fifteen arrives at 310 factors; if both choose a 95 percent probability-mass ellipsoid as the plausibility region, the second would compute a maximum loss 1.4 times larger than the first, even though looking at the same portfolio and the same plausibility level [Breuer2008]. By defining plausibility via the Mahalanobis radius, rather than via probability mass, this problem does not arise [BreuerEtAl2009].

## Partial Scenarios: How to Treat the Non-Stressed Factors

Portfolios are typically modeled with hundreds or thousands of risk factors. Scenarios involving the entire multitude of factors are hardly tractable numerically and complex to interpret. A practicable response consists in designing *partial scenarios*, which involve only a few risk factors — for example, in a loan portfolio, a certain exchange-rate movement or a particular GDP decline. How should the other factors be treated? [BreuerEtAl2009].

Four ways of treating the factors not fixed by a partial scenario can be distinguished [Kupiec1998]:
(i) the other systematic factors remain at their *last observed value*;
(ii) the other macro factors take their *unconditional expected value*;
(iii) the other systematic factors take their *expected value conditional* on the values of the fixed factors, giving the vector $\boldsymbol{r}_C$;
(iv) the other systematic factors are not fixed but distributed according to the distribution conditional on the values of the fixed factors, giving the vector $\boldsymbol{r}_D$.

The first result of Breuer, Jandačka, Rheinberger, and Summer suggests a choice among these alternatives based on the concept of plausibility. Assuming the distribution of the systematic factors is elliptical, with density strictly decreasing in Maha, the following holds [BreuerEtAl2009]:
$$\text{Maha}(\boldsymbol{r}_C) = \text{Maha}(\boldsymbol{r}_D),$$
and this is the maximum plausibility attainable among all macro scenarios that agree on the fixed factors. The result has substantial practical relevance: it is the basis of partial scenario analysis. In the literature on loan portfolio stress testing it has been suggested to use method (iv), which indeed maximizes plausibility [BontiEtAl2005]; the result shows that equivalent plausibility is obtained using the computationally more efficient approach (iii), that is, setting the non-fixed factors to their conditional expected value. Assigning the non-fixed factors values other than the conditional expected value leads to less plausible macro scenarios.

## Severe Scenarios: The Systematic Search for Maximum Loss

An important drawback of stress testing with hand-picked scenarios is the danger of ignoring harmful but plausible scenarios, which can create an illusion of safety. The way to overcome it is to systematically search, over a plausible feasibility domain, for the macro scenarios most harmful to the portfolio: by systematically searching over feasible domains of plausible macro scenarios, one is certain not to miss any harmful but plausible scenario [BreuerEtAl2009]. This is the approach for building a stress test: finding the most harmful relevant scenarios that are nonetheless above a minimum plausibility threshold. The problem is formulated as an optimization and solved numerically with a dedicated algorithm [Pistovcak2004].

The feasibility domain is determined by the plausibility concept and contains all scenarios with Maha below a threshold $k$:
$$\text{Ell}_k := \{\boldsymbol{r} : \text{Maha}(\boldsymbol{r}) \le k\}.$$
Geometrically this is an ellipsoid whose shape is determined by the covariance matrix of the systematic factors. Since a partial scenario does not specify a single portfolio value but a distribution — that of the values conditional on the factors fixed by the scenario — measuring severity requires quantifying the severity of the corresponding conditional distribution. The expected value is used: a partial scenario is severe if it has a low Conditional Expected Profit (CEP). The stress-testing method thus reduces to solving the optimization problem
$$\min_{\boldsymbol{r}\in \text{Ell}_k} \text{CEP}(\boldsymbol{r}).$$

The difference between the lowest CEP in the feasibility domain and the CEP in the expected scenario is the *Maximum Loss* in the feasibility domain. This maximum-loss concept overcomes the dimensional-dependence problem: the maximum expected loss on $\text{Ell}_k$ is not affected by the exclusion or inclusion of macro factors irrelevant to the portfolio's value [Breuer2008], [BreuerEtAl2009].

Worst-case search has three advantages over standard stress testing. First, it achieves a controlled trade-off between plausibility and severity: choosing a higher $k$ yields worse but less plausible scenarios, choosing a lower one yields more plausible but less severe scenarios. Second, it overcomes historical bias by considering all plausible scenarios, so that plausible scenarios not yet occurred historically are not lost. Third, worst-case scenarios reflect portfolio-specific dangers: what is a worst case for one portfolio can be harmless for another, an aspect standard stress testing does not capture [BreuerEtAl2009].

## Useful Scenarios: Key Risk Factors and Risk-Mitigating Action

Risk-mitigating action is suggested by identifying the key risk factors, those that contribute most to the expected loss in the worst-case scenario. Key factors are defined as those with the highest Maximum Loss Contribution (MLC). The Loss Contribution (LC) of risk factor $i$ to the loss in a scenario $\boldsymbol{r}$ is [BreuerEtAl2009]
$$LC(i,\boldsymbol{r}) := \frac{CEP(\boldsymbol{\mu}) - CEP(\mu_1,\dots,\mu_{i-1}, r_i, \mu_{i+1},\dots,\mu_n)}{CEP(\boldsymbol{\mu}) - CEP(\boldsymbol{r})},$$
if $CEP(\boldsymbol{r}) \neq CEP(\boldsymbol{\mu})$. It is the loss that would occur if factor $i$ took the value it has in scenario $\boldsymbol{r}$ while the other factors remained at their expected values $\boldsymbol{\mu}$, expressed as a percentage of the loss in scenario $\boldsymbol{r}$. In particular, considering the worst-case scenario $\boldsymbol{r} = \boldsymbol{r}^{WC}$, the loss contribution of factor $i$ is called the Maximum Loss Contribution:
$$MLC(i) := LC(i,\boldsymbol{r}^{WC}).$$

The maximum loss contributions of the macro factors generally do not sum to 100 percent: sometimes the sum is larger, sometimes smaller. The link with factor interaction is characterized precisely. Assuming that the CEP, as a function of the macro factors, has continuous second derivatives, the contributions sum to 100 percent for all scenarios $\boldsymbol{r}$,
$$\sum_{i=1}^{n} LC(i,\boldsymbol{r}) = 1,$$
if and only if the CEP has the additive form
$$CEP(r_1,\dots,r_n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} g_i(r_i),$$
that is, if and only if all cross derivatives of the CEP vanish identically for $i \neq j$:
$$\frac{\partial^2 CEP(\boldsymbol{r})}{\partial r_i \partial r_j} = 0 \quad (i \neq j).$$
The sum of the contributions measures the role of systematic-risk interaction for the portfolio's value. If the sum is greater than one, the interaction among factors is positive and the total loss in the scenario is smaller than the sum of the losses from the individual movements. The most dangerous case is negative interaction: if the sum is less than one, the total loss is greater than the sum of the losses from the individual movements, and the scenario's damage cannot be fully explained by the individual movements — the simultaneous movement of some factors causes additional damage relative to the individual movements [BreuerEtAl2009].

A consequence external to the stress-testing problem is that analyzing portfolio risk along the separate categories of market and credit risk, and determining risk capital by aggregating these separately computed figures, can underestimate the true portfolio risk, because it ignores the risks arising from simultaneous movements in market and credit factors [BreuerEtAl2009]. Knowledge of the key factors allows designing risk-mitigating actions: one strategy consists in purchasing hedges that pay off exactly when the key factors take their worst-case value; a more complete but more costly strategy purchases hedges that neutralize the damage produced not only by worst-case movements, but by all movements of the key factors [BreuerEtAl2009], [BreuerEtAl2008].

## An Illustration: Foreign Currency Loans

The concepts are illustrated on a stress test for a portfolio of variable-rate loans in domestic currency (EUR) or foreign currency (CHF). The value of each position depends on the systematic factors GDP, domestic interest rate $r_h$, foreign interest rate $r_f$, and exchange-rate change $E$; the distribution of these factors is modeled by a time-series model that accounts for economic interaction among countries and regions. The profit distribution is computed in a Monte Carlo simulation generating 100,000 scenario paths of four steps each; the resulting, non-normal distribution of the factors after the final quarter is used to estimate the covariance matrix of the annual changes [BreuerEtAl2009].

The comparison between hand-picked and systematic search is stark. The hand-picked scenario 'GDP contracts by 3 percent' is a $5{,}42\sigma$ event; comparing it with the worst-case scenario of the *same* plausibility, one observes that for all portfolios the conditional expected profit is considerably lower in the worst case. In the hand-picked GDP scenario expected profits decline only moderately, which could give a sense of safety; but this is an illusion. There are other equally plausible and much more damaging scenarios: for the foreign-currency portfolios the worst-case losses, on the order of 11 percent, exceed the total regulatory capital of 8 percent of the loan portfolio [BreuerEtAl2009].

Identifying the key factors depends on the portfolio. For the foreign-currency loan portfolio the exchange rate is clearly the key factor: in the worst-case scenario the exchange rate alone contributes between 65.3 percent and 100 percent of the losses, while the other factors contribute less than 1 percent. For the domestic portfolio the key factor is GDP, which alone contributes between 46.8 percent and 70.0 percent of the worst-case losses; the negative interaction between GDP and interest-rate movements explains about a third of the worst-case loss. This simultaneous-movement effect is not visible by looking at losses from individual factor movements: for a B+ domestic loan restricted to movements with Maha less than $k=6$, the MLCs of the two factors sum to 62.0 + 9.9 = 71.9 percent, considerably less than 100 percent, signaling that the loss from the joint movement is considerably greater than the sum of the losses from the individual movements [BreuerEtAl2009].

## Reverse Stress Testing

The range of severity must include events capable of generating the greatest harm, both in terms of loss size and reputational damage; a stress-testing program must also determine which scenarios could challenge the bank's viability — *reverse stress tests* — thereby uncovering hidden risks and interactions [BCBS2009]. A reverse stress test starts from a known stress outcome — such as breaching regulatory capital ratios, illiquidity, or insolvency — and then asks which events could lead to such an outcome for the bank. It is important to include some extreme scenarios that would render the bank insolvent, that is, stress events that threaten the survival of the entire firm; for a large, complex firm this is a demanding exercise requiring the involvement of senior management and all material risk areas. A reverse stress test induces consideration of scenarios beyond normal business contexts and leads to events with contagion and systemic implications [BCBS2009].

Before the crisis such analysis was considered of little value by senior management, since the event had only a remote probability of occurring; now banks express the need to examine tail events and assess actions to address them. Areas that particularly benefit from reverse stress testing are business lines where traditional models indicate an exceptionally good risk/return trade-off, new products and markets that have not experienced severe stress, and exposures lacking liquid two-way markets [BCBS2009].

The systematic search for the worst case provides the quantitative counterpart of this exercise. While the direct stress test fixes the plausibility level $k$ and searches for the maximum-loss scenario within $\text{Ell}_k$, the reverse exercise starts from a loss level and a plausibility level and searches for the scenario that produces it. Because the method searches exhaustively over the entire feasibility domain of given plausibility, it ensures that no harmful but plausible scenario is missed, preventing a false sense of security; it does not analyze scenarios that are too implausible, which would compromise the credibility of the analysis; and it allows portfolio-specific identification of the key factors. The key contribution to the maximum loss can be revealed only by accounting for the simultaneous movements of the risk factors [BreuerEtAl2009].

## The Stress Test in the Risk Dashboard

Stress testing does not replace risk measures under normal conditions, but complements them by providing an independent, complementary risk perspective. The Basel Committee explicitly positions the stress test as a complement to tools based on complex quantitative models that use retrospective data and estimated statistical relationships — in particular Value-at-Risk and economic capital — and highlights that stress test outcomes can provide indications about the validity of statistical models at high confidence intervals, such as those used to determine VaR [BCBS2009].

A dashboard thus emerges in which the measures of [[04 Misure di rischio coerenti]] and [[16 Ottimizzazione del CVaR - la formulazione di Rockafellar-Uryasev]] describe the loss distribution within the sample experience, while the stress test acts as an ex-ante complement at the extremes: where the VaR and Conditional Value-at-Risk respectively quantify a quantile and the expected loss beyond that quantile, and where the drawdown measures of [[20 Misure di rischio da drawdown]] describe cumulative peak-to-trough losses, the stress test assesses the portfolio's robustness to severe but plausible scenarios that historical statistical relationships fail to capture, since such relationships tend to break down precisely under stressed conditions [BCBS2009]. In the systematic formulation, severity is measured by the maximum expected loss within the plausibility ellipsoid $\text{Ell}_k$, and the trade-off between severity and plausibility is governed by the Mahalanobis radius $k$; the choice of $k$ plays, for the stress test, the role that the confidence level plays for VaR and CVaR, anchoring the degree of extremity of the scenario to a defined, non-arbitrary plausibility region [BreuerEtAl2009]. The compatibility of these concepts with the standard quantitative risk management framework is what makes the method directly pluggable into the existing dashboard.
