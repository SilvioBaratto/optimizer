> [!abstract] Summary
> The chapter establishes that the state of the market is not constant but alternates between persistent regimes — bullish/bearish, high/low volatility — and that recognizing the current regime is the first step in forming a view on the opportunity set. It presents the Markov regime-switching model and the filter that recursively estimates its probabilities, shows how regimes and time-varying volatility/correlations modify optimal allocation and the cost of ignoring them, and links the real-time measurement of the macro state (nowcasting and diffusion index) to the formation of the views that the Black-Litterman model then ingests.

## The Notion of Regime: Abrupt and Persistent Changes in Market Behavior

Financial markets often change their behavior abruptly. Some changes are transient (isolated jumps), but frequently the new price behavior persists for many periods: the mean, volatility, and correlation structure of equity returns can change radically and remain at the new level, as happened at the onset of the 2008-2009 financial crisis [AngTimmermann2012]. Some of these changes are recurrent — recessions versus expansions, calm versus turbulent markets, bull versus bear phases — while others are unique, associated with structural breaks in regulation, policy, or secular changes; in interest rates, for example, a distinct regime corresponds to the period 1979-1982, during which the U.S. central bank changed its operating procedure [AngTimmermann2012].

Regime-switching models capture this alternation. Their appeal, according to the survey of the field by [AngTimmermann2012], rests on several grounds. First, the idea of a regime is natural and intuitive: the original application of [Hamilton1989] concerned precisely business-cycle recessions and expansions, with regimes closely tied to ex post turning-point datings. Second, these models parsimoniously capture stylized properties of returns — fat tails, persistent turbulent periods followed by calm periods (ARCH effects), skewness, and time-varying correlations — by suitably mixing conditionally normal distributions; they also contain jump models as a special case (a regime abandoned immediately in the following period). Third, since normality (or log-normality) is recovered conditional on the regime, pricing under regimes remains analytically tractable and often solvable in closed form.

A regime is conceptually close to the familiar notion of a "good" and "bad" state, or a low- and high-risk state. As will be seen, however, embedding regimes in an equilibrium model produces results that can contradict intuition: while linear pricing models imply a positive and monotonic risk-return relation [Merton1973], switches between discrete regimes with different consumption growth rates can generate increasing, decreasing, flat, or non-monotonic risk-return relations [AngTimmermann2012]. This chapter first addresses how the latent state is modeled and estimated, then how it modifies portfolio allocation, and finally how the current macro state can be measured in real time from raw data — closing with the link to the formation of views for the Black-Litterman model of the chapter [[03 Limiti della MPT e il modello di Black-Litterman]].

## The Markov Regime-Switching Model (Hamilton)

The contribution of [Hamilton1989] proposes a tractable approach to modeling regime changes: the parameters of an autoregressive process are seen as the outcome of a latent state governed by a discrete-state Markov chain. Let $n_t$ be the trend component of a series $\tilde y_t$. We say $n_t$ follows a *Markov trend in levels* if
$$n_t = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 s_t + n_{t-1},$$
where $s_t \in \{0,1\}$ denotes the unobserved state of the system. Transitions between states are governed by a first-order Markov process:
$$\Pr[S_t=1\,|\,S_{t-1}=1]=p,\quad \Pr[S_t=0\,|\,S_{t-1}=1]=1-p,$$
$$\Pr[S_t=0\,|\,S_{t-1}=0]=q,\quad \Pr[S_t=1\,|\,S_{t-1}=0]=1-q.$$
The process for $S_t$ is strictly stationary and admits an AR(1) representation,
$$s_t = (1-q) + \lambda s_{t-1} + v_t, \qquad \lambda \equiv -1+p+q,$$
from which it follows that the conditional probability converges to the unconditional limiting probability
$$\Pr[S_t=1] = \pi \equiv \frac{1-q}{(1-p)+(1-q)}.$$
The econometrician does not directly observe $s_t$, but must draw probabilistic inference about it from the observed behavior of the series. This is the substantive difference from the earlier approach: while works such as Sclove's maximized the joint likelihood of the series and historical states assumed observable, [Hamilton1989] maximizes the marginal likelihood of the observed series with respect to the population parameters and then uses these parameters to infer the unobserved state. The root of the method is Goldfeld and Quandt's Markov-switching regression [GoldfeldQuandt1973], applied to the parameters of an autoregressive process.

**Stochastic specification.** Combining the trend with a cyclical component, [Hamilton1989] considers $\tilde y_t = n_t + \tilde z_t$, where $\tilde z_t$ follows an ARIMA$(r,1,0)$ process. Differencing and setting $y_t \equiv \tilde y_t - \tilde y_{t-1}$ and $z_t \equiv \tilde z_t - \tilde z_{t-1}$, one obtains
$$y_t = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 s_t + z_t, \qquad z_t = \phi_1 z_{t-1} + \cdots + \phi_r z_{t-r} + \varepsilon_t,$$
with $\{\varepsilon_t\}$ i.i.d. $N(0,\sigma^2)$ independent of $\{n_{t+j}\}$. The growth rate does not change every period, but only in response to discrete and occasional events; added to a linear normal process, the term $\alpha_1 s_t$ generates a non-linear process for the observed series.

## The Non-Linear Filter and Maximum Likelihood Estimation

The operational core of the model is a filter that, given the observed data, recursively estimates the regime probabilities. The *basic filter* takes as input the joint conditional probability
$$\Pr[S_{t-1}=s_{t-1},\dots,S_{t-r}=s_{t-r}\,|\,y_{t-1},y_{t-2},\dots]$$
and returns the analogous probability updated at time $t$, together, as a byproduct, with the conditional likelihood $f(y_t\,|\,y_{t-1},\dots)$. The algorithm proceeds in five steps [Hamilton1989].

*Step 1.* One computes the joint probability at time $t$ conditioned on the past,
$$\Pr[S_t=s_t,\dots,S_{t-r}=s_{t-r}\,|\,y_{t-1},\dots] = \Pr[S_t=s_t\,|\,S_{t-1}=s_{t-1}]\times \Pr[S_{t-1}=s_{t-1},\dots,S_{t-r}=s_{t-r}\,|\,y_{t-1},\dots],$$
where $\Pr[S_t=s_t\,|\,S_{t-1}=s_{t-1}]$ is given by the transition probabilities.

*Step 2.* One multiplies by the conditional density of $y_t$, which under the Gaussian specification is
$$f(y_t\,|\,S_t=s_t,\dots,S_{t-r}=s_{t-r},y_{t-1},\dots) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}\exp\!\left[-\frac{\big((y_t-\alpha_0-\alpha_1 s_t)-\phi_1(y_{t-1}-\alpha_0-\alpha_1 s_{t-1})-\cdots-\phi_r(y_{t-r}-\alpha_0-\alpha_1 s_{t-r})\big)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right],$$
obtaining the joint density-probability of $y_t$ and the states.

*Step 3.* One sums over the states to obtain the marginal likelihood $f(y_t\,|\,y_{t-1},\dots)=\sum_{s_t}\cdots\sum_{s_{t-r}} f(y_t,S_t=s_t,\dots)$.

*Step 4.* By Bayes' theorem the state probability is updated,
$$\Pr[S_t=s_t,\dots,S_{t-r}=s_{t-r}\,|\,y_t,\dots] = \frac{f(y_t,S_t=s_t,\dots\,|\,y_{t-1},\dots)}{f(y_t\,|\,y_{t-1},\dots)}.$$

*Step 5.* One sums over $s_{t-r}$ to obtain the output, which serves as input for the next iteration. By construction all probabilities generated by the filter lie in $[0,1]$ and sum to one.

A byproduct of Step 3 is the conditional sample log-likelihood
$$\log f(y_T,\dots,y_1\,|\,y_0,\dots) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log f(y_t\,|\,y_{t-1},\dots),$$
which is numerically maximized with respect to the parameters $(\alpha_0,\alpha_1,p,q,\sigma,\phi_1,\dots,\phi_r)$. The model is identified only up to the labeling of the states, which is fixed by convention (for example, setting $\alpha_1>0$, so that state 1 is the fast-growth one). The filter admits straightforward generalizations to more than two states (the sums run over $(0,\dots,n-1)$ instead of $\{0,1\}$) and to higher-order dependencies. A *smoother* provides inference about the state using the information available up to $t$ or the entire sample, $\Pr[S_t=s_t\,|\,y_t,y_{t-1},\dots]$ and its full-sample version.

**Application to U.S. GDP.** Applied to real quarterly GDP, with $y_t$ equal to 100 times the change in the log of real GDP over the period 1952:II–1984:IV, the maximum-likelihood method produces $\hat\alpha_1=1{,}522$, $\hat\alpha_0=-0{,}3577$, $\hat p=0{,}9049$, $\hat q=0{,}7550$, $\hat\sigma=0{,}7690$, with autoregressive coefficients $\hat\phi_1\approx 0$, $\hat\phi_2\approx 0$, $\hat\phi_3=-0{,}247$, $\hat\phi_4=-0{,}213$ [Hamilton1989]. Contrary to the long-run trend hypothesis, the likelihood is maximized by a negative growth rate of $-0{,}4\%$ per quarter in state 0 and a positive growth of $+1{,}2\%$ in state 1 ($\alpha_0+\alpha_1$): the states coincide with business-cycle phases, not with secular variations. The estimated state sequence coincides remarkably well with official recession datings and can serve as an alternative objective algorithm for dating the cycle. The expected duration of a recession, conditional on being in state 0, is $\sum_{k=1}^\infty k\,q^{k-1}(1-q)=(1-q)^{-1}\approx 4{,}1$ quarters, against a historical average of 4.7; that of an expansion is $(1-p)^{-1}\approx 10{,}5$ quarters. The transition from expansion to recession is associated with a permanent GDP drop of about 3%.

## Statistical Properties of Regimes: Tails, Skewness, Correlations

Regime-switching models capture the central stylized features of returns. The survey of [AngTimmermann2012] starts from the canonical model in which a variable depends on its own past, on random shocks, and on the regime; restricting regimes to modify the intercept, autocorrelation, and volatility:
$$y_t = \mu_{s_t} + \phi_{s_t} y_{t-1} + \sigma_{s_t}\varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \sim iid(0,1),$$
with the state $s_t$ following a first-order homogeneous Markov chain with transition matrix $\Pi_{[i,j]}=\Pr(s_t=j\,|\,s_{t-1}=i)=p_{ij}$. With recurring regimes, all states recur over time if $p_{ii}<1$; with a change-point process the set of regimes expands and past regimes do not return.

**Fat tails and skewness.** Consider the case without autoregressive terms, $y_t=\mu_{s_t}+\sigma_{s_t}\varepsilon_t$ with $\varepsilon_t\sim iid\,N(0,1)$ and unconditional probability $\pi_0$ of state 0: this is a mixture of two normals. The first four central moments are
$$E[y_t]=\pi_0\mu_0+(1-\pi_0)\mu_1,$$
$$\mathrm{Var}(y_t)=\pi_0(1-\pi_0)(\mu_0-\mu_1)^2+\pi_0\sigma_0^2+(1-\pi_0)\sigma_1^2,$$
$$\mathrm{skew}(y_t)=\pi_0(1-\pi_0)(\mu_0-\mu_1)\big[(1-2\pi_0)(\mu_0-\mu_1)^2+3(\sigma_0^2-\sigma_1^2)\big],$$
with an analogous expression for kurtosis. The differences in means between regimes, $\mu_0-\mu_1$, enter the higher moments: the variance is not the simple average of the variances, because the switch to a regime with a different mean adds a source of risk; skewness arises only if $\mu_0\neq\mu_1$. For the mixture of a $N(1,1)$ with probability $0{,}8$ and a $N(-2,2^2)$ with probability $0{,}2$, the mean is $-0{,}50$, the standard deviation $2{,}18$ (larger than the largest individual deviation), and the skewness coefficient $-0{,}65$ [AngTimmermann2012].

**Persistence.** The autocovariances of the level and of the square,
$$\mathrm{cov}(y_t,y_{t-1})=\pi_0(1-\pi_0)(\mu_0-\mu_1)^2[p_{00}+p_{11}-1],$$
$$\mathrm{cov}(y_t^2,y_{t-1}^2)=\pi_0(1-\pi_0)(\mu_0^2-\mu_1^2+\sigma_0^2-\sigma_1^2)^2[p_{00}+p_{11}-1],$$
show that autocorrelation of the first moments requires differences in means, while volatility persistence can be induced by differences in means or in variances; in both cases persistence increases with the combined persistence of the regimes, measured by $p_{00}+p_{11}-1$.

**Time-varying correlations and exceedance correlations.** A stylized fact is that correlations increase during market downturns [LonginSolnik2001]. Regime models reproduce this thanks to the persistence of the probabilities of remaining in a regime with low mean, high volatility, and high correlation. The diagnostic tool is the *exceedance correlation*: given an exceedance level $\theta$, one selects the subset of bivariate observations $\{(y_1,y_2)\,|\,y_1\geq(1+\theta)\bar y_1 \text{ and } y_2\geq(1+\theta)\bar y_2\}$ for $\theta\geq 0$ (and symmetrically for $\theta\leq 0$), computing the correlation of that subset. In the data, exceedance correlations of equity returns show a pronounced asymmetric pattern, with negative exceedance correlations higher than positive ones; a regime-switching model closely replicates this pattern, while neither a normal distribution (which implies symmetric exceedance correlations tending to zero) nor an asymmetric GARCH model can [AngTimmermann2012, LonginSolnik2001].

## Regimes, Equilibrium, and the Risk-Return Relation

A regime model is not merely empirical: embedded in an equilibrium setting, it generates realistic dynamics in risk-return relations. Starting from the representative-agent pricing model of [Lucas1978], let $P_t$ be the price of the stock that pays a dividend $D_t$; the Euler equation is
$$P_t\,U'(C_t) = \beta\,E_t\big[U'(C_{t+1})(P_{t+1}+D_{t+1})\big].$$
Assuming power utility $U(C)=C^{1+\gamma}/(1+\gamma)$ and consumption equal to the dividend $C_t=D_t$, [AngTimmermann2012] considers a dividend process subject to switches both in mean and in volatility:
$$D_{t+1}=D_t\exp\!\big(\alpha_0+\alpha_1 s_{t+1}+(\sigma_0+\sigma_1 s_{t+1})\varepsilon_{t+1}\big),\qquad \varepsilon_t\sim iid\,N(0,1),$$
with $s_t\in\{0,1\}$ following the two-state Markov chain independent of $\varepsilon_t$. Investors know $s_t$ at time $t$ (but not $s_{t+1}$) and set prices conditional on the prevailing regime. Conditioning on the future regime and weighting by the transition probabilities yields a closed-form expression for the expected future dividend, even though dividend growth is not log-normal. The solution for the price takes the form $P_t=\rho(s_t)D_t$, with a price-dividend ratio constant within each regime but dependent in a highly non-linear way on the process parameters and preferences, and assuming a finite number of values equal to the number of regimes.

The intuition, with persistent high- and low-growth regimes, is twofold. Starting from the high-growth regime, investors expect high future growth of the endowment: this lowers the relative price of future endowments, increases saving and demand for the risky asset, and thus raises the current price; conversely, the desire to smooth consumption intertemporally leads them to consume more today and sell the risky asset, lowering the price. Which effect dominates depends on the concavity of utility: with $\gamma=-1$ (logarithmic utility) the two effects cancel out and the price-dividend ratio is independent of the regime; with $\gamma>-1$ the intertemporal relative-price effect prevails; with $\gamma<-1$ the consumption-smoothing effect prevails. It follows that the conditional expected return depends on the current regime and is time-varying, and the usual monotonic and linear relation between equity premium and conditional variance may not hold: when $\gamma$ is close to $-1$ the average return can be highest in the high-growth regime while the variance of returns is highest in the low-growth regime. Regimes in the consumption/dividend process thus endogenously generate differences in expected returns and volatility, as well as skewness, kurtosis, serial correlation, and volatility clustering in equilibrium returns.

**Learning about the unobserved state.** When regimes are not identifiable in real time, the state is a latent variable and a filtering problem arises: agents update their beliefs with Bayes' rule, weighing how likely new observations are under the different regimes. Even with known parameters, since the regime is rarely known with certainty and can change abruptly, the dynamics of learning affect equilibrium prices: agents' attempt to hedge against their own regime uncertainty can generate patterns of over- and under-reaction to information, and higher price volatility in periods of high uncertainty about the state, typically around recessions [AngTimmermann2012]. Simulation shows that the filtered probability tracks the underlying regime with good accuracy, but sometimes misses an important change and sometimes raises false alarms — testifying to the difficulty of real-time tracking.

## Portfolio Allocation with Two Regimes: The Opportunity Set Changes with the State

The fact that correlations and volatilities increase in bear markets has led some to doubt the benefits of international diversification. To formally assess this claim, [AngBekaert2002] solves the dynamic choice problem of a U.S. investor who rebalances across $N$ assets every month over a horizon of $T$ months:
$$\max_{\alpha_0,\dots,\alpha_{T-1}} \; E_0[U(W_T)], \qquad \text{s.t. } \alpha_t'\mathbf{1}=1,$$
with CRRA utility $U(W_T)=W_T^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$ and gross portfolio return $R_{t+1}(\alpha_t)=\sum_{j=1}^N \exp(y^j_{t+1})\alpha^j_t$. The opportunity set is time-varying and modeled with a regime-switching process. Introducing regimes $s_t=1,\dots,K$ following a Markov chain with transition probabilities $p_{ij,t}=p(s_{t+1}=j\,|\,s_t=i,\mathcal{F}_t)$, the density of $y_{t+1}$ conditional on $s_t$ is a mixture
$$g(y_{t+1}\,|\,s_t=i;\mathcal{F}_t)=\sum_{j=1}^K p_{ij,t}\,f(y_{t+1}\,|\,s_{t+1}=j),$$
which allows fat tails, persistent stochastic volatility, and other properties of equity returns to be captured. The benchmark model is
$$y_{t+1}=\mu(s_{t+1})+\Sigma^{1/2}(s_{t+1})\,\epsilon_{t+1},$$
with two states and transition matrix
$$\begin{pmatrix} P & 1-P \\ 1-Q & Q \end{pmatrix},\qquad P=p(s_{t+1}=1\,|\,s_t=1),\; Q=p(s_{t+1}=2\,|\,s_t=2).$$

**The two regions of the opportunity set.** Estimated on monthly equity returns for the United States, the United Kingdom, and Germany, the model identifies a regime (regime 1) with a lower conditional mean, much higher volatility, and higher correlations — a "bear" regime — and a regime (regime 2) with a higher mean, lower volatility, and lower correlations. Volatility and correlations rise together, in agreement with empirical evidence; the strongest differentiating effect between regimes is volatility, whose equality across regimes is rejected at very low significance levels, while the evidence of different correlations and means is weaker. The expected duration of regime 1 is about 6.9 months, that of regime 2 is 4.25 years. The regime model reproduces the asymmetric exceedance correlations of the data where the normal model and the asymmetric GARCH fail.

**Regime-conditional choice and hedging demands.** When returns are i.i.d., the optimal CRRA weights are constant and the $T$-period problem reduces to the myopic one-period problem [AngBekaert2002]. With regimes, the optimal weights become functions of the current state, $\alpha_t^*(s_t)$, and the persistence of regimes induces intertemporal hedging demands: the investor wants to hedge against future unfavorable changes in the opportunity set, so that weights at different horizons differ from current ones. For normal distributions with switching, the first-order conditions have no closed-form solution and are solved numerically by quadrature; the introduction of regimes makes the one-period first-order condition a linear combination of the conditions for each regime, weighted by the transition probabilities known at $t$. Since U.S. volatility is lower in regime 1, the United States constitutes the "safest" asset, and risk-averse investors hold more U.S. equity in both regimes, and even more so in the bear regime. The estimated transition matrix is of the *momentum* type (more likely to continue in the same state than to switch to the other): under a momentum process, as the horizon lengthens risk-averse investors *reduce* their exposure to risky assets, in contrast to the opposite-signed *rebound* process [AngBekaert2002].

**The cost of ignoring regimes.** The economic cost of following suboptimal strategies is measured as a monetary compensation in "cents per dollar" of wealth, that is, the change in the certainty equivalent. Three costs are assessed: that of not diversifying internationally, that of ignoring regime changes (using i.i.d. weights), and that of myopia. The cost of ignoring regimes is small for all-equity portfolios — because the high-volatility regime mainly induces a shift toward lower-volatility assets, which i.i.d. weights approximate reasonably well — but increases significantly when a conditionally risk-free asset is available, since the high-volatility, high-correlation regime induces a dramatic shift toward cash (flight to cash). In that case the cost of ignoring regimes becomes of the same order of magnitude as the cost of not diversifying internationally, on the order of about one cent per dollar at the annual horizon for a risk aversion of 5, with costs increasing at longer horizons. The cost of myopia, on the other hand, is negligible for all the models: intertemporal hedging demands are statistically insignificant in the benchmarks considered. The main conclusion is that the existence of a high-volatility bear regime does *not* eliminate the benefits of international diversification.

## Four Economically Interpretable Multi-Asset Regimes (Guidolin-Timmermann)

Extending the analysis to multiple asset classes, [GuidolinTimmermann2007] studies the allocation decisions of a U.S. investor across large caps, small caps, and long-term bonds (with cash as the residual). The model for the joint distribution is a regime-switching vector autoregression that allows the mean, covariance, and serial correlations to vary with the state $S_t$:
$$\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf r_t \\ \mathbf z_t \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol\mu_{s_t} \\ \boldsymbol\mu_{z s_t} \end{pmatrix} + \sum_{j=1}^{p} \mathbf A_{j,s_t}\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf r_{t-j} \\ \mathbf z_{t-j} \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol\varepsilon_t \\ \boldsymbol\varepsilon_{zt} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad (\boldsymbol\varepsilon_t'\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon_{zt}')' \sim N(\mathbf 0,\boldsymbol\Omega_{s_t}),$$
with $S_t$ following a first-order Markov chain with transition matrix $\mathbf P$ of constant probabilities $\Pr(s_t=i\,|\,s_{t-1}=j)=p_{ji}$. Since the state is unobservable, it is treated as a latent variable and the parameters are updated with Hamilton's EM algorithm [Hamilton1989]. With $k=1$ the model reduces to the linear single-state model. Tests based on information criteria and diagnostics select a four-state model; specifications with fewer states or with constant volatility across states are found to be clearly misspecified.

**The four regimes.** The four states are economically interpretable [GuidolinTimmermann2007]. Regime 1 is a *crash* state characterized by large negative average excess returns and high volatility; it includes the two oil shocks of the 1970s, the October 1987 crash, the early 1990s, and the "Asian flu." Regime 2 is a *slow growth* state, with low volatility and small positive returns. Regime 3 is a persistent *bull* state in which equity prices — especially of small firms — grow rapidly. Regime 4 is a *recovery* state with strong market rebounds and high volatility for small caps and bonds. Correlations vary substantially across regimes: the correlation between large and small caps ranges from 0.82 in the crash to 0.50 in the recovery, and the correlation between stocks and bonds changes sign, from $-0{,}40$ in the crash to positive values in the other states. Stationary state probabilities are 9% (crash), 40% (slow growth), 28% (bull), and 23% (recovery); average durations are about two months for the crash, seven for slow growth, eight for the bull, and about three for the recovery. Correlations between smoothed state probabilities and recession datings are positive for the high-volatility states (0.32 for the crash, 0.18 for the recovery), confirming that these states occur around recession periods.

**Optimal weights as state probabilities vary.** The investor has power utility $u(W_{t+T})=W_{t+T}^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$, with $\gamma>1$. Since the state is unobservable, learning is incorporated by letting the investor optimally revise their beliefs about the underlying state at each instant, with the updating equation
$$\boldsymbol\pi_{b+1}(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_t)=\frac{(\boldsymbol\pi_b'\hat{\mathbf P}^{\varphi})'\odot\boldsymbol\eta(\mathbf y_{b+1};\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_t)}{[(\boldsymbol\pi_b'\hat{\mathbf P}^{\varphi})'\odot\boldsymbol\eta(\mathbf y_{b+1};\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_t)]'\boldsymbol\iota_k},$$
where $\odot$ is the element-wise product and $\boldsymbol\eta$ the vector of observation densities in each state. Optimal allocations vary considerably as the state probabilities vary and over time, as the investor revises their estimates. Since equities are unattractive in the crash state, the short-horizon investor holds very few equities in it; but, since the crash is not very persistent and has a high probability of switching to recovery, the equity allocation in this state *increases* with the horizon (an increasing curve). In the more persistent slow-growth and bull states, short-horizon investors hold large equity positions, which decrease at long horizons because opportunities will almost certainly worsen (a decreasing curve). The relation between equity demand and horizon is thus non-monotonic and depends on the state: the common advice to increase equity exposure as the horizon lengthens is not robust and holds in only one of the four regimes. The buy-and-hold problem corresponds to solving the allocation only once; introducing rebalancing, as its frequency increases the positions respond much less to the horizon, reflecting the possibility of modifying the allocation before the end of the horizon.

**Welfare cost of ignoring regimes.** The economic value of accounting for regimes is measured by the compensating premium $\eta_t^{IID}$ that makes the investor indifferent between the strategy that exploits regimes and the one constrained to i.i.d. weights. Since the i.i.d. strategy is a special constrained case, its value function cannot exceed that of the four-state model. The cost of ignoring regimes reaches 3% over short horizons — where market timing is exploited more aggressively — and about 130 basis points per year over longer horizons; accounting for parameter uncertainty, the cost remains economically significant, with the lower bound of the confidence band reaching 7–8% of wealth at long horizons. Out-of-sample forecasting experiments confirm that correctly specifying the number of regimes — four instead of two — is economically important and leads to significantly higher realized utility [GuidolinTimmermann2007].

## Measuring the Current Macro State from Raw Data: Nowcasting and Diffusion Index

Recognizing the regime requires a timely measure of the current macroeconomic state. Real-time decisions are based on assessments of current and future economic conditions built from incomplete data: most data are published with a lag and subsequently revised, and reconstructing GDP, inflation, and other key variables for the current quarter is a central task [GiannoneReichlinSmall2008]. *Nowcasting* formalizes the updating of the current estimate as new data are published within the month.

**Nowcasting as a factor model.** The goal is the projection of the variable of interest onto the available information set, $\mathrm{Proj}[GDP_t\,|\,\Omega^n_v]$, where $\Omega^n_v$ includes $n$ monthly series (in practice about 200 series for the U.S. economy) up to month $v$. Since the series are published non-synchronously, the panel is unbalanced at the end of the sample; this makes it impossible to discard information and requires projecting onto an unbalanced dataset. The framework is the parametric dynamic factor model: each stationary series decomposes into a common component and an idiosyncratic one,
$$y_{it|v_j}=\mu_i+\lambda_i F_t + \xi_{it|v_j},$$
where $\chi_{it}\equiv\lambda_i F_t$ is the common component, a function of a few unobserved common factors $F_t$ that capture "almost all" the comovements of the economy, and $\xi_{it|v_j}$ is the idiosyncratic component, orthogonal, which also absorbs revision errors. The factor dynamics are
$$F_t = A F_{t-1} + B u_t, \qquad u_t \sim WN(0,I_q),$$
with $B$ of full rank $q$ and the roots of $\det(I_r - Az)$ outside the unit circle. In empirical estimates $r=10$ factors and $q=2$ pervasive shocks: the economy is described as driven by two large shocks with heterogeneous dynamics. The factors are consistently estimated by principal components, after which the Kalman filter updates the signal estimate on the unbalanced panel,
$$\hat F_{t|v_j}=\mathrm{Proj}[F_t\,|\,\Omega_{v_j};\hat A,\hat B,\hat\Psi];$$
by imposing infinite idiosyncratic variance when a variable is not yet available, the filter assigns zero weight to missing series in the computation of the factors.

**News and real-time uncertainty.** Every release expands the information set from $\Omega_{v_{j-1}}$ to $\Omega_{v_j}$; the *news* induced by the release of block $j$ is the change in the current estimate,
$$NEWS[i,v_j]=\hat y_{it|v_j}-\hat y_{it|v_{j-1}},$$
which is not a standard Wold forecast error, since the number of variables varies from month to month and is affected by the order in which the data arrive. The uncertainty associated with the projection, $Vz^q_{k|v_j}=E[(\hat z^q_{k|v_j}-z^q_k)^2]$, decreases as the dataset expands and splits into a component due to uncertainty about the common factors and one due to the idiosyncratic component. The framework also allows distinguishing the marginal contribution of a release due to *timeliness* from that due to *quality*, defined as the marginal impact holding the release date fixed. The main empirical findings are that intra-monthly information matters — the precision of the signal increases monotonically over the course of the quarter — and that business surveys (the Philadelphia Federal Reserve Bank surveys) have a large marginal impact on the nowcast, greater than that of the employment report, because they arrive earlier; controlling for timeliness, the contribution of "hard" data (prices and real variables) becomes substantial. Interest rates affect the precision of the GDP estimate but not of inflation, while asset prices affect inflation but not GDP [GiannoneReichlinSmall2008].

**The diffusion index.** The factor representation that compresses hundreds of series into a few state factors is formalized by [StockWatson2002] in the context of forecasting. The variable to be forecast and a vector $X_t$ of $N$ predictors admit an approximate dynamic factor model:
$$y_{t+1}=\beta(L)f_t+\gamma(L)y_t+\epsilon_{t+1}, \qquad X_{it}=\lambda_i(L)f_t+e_{it},$$
with $e_t$ idiosyncratic disturbances and $\bar r$ common dynamic factors $f_t$. Assuming finite lags, the model is rewritten in static form
$$y_{t+1}=\beta' F_t + \gamma(L)y_t + \epsilon_{t+1}, \qquad X_t = \Lambda F_t + e_t,$$
where $F_t$ collects the factors and their lags. The advantage is that the factors — the *diffusion indexes* — can be estimated by principal components even for very large $N$; under suitable moment conditions and an asymptotic rank condition on $\Lambda$, the feasible forecast is asymptotically efficient to the first order, its mean squared error approximating that of the infeasible optimal forecast as $N,T\to\infty$. The $h$-step-ahead forecast is
$$\hat y^h_{T+h|T}=\hat\alpha_h+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\hat\beta_{hj}\hat F_{T-j+1}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\hat\gamma_{hj}\,y_{T-j+1}.$$
On 215 U.S. monthly series, the first six factors explain 39% of the variance and the first twelve explain 53%: a few factors suffice. The first factor loads mainly on production and employment, the second on interest rate spreads, unemployment, and capacity utilization, the third on interest rates, the fourth on equity returns, the fifth on inflation, the sixth on housing starts. In the presence of missing data, unbalanced panels, or mixed frequencies, the factors are estimated with the EM algorithm. Out of sample, forecasts based on diffusion indexes clearly outperform univariate autoregressions, VARs, and leading-indicator models [StockWatson2002]. The root of this idea traces back to the dynamic generalization of classical factor analysis by [SargentSims1977] and to the notion of a common business cycle.

## From Estimated Regime to View: The Bridge to Black-Litterman

The elements gathered in this chapter compose a chain that leads from the state of the market to the portfolio view. On one hand, the Markov regime-switching model and its filter provide, at every instant, a probabilistic estimate of the current regime — $\Pr(s_t\,|\,I_t)$ — from observed returns [Hamilton1989, AngTimmermann2012]; on the other, nowcasting as a factor model and the diffusion index provide in real time a measure of the current macroeconomic state, updated with every data release [GiannoneReichlinSmall2008, StockWatson2002]. Since regimes in the consumption/dividend process translate into regimes in asset returns [AngTimmermann2012], the two measures are complementary: as noted by the survey of the field, it remains an open question whether regimes inferred from returns — observed at high frequency — can be used to forecast regimes in macro variables, sampled at low frequency.

The relevance for portfolio selection is direct. The investment opportunity set changes with the state: correlations and volatilities rise in bear markets, expected returns and higher moments are conditioned on the regime, and the optimal weights $\alpha_t^*(s_t)$ vary as the state probabilities vary [AngBekaert2002, GuidolinTimmermann2007]. The investor who exploits a higher-Sharpe-ratio frontier when the low-volatility regime prevails, and who shifts toward the risk-free asset when the bad regime prevails, does better than one who ignores regimes and sits on the unconditional frontier — the average of the individual regimes' frontiers [AngTimmermann2012].

It is here that the Black-Litterman model of the chapter [[03 Limiti della MPT e il modello di Black-Litterman]] connects. The estimated market regime — whether the market is in a crash, slow-growth, expansion, or recovery state, and with what probability — is precisely what views on expected returns and covariances are formed from, which that model then ingests and combines with market equilibrium [BlackLitterman1992]. The regime provides the economic content of the view: the filtered probability of being in a bear regime justifies a view of lower expected returns and higher correlations, which the Black-Litterman-style updating translates into a tilt of the weights relative to the market portfolio. In this sense, recognizing the state of the market is the first step — not the last — in the chain leading to allocation.
