> [!abstract] Summary
> The chapter transforms Conditional Value-at-Risk from a risk measure into a minimizable objective function. Following Rockafellar and Uryasev, it establishes that a single auxiliary function — convex in the portfolio weights and in a threshold variable — jointly minimized, simultaneously returns the Value-at-Risk (the optimal threshold) and the Conditional Value-at-Risk (the minimum value), and that on scenarios the CVaR minimization problem reduces to a linear program. It is finally shown that CVaR is a coherent risk measure even for general and discontinuous loss distributions, its representation as a weighted average of tail losses is given, and its relationship to Expected Shortfall is clarified.

## From CVaR as a Measure to CVaR as an Objective Function

In the chapter [[04 Misure di rischio coerenti]], Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) was introduced as a risk measure; here it becomes the quantity to be minimized in portfolio choice. The starting point is the observation, advanced by [RockafellarUryasev2000], that Value-at-Risk (VaR), despite its high status and its being written into industry regulations, has undesirable mathematical characteristics: it lacks subadditivity and convexity, as shown by [ArtznerEtAl1999], and is coherent only when based on the standard deviation of normal distributions. Moreover, when computed from scenarios, VaR is difficult to optimize: as a function of portfolio positions it can be ill-behaved and exhibit multiple local extrema, which is a major obstacle in determining an optimal mix. CVaR, by contrast, is known to have better properties: [RockafellarUryasev2000] recall the result of [Pflug2000] that it is a coherent risk measure, with the properties of translation-equivariance, positive homogeneity, convexity, and monotonicity with respect to stochastic dominance.

Consider a decision vector $\mathbf{w}$, interpretable as a portfolio to be chosen from a feasible set $X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}$, and a random vector $\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}$ representing the uncertainties — for example in market parameters — capable of affecting the outcome. To each pair $(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})$ is associated a loss $f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})$; the loss can be negative and in effect constitute a gain [RockafellarUryasev2000]. For each $\mathbf{w}$, the loss $f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})$ is a random variable whose distribution is induced by that of $\mathbf{y}$, with density $p(\mathbf{y})$. The probability that the loss does not exceed a threshold $\alpha$ is
$$\Psi(\mathbf{w},\alpha)\;=\;\int_{f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})\le\alpha}p(\mathbf{y})\,d\mathbf{y},$$
that is, as a function of $\alpha$ with $\mathbf{w}$ fixed, the cumulative distribution function of the loss associated with $\mathbf{w}$: it completely determines the behavior of the random variable and is fundamental in defining VaR and CVaR [RockafellarUryasev2000].

Fixing a probability level $\beta\in(0,1)$ — typically $0{,}90$, $0{,}95$, or $0{,}99$ in applications — the $\beta$-VaR of the loss associated with $\mathbf{w}$ is the smallest amount $\alpha$ such that, with probability $\beta$, the loss does not exceed it:
$$\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})\;=\;\min\{\,\alpha\in\mathbb{R}:\Psi(\mathbf{w},\alpha)\ge\beta\,\}.$$
The $\beta$-CVaR is the conditional expectation of the loss in the part of the distribution that exceeds this amount:
$$\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})\;=\;(1-\beta)^{-1}\int_{f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})\ge\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})}f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})\,p(\mathbf{y})\,d\mathbf{y}.$$
In this first exposition it is assumed, for simplicity, that the distributions are such that they present no jumps, that is, that $\Psi(\mathbf{w},\alpha)$ is everywhere continuous with respect to $\alpha$; the probability that $f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})\ge\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})$ is then equal to $1-\beta$, and $\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})$ turns out to be the conditional expectation of the loss given that it is $\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})$ or greater [RockafellarUryasev2000]. The definitions ensure that the $\beta$-VaR is never greater than the $\beta$-CVaR: portfolios with low CVaR necessarily also have low VaR. Removing the continuity assumption is addressed later, following [RockafellarUryasev2002].

## The Rockafellar-Uryasev Auxiliary Function

The difficulty of working directly with $\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})$ lies in the fact that its definition depends on the value $\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})$ of the VaR, which often has troublesome mathematical properties. The central contribution of [RockafellarUryasev2000] is the characterization of $\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})$ and $\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})$ by means of a single auxiliary function, defined on $X\times\mathbb{R}$,
$$F_{\beta}(\mathbf{w},\alpha)\;=\;\alpha+(1-\beta)^{-1}\int_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}[\,f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})-\alpha\,]^{+}\,p(\mathbf{y})\,d\mathbf{y}\;=\;\alpha+(1-\beta)^{-1}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left\{[\,f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})-\alpha\,]^{+}\right\},$$
where $[t]^{+}=\max\{0,t\}$, that is, $[t]^{+}=t$ for $t>0$ and $[t]^{+}=0$ for $t\le0$. The variable $\alpha$ is a free threshold; the integral term accumulates, rescaled by the factor $(1-\beta)^{-1}$, only the excesses of the loss beyond $\alpha$.

The crucial property is stated in the first theorem of [RockafellarUryasev2000]: as a function of $\alpha$, $F_{\beta}(\mathbf{w},\alpha)$ is convex and continuously differentiable, and the $\beta$-CVaR is obtained by minimizing it:
$$\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})\;=\;\min_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}\,F_{\beta}(\mathbf{w},\alpha).$$
The set of values of $\alpha$ at which the minimum is attained,
$$A_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})\;=\;\arg\min_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}F_{\beta}(\mathbf{w},\alpha),$$
is a nonempty, closed, and bounded interval (possibly reduced to a single point), and the $\beta$-VaR is the left endpoint of $A_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})$. In particular one always has
$$\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})\in\arg\min_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}F_{\beta}(\mathbf{w},\alpha)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})=F_{\beta}(\mathbf{w},\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})).$$
The power of these formulas is manifest: a continuously differentiable convex function is particularly easy to minimize numerically, and it further turns out that the $\beta$-CVaR can be computed without first having to compute the $\beta$-VaR on which its definition depends — the VaR is obtained, if anything, as a byproduct [RockafellarUryasev2000].

The mechanism linking the optimal threshold to the VaR emerges clearly from the derivative calculation, carried out by [RockafellarUryasev2002] in the proof of the general version of the theorem. The difference quotient is
$$\frac{F_{\beta}(\mathbf{w},\alpha')-F_{\beta}(\mathbf{w},\alpha)}{\alpha'-\alpha}=1+(1-\beta)^{-1}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left\{\frac{[f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})-\alpha']^{+}-[f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})-\alpha]^{+}}{\alpha'-\alpha}\right\}.$$
Passing to the limit as $\alpha'\downarrow\alpha$ and using that the expectation on the right-hand side tends to $-[1-\Psi(\mathbf{w},\alpha)]$, one obtains the right derivative
$$\frac{\partial^{+}F_{\beta}}{\partial\alpha}(\mathbf{w},\alpha)=1+\frac{\Psi(\mathbf{w},\alpha)-1}{1-\beta}=\frac{\Psi(\mathbf{w},\alpha)-\beta}{1-\beta},$$
and, symmetrically, the left derivative $\partial^{-}F_{\beta}/\partial\alpha=(\Psi(\mathbf{w},\alpha^{-})-\beta)/(1-\beta)$, where $\Psi(\mathbf{w},\alpha^{-})$ is the left limit. By convexity the minimum is attained at the points where $\partial^{-}F_{\beta}/\partial\alpha\le0\le\partial^{+}F_{\beta}/\partial\alpha$, that is, where
$$\Psi(\mathbf{w},\alpha^{-})\;\le\;\beta\;\le\;\Psi(\mathbf{w},\alpha).$$
Setting the derivative to zero is thus equivalent to imposing $\Psi(\mathbf{w},\alpha)=\beta$: the threshold that minimizes the auxiliary function is precisely the $\beta$-order quantile of the loss, that is, the VaR (the lower endpoint of the interval of minimizers). This is the precise sense in which minimizing $F_{\beta}$ returns the VaR as the argument of the minimum and the CVaR as the value of the minimum.

## Joint Minimization: Simultaneous VaR and CVaR

The first theorem concerns minimization with respect to $\alpha$ alone, with $\mathbf{w}$ fixed. The second theorem of [RockafellarUryasev2000] extends the result to the joint choice of $\mathbf{w}$ and $\alpha$, and it is this that makes the auxiliary function a portfolio optimization tool. Minimizing the $\beta$-CVaR with respect to $\mathbf{w}\in X$ is equivalent to minimizing $F_{\beta}(\mathbf{w},\alpha)$ with respect to $(\mathbf{w},\alpha)\in X\times\mathbb{R}$, in the sense that
$$\min_{\mathbf{w}\in X}\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})\;=\;\min_{(\mathbf{w},\alpha)\in X\times\mathbb{R}}F_{\beta}(\mathbf{w},\alpha).$$
Moreover a pair $(\mathbf{w}^{*},\alpha^{*})$ attains the second minimum if and only if $\mathbf{w}^{*}$ attains the first and $\alpha^{*}\in A_{\beta}(\mathbf{w}^{*})$. In particular, in the typical case where the interval $A_{\beta}(\mathbf{w}^{*})$ reduces to a single point, the joint minimization of $F_{\beta}$ produces a pair $(\mathbf{w}^{*},\alpha^{*})$ such that $\mathbf{w}^{*}$ minimizes the $\beta$-CVaR and $\alpha^{*}$ provides its corresponding $\beta$-VaR [RockafellarUryasev2000]. A single minimization problem, therefore, computes the VaR and optimizes the CVaR simultaneously.

The underlying principle is the standard one in optimization, according to which minimization with respect to $(\mathbf{w},\alpha)$ can be carried out by first minimizing with respect to $\alpha$ for each $\mathbf{w}$ — which returns $\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})$ by the first theorem — and then with respect to $\mathbf{w}$ [RockafellarUryasev2002]. The practical importance lies in the fact that, to determine a $\mathbf{w}$ that gives the minimum $\beta$-CVaR, it is not necessary to work directly with $\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})$, which is arduous given the nature of its definition in terms of the $\beta$-VaR; one can instead work with the much simpler expression $F_{\beta}(\mathbf{w},\alpha)$, with its convexity in the variable $\alpha$ and, very often, in the entire pair $(\mathbf{w},\alpha)$ [RockafellarUryasev2000]. The character of this problem — the presence of an "expectation" in the definition of $F_{\beta}$ — places it within the scope of stochastic optimization, or more specifically stochastic programming, for which a vast literature of numerical algorithms is available [RockafellarUryasev2000].

## Convexity and the Structure of the Portfolio Problem

The property that makes joint minimization tractable is convexity. The second theorem of [RockafellarUryasev2000] adds that $F_{\beta}(\mathbf{w},\alpha)$ is convex with respect to $(\mathbf{w},\alpha)$, and $\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})$ is convex with respect to $\mathbf{w}$, whenever $f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})$ is convex with respect to $\mathbf{w}$. In that case, if the constraints are such that $X$ is a convex set, the joint minimization is an instance of convex programming. The convexity of $\phi_{\beta}$ follows from that of $F_{\beta}$: in convex analysis, minimizing a convex function of two vector variables with respect to one of them, the residual is a convex function of the other [RockafellarUryasev2002]. This is a decisive property in optimization, because it eliminates the possibility that a local minimum differs from the global minimum [RockafellarUryasev2000], and it is the feature in which CVaR is sharply distinguished from VaR (see [[14 Ottimizzazione convessa - coni, dualità e KKT]]).

In the case of portfolio selection, $\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})$ with $w_{j}$ the position in instrument $j$, and, letting $y_{j}$ be the return of instrument $j$, it is assumed that
$$w_{j}\ge0\quad(j=1,\dots,n),\qquad\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}=1.$$
The portfolio return is the sum of the individual instrument returns weighted by the proportions $w_{j}$, and the loss, being its negative, is linear in $\mathbf{w}$:
$$f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})=-[\,w_{1}y_{1}+\dots+w_{n}y_{n}\,]=-\mathbf{w}^{\!\top}\mathbf{y}.$$
The auxiliary function then becomes
$$F_{\beta}(\mathbf{w},\alpha)=\alpha+(1-\beta)^{-1}\int_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}[\,-\mathbf{w}^{\!\top}\mathbf{y}-\alpha\,]^{+}\,p(\mathbf{y})\,d\mathbf{y},$$
convex in $(\mathbf{w},\alpha)$ because the loss is linear in $\mathbf{w}$. Denoting by $\mathbf{m}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ the mean and covariance matrix of $\mathbf{y}$, the loss has mean $\mu(\mathbf{w})=-\mathbf{w}^{\!\top}\mathbf{m}$, linear in $\mathbf{w}$, and variance $\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{w})=\mathbf{w}^{\!\top}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{w}$, quadratic in $\mathbf{w}$; requiring that only portfolios with expected return at least a level $R$ be admitted introduces the linear constraint $\mu(\mathbf{w})\le -R$ [RockafellarUryasev2000]. The resulting feasible set $X$ is convex — indeed polyhedral, due to the linearity of all constraints — and the minimization of $F_{\beta}$ over $X\times\mathbb{R}$ is consequently a convex programming problem.

A precise link to the mean-variance framework of the chapter [[02 Selezione media-varianza]] emerges in the normal case. Under normality assumptions and with $\beta\ge0{,}5$, [RockafellarUryasev2000] express VaR and CVaR in terms of mean and standard deviation:
$$\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})=\mu(\mathbf{w})+c_{1}(\beta)\,\sigma(\mathbf{w}),\qquad c_{1}(\beta)=\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{erf}^{-1}(2\beta-1),$$
$$\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})=\mu(\mathbf{w})+c_{2}(\beta)\,\sigma(\mathbf{w}),\qquad c_{2}(\beta)=\left(\sqrt{2\pi}\,\exp\!\big[(\mathrm{erf}^{-1}(2\beta-1))^{2}\big]\,(1-\beta)\right)^{-1}.$$
Since for $\beta\ge0{,}5$ the coefficients $c_{1}(\beta)$ and $c_{2}(\beta)$ are positive, if the return constraint is active at the optimum, minimizing $\alpha_{\beta}$, minimizing $\phi_{\beta}$, or minimizing $\sigma(\mathbf{w})^{2}$ leads, on the set where $\mu(\mathbf{w})=-R$, to the same portfolio: under normality and with an active constraint, minimum CVaR, minimum VaR, and minimum variance of [Markowitz1952] identify a common portfolio [RockafellarUryasev2000]. This is the case in which CVaR maintains consistency with VaR, that is, the only context — the elliptical one — in which the three approaches coincide [RockafellarUryasev2002].

## The Scenario Formulation and the Linear Program

The decisive advantage of the auxiliary function is that the integral in the definition of $F_{\beta}(\mathbf{w},\alpha)$ does not require an analytical expression for the density $p(\mathbf{y})$: it suffices to have an algorithm that generates samples from it [RockafellarUryasev2000]. Sampling the distribution of $\mathbf{y}$ and obtaining a collection of vectors $\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{q}$, the expectation is approximated by the sample mean, giving
$$\tilde{F}_{\beta}(\mathbf{w},\alpha)=\alpha+\frac{1}{q(1-\beta)}\sum_{k=1}^{q}[\,f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y}_{k})-\alpha\,]^{+}.$$
The expression $\tilde{F}_{\beta}(\mathbf{w},\alpha)$ is convex and piecewise linear with respect to $\alpha$; although not differentiable in $\alpha$, it can be minimized readily, in particular by representation as a linear programming problem [RockafellarUryasev2000]. Since the convexity of $f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})$ with respect to $\mathbf{w}$ produces the convexity of $\tilde{F}_{\beta}$, this property is transmitted to the approximation.

The reduction to a linear program is obtained by introducing, for each scenario, a real auxiliary variable $u_{k}$ representing the excess of the loss beyond the threshold. In the portfolio case, with $f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y}_{k})=-\mathbf{w}^{\!\top}\mathbf{y}_{k}$, minimizing $\tilde{F}_{\beta}$ over $X\times\mathbb{R}$ is equivalent to minimizing the linear expression
$$\alpha+\frac{1}{q(1-\beta)}\sum_{k=1}^{q}u_{k}$$
subject to the linear portfolio constraints ($w_{j}\ge0$, $\sum_j w_{j}=1$, $\mu(\mathbf{w})\le -R$) and the constraints
$$u_{k}\ge0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{w}^{\!\top}\mathbf{y}_{k}+\alpha+u_{k}\ge0\qquad(k=1,\dots,q).$$
The two inequalities jointly impose $u_{k}\ge\max\{0,\,f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y}_{k})-\alpha\}=[\,f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y}_{k})-\alpha\,]^{+}$; since at the optimum the coefficient of $u_{k}$ is positive, each $u_{k}$ is pushed to its minimum feasible value, and the weighted sum exactly reproduces the sample approximation of the excess term [RockafellarUryasev2000]. The unknown variables are the weights $\mathbf{w}$, the threshold $\alpha$, and the $q$ excesses $u_{k}$; the objective and the constraints are all linear. It is fundamental to observe that this reduction to linear programming does not depend on $\mathbf{y}$ having a special distribution, such as the normal: it works for non-normal distributions in exactly the same way [RockafellarUryasev2000].

This is the reason why minimizing the CVaR of a portfolio over scenarios is a problem that can be solved with a linear programming solver: the loss realizations are sampled, an excess variable is introduced per scenario, and the resulting linear program is handed to the solver, which simultaneously returns the optimal weights, the threshold (the VaR), and the objective value (the CVaR). The connection to quantitative portfolio construction is discussed in [[13 Selezione e costruzione quantitativa]]. [RockafellarUryasev2000] observe that approaches such as the mean absolute deviation of [KonnoYamazaki1991] and the minimax of [Young1998] lend themselves to similar schemes, since they too rely on linear programming algorithms.

## CVaR for General Distributions: VaR, Upper-VaR, and the Tail Distribution

The continuity assumption on $\Psi(\mathbf{w},\cdot)$ is convenient but restrictive: many optimization problems under uncertainty involve discontinuous loss distributions, in which discrete probabilities arise from scenario models or from finite sampling of random variables [RockafellarUryasev2002]. In [RockafellarUryasev2002] the limitations are removed and the foundations of the methodology are completed for general distributions. It is assumed that $\mathbf{y}$ is governed by a probability measure $P$ independent of $\mathbf{w}$ — independence is essential for the convexity results underpinning the reductions to linear programming — with the technical assumptions that $f$ is continuous in $\mathbf{w}$, measurable in $\mathbf{y}$, and $\mathbb{E}\{|f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})|\}<\infty$ [RockafellarUryasev2002]. Letting $\Psi(\mathbf{w},\alpha^{-})$ be the left limit of $\Psi(\mathbf{w},\cdot)$ at $\alpha$, the difference
$$\Psi(\mathbf{w},\alpha)-\Psi(\mathbf{w},\alpha^{-})=P\{\mathbf{y}\mid f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})=\alpha\}$$
when positive signals the presence of a probability "atom" at $\alpha$: the cumulative distribution function has a jump.

The VaR retains the definition $\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})=\min\{\alpha\mid\Psi(\mathbf{w},\alpha)\ge\beta\}$; the minimum is attained because $\Psi(\mathbf{w},\cdot)$ is nondecreasing and right-continuous. When $\Psi(\mathbf{w},\cdot)$ is continuous and strictly increasing, $\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})$ is the unique $\alpha$ satisfying $\Psi(\mathbf{w},\alpha)=\beta$; otherwise the equation may have no solution or an entire interval of solutions [RockafellarUryasev2002]. The first case corresponds to a vertical jump in the graph of $\Psi$; the second to a constant stretch. To cover the latter, the upper-VaR is introduced,
$$\alpha_{\beta}^{+}(\mathbf{w})=\inf\{\alpha\mid\Psi(\mathbf{w},\alpha)>\beta\},$$
with $\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})\le\alpha_{\beta}^{+}(\mathbf{w})$ always; the two values coincide except when $\Psi$ is constant at level $\beta$ over an interval. [RockafellarUryasev2002] point out that the situation of multiple solutions entails an instability of the VaR: a jump is certain if a slightly higher confidence level is required — a disquieting degree of instability for a risk measure that can bear enormous sums.

The general CVaR is defined as the mean of the tail distribution of order $\beta$, where this distribution has cumulative distribution function
$$\Psi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w},\alpha)=\begin{cases}0 & \text{for }\alpha<\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w}),\\[2pt] [\,\Psi(\mathbf{w},\alpha)-\beta\,]/[\,1-\beta\,] & \text{for }\alpha\ge\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w}).\end{cases}$$
This is itself a legitimate cumulative distribution function — nondecreasing, right-continuous, with limit $1$ — obtained by rescaling the portion of the graph of $\Psi$ between the levels $\beta$ and $1$ so as to extend it between $0$ and $1$ [RockafellarUryasev2002]. The subtlety lies in the case of an atom at $\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})$: only the part of the atom exceeding the level $\beta$ is aggregated to the interval strictly beyond the threshold, so as to obtain exactly probability $1-\beta$. Alongside the CVaR, the upper-CVaR and lower-CVaR are then defined,
$$\phi_{\beta}^{+}(\mathbf{w})=\mathbb{E}\{f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})\mid f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})>\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})\},\qquad\phi_{\beta}^{-}(\mathbf{w})=\mathbb{E}\{f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})\mid f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})\ge\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})\}.$$
In general $\phi_{\beta}^{-}(\mathbf{w})\le\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})\le\phi_{\beta}^{+}(\mathbf{w})$, with equality when the cumulative distribution function has no jump at the threshold; when a jump exists — and for scenario models this is always the case — the inequalities can be strict [RockafellarUryasev2002]. It is here that CVaR separates from the two conditional quantiles that, for continuous distributions, coincide with it.

## Discrete Representation and the Link to Expected Shortfall

The unusual feature of CVaR that generates its power is the way it "splits" a probability atom present at the threshold [RockafellarUryasev2002]. The proposition on CVaR as a weighted average formalizes this: setting
$$\lambda_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})=[\,\Psi(\mathbf{w},\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w}))-\beta\,]/[\,1-\beta\,]\in[0,1],$$
if there is a possibility of a loss greater than $\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})$ then
$$\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})=\lambda_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})\,\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})+[\,1-\lambda_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})\,]\,\phi_{\beta}^{+}(\mathbf{w}),$$
while, if $\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})$ is the maximum possible loss, $\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})=\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})$ [RockafellarUryasev2002]. The CVaR is thus a weighted average of the VaR and the upper-CVaR, with weights depending on the decision. The corollary immediately follows that CVaR dominates VaR, $\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})\ge\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})$, with strict inequality except when there is no possibility of a loss exceeding the threshold [RockafellarUryasev2002].

In the extreme case of scenario models, in which the probability is concentrated at a finite number of points, the representation becomes explicit. Ordering the loss values as $z_{1}<z_{2}<\dots<z_{N}$ with probabilities $p_{k}>0$, and letting $k_{\beta}$ be the unique index for which
$$\sum_{k=1}^{k_{\beta}}p_{k}\ge\beta>\sum_{k=1}^{k_{\beta}-1}p_{k},$$
the $\beta$-VaR is $\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})=z_{k_{\beta}}$, while the $\beta$-CVaR is
$$\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})=\frac{1}{1-\beta}\left[\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{k_{\beta}}p_{k}-\beta\Big)z_{k_{\beta}}+\sum_{k=k_{\beta}+1}^{N}p_{k}\,z_{k}\right],$$
with residual weight $\lambda_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})=(1-\beta)^{-1}\big(\sum_{k=1}^{k_{\beta}}p_{k}-\beta\big)$ assigned to the threshold value [RockafellarUryasev2002]. CVaR thus appears as a weighted average of the losses in the tail: the VaR value $z_{k_{\beta}}$ receives only the fraction of its probability that exceeds $\beta$, and the more severe losses $z_{k_{\beta}+1},\dots,z_{N}$ enter with their full probability. If the highest point $z_{N}$ has probability $p_{N}>1-\beta$, then $\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})=\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})=z_{N}$: at confidence levels close enough to $1$, CVaR, lower-CVaR, and VaR coincide with the maximum loss [RockafellarUryasev2002].

This structure clarifies CVaR's position in the nomenclature of tail measures. The upper-CVaR $\phi_{\beta}^{+}(\mathbf{w})$ has sometimes been called "mean shortfall," and the closely related quantity $\mathbb{E}\{f-\alpha_{\beta}\mid f>\alpha_{\beta}\}=\phi_{\beta}^{+}(\mathbf{w})-\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})$ goes by the name "mean excess loss"; the lower-CVaR $\phi_{\beta}^{-}(\mathbf{w})$ was named "tail VaR" by [ArtznerEtAl1999] [RockafellarUryasev2002]. The term "expected shortfall" has been interpreted in different ways: [AcerbiEtAl2001] use it in one sense, while [AcerbiTasche2002] take it as synonymous with the CVaR itself [RockafellarUryasev2002]. Since — as will be seen — it is the CVaR, and not the upper-CVaR or lower-CVaR, that is coherent in the presence of discontinuities, the identification of Expected Shortfall with the CVaR is the one that preserves its good properties. [RockafellarUryasev2002] note that the lack of coherence of $\phi_{\beta}^{+}$ and $\phi_{\beta}^{-}$ in the presence of discreteness does not appear to be widely appreciated.

## Coherence of CVaR and Persistence of the Minimization Formula

The fundamental result of [RockafellarUryasev2002] is that the minimization formula developed under special assumptions persists for general distributions. The auxiliary function is rewritten as
$$F_{\beta}(\mathbf{w},\alpha)=\alpha+\frac{1}{1-\beta}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left\{[\,f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})-\alpha\,]^{+}\right\},\qquad[t]^{+}=\max\{0,t\},$$
and the fundamental minimization theorem establishes that, as a function of $\alpha$, $F_{\beta}(\mathbf{w},\cdot)$ is finite and convex (hence continuous), with
$$\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})=\min_{\alpha}F_{\beta}(\mathbf{w},\alpha),$$
where the argmin is a nonempty, closed, and bounded interval of which $\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})$ is the lower endpoint and $\alpha_{\beta}^{+}(\mathbf{w})$ the upper endpoint [RockafellarUryasev2002]. The convexity of $F_{\beta}(\mathbf{w},\cdot)$ follows immediately from the convexity of $[f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})-\alpha]^{+}$ with respect to $\alpha$; no analogous formula holds, however, for $\phi_{\beta}^{+}$ or $\phi_{\beta}^{-}$. The theorem highlights the deep reason why CVaR is better behaved than VaR when it is necessary to handle dependence on a choice $\mathbf{w}\in X$: in a minimization problem, the optimal value — here $\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})$ — is a far more tractable function of the parameters than the set of optimal solutions, which is instead the argmin interval having $\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})$ as its lower endpoint [RockafellarUryasev2002].

The convexity of CVaR in the weights is now stated in full generality. If $f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})$ is convex with respect to $\mathbf{w}$, then $\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})$ is convex with respect to $\mathbf{w}$, and $F_{\beta}(\mathbf{w},\alpha)$ is jointly convex in $(\mathbf{w},\alpha)$ [RockafellarUryasev2002]. If moreover $f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})$ is sublinear in $\mathbf{w}$ — where a function $h$ is sublinear if $h(\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{w}')\le h(\mathbf{w})+h(\mathbf{w}')$ (subadditivity) and $h(\lambda\mathbf{w})=\lambda h(\mathbf{w})$ for $\lambda>0$ (positive homogeneity) — then $\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})$ is sublinear, and $F_{\beta}$ is jointly sublinear. Sublinearity is equivalent to the combination of convexity and positive homogeneity, and linearity is a special case of it [RockafellarUryasev2002]; the linear case $f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})=w_{1}f_{1}(\mathbf{y})+\dots+w_{n}f_{n}(\mathbf{y})$, common to numerous applications, thus falls under sublinearity.

Coherence follows from this. In the framework of [ArtznerEtAl1999], a risk measure $\rho$ on a linear space of random variables (interpreted as losses $z$) is coherent if it is sublinear,
$$\rho(z+z')\le\rho(z)+\rho(z'),\qquad\rho(\lambda z)=\lambda\rho(z)\ \ (\lambda\ge0),$$
satisfies $\rho(z)=c$ when $z\equiv c$ is constant, and obeys the monotonicity $\rho(z)\le\rho(z')$ when $z\le z'$ in the sense of first-order stochastic dominance [RockafellarUryasev2002]. The coherence corollary states that, on the basis of the general definition as the mean of the tail distribution, the $\beta$-CVaR is a coherent risk measure: when $f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})$ is linear in $\mathbf{w}$, $\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})$ is not only sublinear but satisfies $\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})=c$ when $f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})\equiv c$ (accurately reflecting the absence of risk) and the monotonicity $\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})\le\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w}')$ when $f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})\le f(\mathbf{w}',\mathbf{y})$ [RockafellarUryasev2002]. These properties follow from the fundamental minimization formula; the decisive role, for the assertion referring to CVaR as defined on the tail distribution and not merely to the functional defined by the formula, is played precisely by the subtleties of CVaR as an "adjusted" conditional expectation that splits probability atoms. [RockafellarUryasev2002] note that [Pflug2000] had already shown that a measure defined by the expression $\rho(z)=\min_{\alpha}\{\alpha+(1-\beta)^{-1}\mathbb{E}[z-\alpha]^{+}\}$ would be coherent. The coherence of CVaR is a formidable advantage not shared by any other widely applicable risk measure proposed so far [RockafellarUryasev2002]. CVaR moreover possesses stability with respect to the level $\beta$: $\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})$ behaves continuously as $\beta\in(0,1)$ varies and admits right and left derivatives, while other proposed measures are not stable [RockafellarUryasev2002].

## The Computational Link and the Extensions

Joint minimization extends from the special assumptions to the general case. The shortcut theorem of [RockafellarUryasev2002] states that minimizing $\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})$ with respect to $\mathbf{w}\in X$ is equivalent to minimizing $F_{\beta}(\mathbf{w},\alpha)$ over $X\times\mathbb{R}$,
$$\min_{\mathbf{w}\in X}\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})=\min_{(\mathbf{w},\alpha)\in X\times\mathbb{R}}F_{\beta}(\mathbf{w},\alpha),$$
with the characterization of the optimal pairs reproducing that of the continuous case. It follows that VaR and CVaR are computed as a byproduct: if $(\mathbf{w}^{*},\alpha^{*})$ minimizes $F_{\beta}$ over $X\times\mathbb{R}$, then $\mathbf{w}^{*}$ minimizes $\phi_{\beta}$ over $X$, and $\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w}^{*})=F_{\beta}(\mathbf{w}^{*},\alpha^{*})$ holds with $\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w}^{*})\le\alpha^{*}\le\alpha_{\beta}^{+}(\mathbf{w}^{*})$; if the argmin in $\alpha$ reduces to a point, $\alpha_{\beta}(\mathbf{w}^{*})=\alpha^{*}$ [RockafellarUryasev2002]. When $X$ and $f(\cdot,\mathbf{y})$ are convex, the optimization problem is one of convex programming and thus particularly favorable for computation; by contrast, the analogous problem formulated in terms of VaR can be quite unfavorable [RockafellarUryasev2002].

Linear programming techniques also apply in this general setting. When $Y$ is a discrete space with elements $\mathbf{y}_{k}$, $k=1,\dots,N$, of probability $p_{k}$, the auxiliary function is
$$F_{\beta}(\mathbf{w},\alpha)=\alpha+\frac{1}{1-\beta}\sum_{k=1}^{N}p_{k}\,[\,f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y}_{k})-\alpha\,]^{+},$$
and its minimization, or a constraint of the type $\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})\le\omega$, is handled by introducing variables $\eta_{k}\ge0$ with $f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y}_{k})-\alpha-\eta_{k}\le0$ and requiring $\alpha+(1-\beta)^{-1}\sum_{k}p_{k}\eta_{k}\le\omega$; when $f$ is linear in $\mathbf{w}$, these reconstituted constraints are linear [RockafellarUryasev2002]. CVaR can thus enter a model not only as an objective function but also as a constraint, to "model" risk at one or more confidence levels $\beta_{i}$ with tolerances $\omega_{i}$ [RockafellarUryasev2002]. It is in this way, with the conversion to a linear program via per-scenario excess variables, that the problem of minimizing a portfolio's CVaR is handed to a linear programming solver, regardless of the shape of the return distribution.

The methodology lends itself to extensions. In [RockafellarUryasev2002] it is applied to portfolio replication with a CVaR constraint, in which the mean absolute deviation from a benchmark index is minimized while imposing a limit $\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{w})\le\omega$ on the most severe underperformances; the numerical tests, conducted with a linear programming solver, confirm that introducing the CVaR constraint improves the risk characteristics both in-sample and out-of-sample. A closely related notion, conditional drawdown-at-risk (CDaR), is the subject of the chapter [[20 Misure di rischio da drawdown]]. The framework established here — CVaR as a convex objective, joint minimization with the threshold, reduction to a linear program on scenarios, coherence for general distributions — constitutes the basis on which the risk budgeting approaches of the chapter [[17 Risk budgeting e risk parity]] rest and, more generally, the use of tail risk measures in quantitative portfolio construction.
