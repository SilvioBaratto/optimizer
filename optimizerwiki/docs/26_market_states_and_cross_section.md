> [!abstract] Summary
> The chapter establishes that the profitability of cross-sectional selection signals — momentum and value — is not constant but conditioned by the market state: momentum is strong after bull markets and null or negative after bear markets, with long-run reversal, and it crashes in panic phases because its betas are a state-dependent short optionality. The value premium is counter-cyclical and time-varying, explainable both by a volatility-regime model and by conditionally counter-cyclical betas. It is also shown that value and momentum have a common-factor structure across asset classes linked to funding-liquidity risk, and that the same Markov apparatus that governs allocation provides the state that conditions selection.

## The Bridge Between Market State and the Cross-Section

The chapters [[09 Selezione dei titoli su fondamentali]], [[12 Dal segnale all'alpha - information coefficient e la legge fondamentale]], and [[13 Selezione e costruzione quantitativa]] treated cross-sectional selection signals — in particular momentum and value — as if their profitability were a stationary parameter, summarized by an average information coefficient. The chapter [[21 Regimi di mercato e formazione delle view]] introduced, at the opposite extreme, the idea that the market passes through distinct states governed by a latent Markov chain. This chapter builds the bridge between the two: the premium of cross-sectional signals is not a constant but a function of the market state, and the same regime-switching apparatus that conditions allocation across asset classes also conditions selection within them.

The thesis common to the sources is that the expected return of a long-short strategy built on a signal — a zero-cost portfolio that buys securities with a high signal and sells those with a low signal — is time-varying and linked to variables describing the macro-financial state. For momentum the relevant state is the market's past return and its volatility [CooperGutierrezHameed2004; DanielMoskowitz2016]; for value it is the phase of the business cycle, measured by a volatility regime or by state variables approximating the expected market premium [GulenXingZhang2011; PetkovaZhang2005]. Across asset classes, value and momentum share a common-factor structure traceable to a single funding-liquidity state [AsnessMoskowitzPedersen2013]. The methodological glue is the multivariate regime-switching model [GuidolinTimmermann2007], which provides the state variable $s_t$ on which both portfolio weights and the alpha expectation of the signals can be conditioned.

## Market States and Momentum

The first result concerns the dependence of momentum profit on the past market state. A momentum strategy is constructed relative to Jegadeesh and Titman [JegadeeshTitman1993]: at the start of each month securities are ranked by cumulative return over the previous six months (months $t-6$ to $t-1$, skipping month $t$), the decile of winners is bought and that of losers is sold, held for the following six months. The momentum profit $\pi_t$ is the return of this winner-minus-loser portfolio, measured both raw and risk-adjusted with the Fama and French three-factor model [FamaFrench1993].

The **market state** is defined ex ante by the sign of the market's cumulative past return — the return of the CRSP value-weighted index — over a long horizon, typically 36 months, though the results also hold at 12 and 24 months. Denoting by $R^{m}_{t-1-J,\,t-1}$ the market return over the $J$ months preceding formation,
$$\text{state}_t = \begin{cases} \text{UP} & \text{if } R^{m}_{t-1-J,\,t-1} \ge 0,\\[2pt] \text{DOWN} & \text{if } R^{m}_{t-1-J,\,t-1} < 0. \end{cases}$$
The average profit conditional on the state is then estimated as the coefficients $\alpha_{\text{UP}}$ and $\alpha_{\text{DOWN}}$ of the regression
$$\text{CAR}_{t} = \alpha_{\text{UP}}\, \mathbb{1}_{\{\text{UP}\}} + \alpha_{\text{DOWN}}\, \mathbb{1}_{\{\text{DOWN}\}} + \varepsilon_t,$$
where $\text{CAR}_t=\sum_{k=K_1}^{K_2} r_{t+k}$ is the portfolio's cumulative return over the holding months $(K_1,K_2)$ and standard errors are corrected for heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation (HAC) to account for the overlap of the windows.

The empirical result is sharp and asymmetric: **momentum is profitable only after rising markets**. In the first six-month holding period following an UP state, average profit is strong and positive — about $0{,}93\%$ per month raw ($t\approx 8{,}4$) and about $1{,}12\%$ CAPM-adjusted — while after a DOWN state it is null or slightly negative — about $-0{,}37\%$ raw and $0{,}01\%$ adjusted, statistically indistinguishable from zero. The difference between the two states is significant ($t\approx 2{,}3$) [CooperGutierrezHameed2004]. The cross-sectional selection signal that earlier chapters treated as always profitable actually has a premium concentrated in a single regime. The relationship with the market's past return is also non-linear: regressing profit on the lagged market return and its square, the linear coefficient is positive and the quadratic one negative, so profit rises moving from negative to moderately positive states but attenuates at the bullish extremes.

The second result concerns the long horizon. The profit realized after UP states **reverses**: over holding months 13 to 60, the winner-minus-loser portfolio that follows a bullish state returns about $-0{,}36\%$ per month raw ($t\approx -3{,}2$). This combination — short-term momentum followed by long-term reversal — is the empirical signature of behavioral **overreaction** theories, following the long-run evidence of De Bondt and Thaler [DeBondtThaler1985]: models of self-attribution and overconfidence [DanielHirshleiferSubrahmanyam1998] and of gradual information diffusion [HongStein1999] predict that short-term price continuation and its subsequent correction are most intense precisely when aggregate sentiment, approximated by the past market state, is high.

The state dependence survives the controls that a risk-based reading would require. Comparing the lagged market return with a macroeconomic factor model — dividend yield, default spread, term spread, and yield spread [ChordiaShivakumar2002] — as predictors of momentum profits, the lagged market return turns out to be the best out-of-sample predictor, while the macro variables do not significantly reduce the forecast error [CooperGutierrezHameed2004]. Since the market state is not a simple reflection of an omitted business-cycle variable and does not predict the market's future return in a compensating way, the momentum premium conditioned on the UP state remains difficult to rationalize as compensation for systematic risk.

## Momentum Crashes and Short Optionality

The state dependence of momentum has a dramatic counterpart in the tail of its distribution. The winner-minus-loser (WML) strategy, despite having a high average return — on the order of 17.9% annually, with a Sharpe ratio higher than the market's and an unconditional CAPM alpha of about 22% annually — suffers infrequent but extreme **crashes** (momentum crashes), concentrated in clearly identifiable phases [DanielMoskowitz2016]. The monthly distribution of WML is strongly left-skewed and its worst returns cluster in a few episodes: of the fifteen worst months of the strategy, fourteen occur when the market's return over the preceding two years is negative.

Crashes occur in **panic states**: after sharp market declines — when the market is in a bear condition, measured by a negative two-year cumulative past return — and under a high-volatility regime, materializing in conjunction with market **rebounds**. The emblematic historical episodes are the summer of 1932 and the months following the March 2009 low: in July-August 1932 the loser portfolio returned about $+232\%$ against $+32\%$ for the winners; in March-May 2009 the losers returned about $+163\%$ against $+8\%$ for the winners. In both cases, after a prolonged decline, the market's violent rebound produced enormous losses for the WML portfolio — the worst month, August 1932, showing about $-74\%$ — because the past losers, heavily depreciated and distressed securities, gained far more than the winners in the recovery [DanielMoskowitz2016].

The mechanism is the **state dependence of the betas** of the WML portfolio. In bull markets the portfolio has a modest market beta; in bear markets, the short leg (the losers) acquires an exposure similar to that of a call option on the market, because the residual net worth of distressed firms reacts convexly to a recovery, consistent with the option-theoretic reading of equity as an option on firm value [Merton1974]. The WML portfolio thus finds itself **short this optionality**: it has a negative beta that becomes strongly negative when the market, starting from a bear state, rebounds. This is formalized with a state-conditioned beta regression,
$$\tilde{R}_{\text{WML},t} = \big[\alpha_0 + \alpha_B\, \mathbb{1}_{B,t-1}\big] + \big[\beta_0 + \mathbb{1}_{B,t-1}\big(\beta_B + \mathbb{1}_{U,t}\,\beta_{B,U}\big)\big]\, \tilde{R}_{m,t} + \tilde{\varepsilon}_t,$$
where $\mathbb{1}_{B,t-1}$ indicates the bear-market state (negative two-year cumulative market return) and $\mathbb{1}_{U,t}$ indicates that the contemporaneous market is rising. The estimate gives a base beta $\beta_0 \approx -0{,}03$, a deterioration in the bear state $\beta_B \approx -0{,}66$ (strongly significant), and, above all, a markedly negative interaction term $\beta_{B,U} \approx -0{,}82$: in the bear state, when the market rises, the WML beta is strongly negative, so that the portfolio's up-market beta (about $-1{,}5$) is far more negative than its down-market beta (about $-0{,}7$). The strategy thus loses precisely on rebounds [DanielMoskowitz2016]. It is this short optionality that generates the left skew and the crashes.

The decisive practical consequence is that, since crashes depend on ex ante observable variables — the bear state and realized volatility — they are **partly predictable** and hence manageable. Regressing future momentum returns on the bear-market indicator and on expected market variance — measured as the realized variance of daily market returns over the preceding 126 days (about six months) — yields predictive power: the combination of bear state and higher volatility signals low expected momentum returns and a thick left tail, with a substantial share of the variance of WML returns explained by the state [DanielMoskowitz2016].

## Dynamic Weighting of Momentum

The predictability of crashes translates into a rule for **dynamic weighting** of the strategy. If the investor is assumed to control the scale of the WML position to maximize a mean-variance utility, the optimal weight is proportional to the ratio of the conditional mean to the conditional variance of the strategy's return:
$$w^{*}_{t-1} = \frac{1}{2\lambda}\, \frac{\mu_{t-1}}{\sigma_{t-1}^{2}},$$
where $\lambda$ is a time-invariant scalar governing the risk level, $\mu_{t-1}=\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[R_{\text{WML},t}]$ is the conditional forecast of the momentum return, and $\sigma_{t-1}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[(R_{\text{WML},t}-\mu_{t-1})^{2}]$ its conditional variance [DanielMoskowitz2016]. Two ingredients feed the formula. The first is **volatility scaling**: estimating $\sigma_{t-1}^{2}$ from recent realized variance gives the constant-volatility variant $w_{t-1} \propto \sigma_{\text{target}}/\sigma_{t-1}$, which reduces exposure when momentum volatility is high — that is, precisely in panic states. The second is the **mean forecast** $\mu_{t-1}$, made a function of the bear-market indicator and of expected variance, so as to further reduce (or invert) the position when the state signals a negative expected return.

Applied historically, the dynamic momentum strategy strongly dampens crashes and substantially improves risk-adjusted performance: the Sharpe ratio rises from about $0{,}6$ for the constant-weight strategy to about $1{,}0$ for volatility scaling alone and to about $1{,}2$ for the full dynamic strategy, with an improvement that persists across subperiods and across multiple asset classes [DanielMoskowitz2016]. The message for cross-sectional selection is that the momentum signal must be used with an intensity conditioned on the state: it is not a static alpha to be chased with fixed weight, but an alpha whose optimal exposure is a function of the market state and of volatility, consistent with the logic of dynamic signal-exposure management in the chapters [[11 Decadimento del segnale, orizzonte e turnover]] and [[13 Selezione e costruzione quantitativa]].

## The Regime-Dependent Value Premium

The second major cross-sectional signal, value, also has a premium that depends on the regime. The reference model is a two-state Markov chain on the returns of book-to-market-sorted portfolios, with time-varying transition probabilities à la Perez-Quiros and Timmermann [PerezQuirosTimmermann2000] and estimated by maximum likelihood in the tradition of Hamilton's filter [Hamilton1989; GulenXingZhang2011]. Let $s_{t}\in\{1,2\}$ be the latent state; the excess return of a portfolio follows
$$r_{t} = \beta_{0,s_t} + \beta_{1,s_t}\,TB_{t-1} + \beta_{2,s_t}\,DEF_{t-1} + \beta_{3,s_t}\,\Delta M_{t-2} + \beta_{4,s_t}\,DIV_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t\sim N\!\big(0,\sigma^2_{s_t}\big),$$
where $TB$ is the one-month Treasury bill rate, $DEF$ the default spread (Baa minus Aaa bonds), $\Delta M$ the growth rate of the monetary base (lagged two months), and $DIV$ the market's dividend yield. Both the intercepts $\beta_{0,s_t}$, the loadings $\beta_{k,s_t}$ on the macro variables, and the variance $\sigma^2_{s_t}$ depend on the state; the transition probabilities in turn depend on the short-term rate, $p_t=\Phi(\pi_0+\pi_1 TB_{t-1})$ and $q_t=\Phi(\pi_0+\pi_2 TB_{t-1})$, with $\Phi$ the normal CDF. **State 1** is the **high-volatility** regime, with volatility roughly double that of state 2 and associated with recessions; state 2 is the low-volatility regime of expansions. A joint bivariate specification models a growth decile and a value decile together with state-dependent correlation $\Sigma_{ij,s_t}=\rho_{s_t}\,\sigma_{i,s_t}\sigma_{j,s_t}$.

The central result is an **asymmetry of sensitivity between value and growth concentrated in the high-volatility state**. In state 1 the expected returns of value load on macro conditions much more than those of growth: on the default spread the value loading is about $7{,}76$ against $4{,}60$ for growth, and on the short-term rate about $-10{,}76$ against $-6{,}74$; in state 2 both loadings are small and insignificant [GulenXingZhang2011]. The test of the hypothesis $\beta^{G}_{k,1}-\beta^{G}_{k,2}=\beta^{V}_{k,1}-\beta^{V}_{k,2}$ is rejected: the variation in coefficients between the two states is significantly larger for value. Since state 1 coincides with economic deterioration, it follows that the **value premium is counter-cyclical and time-varying**: the expected value-minus-growth differential is about $12{,}4\%$ annually conditional on the high-volatility state versus about $0{,}6\%$ in the low-volatility state, while the average expected premium is about $0{,}39\%$ monthly and is positive in about 73% of months [GulenXingZhang2011].

The economic interpretation is of the downside-risk type: in bad times the fundamentals of value firms are hit disproportionately due to greater operating leverage, costly asset reversibility, and tighter financial constraints, so that the higher expected return of value is compensation for this heightened risk in adverse phases [Zhang2005; GulenXingZhang2011]. The non-linearity of the regime framework is essential: a linear predictive regression does not capture the peaks in expected premium that the Markov model identifies. This justifies, on the value side, the same conclusion that momentum imposed: the signal's premium is a state-conditioned quantity, not a constant to be plugged as-is into the fundamental law of active management of the chapter [[12 Dal segnale all'alpha - information coefficient e la legge fondamentale]].

## The Risk-Based Mechanism: Counter-Cyclical Betas

The dependence of the value premium on the cycle finds a foundation in a conditional CAPM with time-varying beta [PetkovaZhang2005]. The hypothesis is that a portfolio's market beta is a linear function of state variables describing the phase of the cycle:
$$\beta_{i,t} = b_{i0} + b_{i1}\, \text{DIV}_{t} + b_{i2}\, \text{DEF}_{t} + b_{i3}\, \text{TERM}_{t} + b_{i4}\, \text{TB}_{t},$$
where $\text{DIV}$ is the market's dividend yield, $\text{DEF}$ the default spread (Moody's Baa minus Aaa), $\text{TERM}$ the term spread, and $\text{TB}$ the short-term Treasury bill rate. The same variables approximate the **expected market premium** $\gamma_{t}=E_{t}[r_{m,t+1}-r_{f}]$, obtained by projecting the market's excess return on them: $\gamma_{t}=\delta_0+\delta_1\text{DIV}_t+\delta_2\text{DEF}_t+\delta_3\text{TERM}_t+\delta_4\text{TB}_t$. The expected premium serves as a proxy for the state of the cycle: $\gamma_{t}$ is high in bad times (recessions) and low in good times.

The empirical result is the signature of the risk-based explanation: **value stocks' betas covary positively with the expected market premium** — rising in bad times, when $\gamma_t$ is high — while **growth stocks' betas covary negatively** with $\gamma_t$ — falling in bad times [PetkovaZhang2005]. Value betas are thus counter-cyclical, growth betas pro-cyclical. Concretely, the conditional beta of the HML portfolio moves from negative values at cycle peaks to positive values at troughs, with a peak-trough difference on the order of $0{,}5$–$0{,}7$. This is exactly the pattern that an asset pricing model with a time-varying risk premium requires for value to be riskier: a security whose beta is high precisely when the price of risk is high commands a higher expected return.

The quantitative scope of the mechanism can be read from the decomposition of the unconditional expected return. Starting from the conditional CAPM $E_{t}[r_{i,t+1}-r_f]=\beta_{i,t}\,\gamma_{t}$ and taking the unconditional expectation,
$$E\big[r_{i}-r_f\big] = \bar{\gamma}\,\bar{\beta}_{i} + \operatorname{Cov}\!\big(\beta_{i,t},\, \gamma_{t}\big) = \bar{\gamma}\,\bar{\beta}_{i} + \operatorname{Var}[\gamma_t]\,\omega_i,$$
where $\bar{\beta}_{i}$ and $\bar{\gamma}$ are the unconditional means and $\omega_i=\operatorname{Cov}(\beta_{i,t},\gamma_t)/\operatorname{Var}[\gamma_t]$ is the beta-premium sensitivity. The expected return decomposes into an average-beta term plus a **covariance term** between beta and the expected market premium. The estimate gives $\omega_i$ strongly positive for value (on the order of $18$–$20$) and negative for growth, consistent with the expected sign. However, for the value-minus-growth spread the **magnitude is modest**: the covariance term explains only part of the observed value premium (about $0{,}39\%$ monthly for HML) and is not sufficient to rationalize it, so the conditional CAPM alphas remain positive and significant [PetkovaZhang2005]. The chapter's contribution, however, remains the qualitative mechanism: conditional betas are the state-dependent channel through which the cycle enters the premium of value selection, in continuity with Lettau and Ludvigson's conditioning on the conditional CAPM [LettauLudvigson2001] and with Fama and French's definition of the factors [FamaFrench1992; FamaFrench1993].

## Common-Factor Structure and Funding-Liquidity Across Asset Classes

The preceding results concern equity securities; their generality emerges when value and momentum are studied simultaneously across many asset classes. Measuring a value signal and a momentum signal consistently in eight markets — individual equities of the United States, United Kingdom, continental Europe, and Japan, plus equity index futures, currencies, government bonds, and commodities, for a total of 48 test portfolios — positive and pervasive premia are found for both signals almost everywhere [AsnessMoskowitzPedersen2013]. In equities value is the book-to-market $BE/ME$ [FamaFrench1992; FamaFrench1993; LakonishokShleiferVishny1994] and momentum is the cumulative return of the last twelve months skipping the most recent one ($MOM2\text{-}12$) [JegadeeshTitman1993; Asness1994]; in the other classes value is the past five-year return with reversed sign, à la De Bondt-Thaler [DeBondtThaler1985], and momentum the twelve-month return.

The structural discovery is the **correlation of the signals across markets**: value in one market is positively correlated with value in other markets (average correlation on the order of $0{,}68$ among equity markets) and momentum with momentum elsewhere ($\approx 0{,}65$), while value and momentum are **negatively correlated** with each other (on the order of $-0{,}5$ within each class). A principal components analysis confirms a single latent factor loading with opposite signs on value and momentum, so there exist a global value factor and a global momentum factor that cut across asset class boundaries — a common-factor structure not explainable by local exposures [AsnessMoskowitzPedersen2013]. The negative correlation makes their combination particularly powerful: the 50/50 combination of the two signals, diversified across all classes and rescaled to equal volatility, achieves a Sharpe ratio on the order of $1{,}45$, well above that of either signal in isolation. This is formalized in a **global three-factor model** — the market, an everywhere-value factor, and an everywhere-momentum factor,
$$R^{p}_{t}-r_{f,t} = \alpha^{p} + \beta^{p}\,MKT_t + v^{p}\,VAL^{\text{everywhere}}_t + m^{p}\,MOM^{\text{everywhere}}_t + \varepsilon^{p}_t,$$
which explains about $71\%$ of the cross-sectional variation of the 48 portfolios with average pricing errors on the order of 18 basis points per month [AsnessMoskowitzPedersen2013].

The commonality across markets calls for a shared risk source, and the candidate is the **funding-liquidity** state. Regressing the returns of the two signals on shocks to funding liquidity — approximated by the TED spread and other funding spreads (LIBOR-term repo, swap-Treasury), purged via an autoregressive model — it is found that **momentum loads positively on liquidity risk** while **value loads negatively** [AsnessMoskowitzPedersen2013]. The signal, barely distinguishable market by market, becomes sharp in the aggregate: the beta on the funding-liquidity shock is on the order of $-4{,}74$ (t-statistic) for value and $+3{,}58$ for momentum. Momentum tends to suffer in liquidity crises, when funding dries up and crowded positions are liquidated, and is consistently exposed to funding shocks in the sense of funding-liquidity theory [BrunnermeierPedersen2009]; value, with opposite-sign exposure, gains in those same phases. The combination of the two thus ends up **hedging** part of the funding-liquidity risk, which explains at once the high Sharpe ratio of the joint portfolio and the negative correlation between the signals. The shared macro-financial state — the availability of funding for arbitrageurs — is the common variable linking the premia of value and momentum coherently across asset classes, also in the tradition of asset-level liquidity risk [PastorStambaugh2003; AcharyaPedersen2005].

## The Shared Regime Engine Between Allocation and Selection

The last step closes the bridge with the chapter [[21 Regimi di mercato e formazione delle view]]: the state $s_t$ on which cross-sectional selection is conditioned can be the same state estimated by the regime-switching model that governs allocation across asset classes [GuidolinTimmermann2007]. The model is multivariate on the excess returns of large-cap equities, small-cap equities, and long-term bonds:
$$r_{t} = \mu_{s_{t}} + \varepsilon_{t}, \qquad \varepsilon_{t}\sim N\!\big(0,\, \Omega_{s_{t}}\big),$$
where $s_{t}\in\{1,\dots,k\}$ is a latent Markov chain with transition matrix $P=[p_{ij}]$, $p_{ij}=\Pr(s_{t}=j\mid s_{t-1}=i)$, and $\mu_{s_t}$, $\Omega_{s_t}$ are the state-dependent mean vector and covariance matrix. The structure is thus the one already encountered: means, volatilities, and — crucially in a multivariate setting — **correlations** change with the regime.

Maximum-likelihood estimation identifies **four distinct regimes**, labeled by their properties. The **crash state** has strongly negative means, the highest volatility, and, above all, **negative** stock-bond correlation (on the order of $-0{,}41$ between large caps and bonds), with an average duration of about two months; the **slow-growth state** has means close to zero, low volatility, positive correlations, and is persistent (about seven months); the **bull state** has positive means — higher for small caps — moderate volatility, and is the most persistent (about eight months); the **recovery state** has high positive means and very high volatility, of short duration (about three months). The stationary probabilities of the four states are on the order of $(9\%, 40\%, 28\%, 23\%)$ [GuidolinTimmermann2007]. The **sign-switching of correlations** is the key feature: bonds are diversifiers in the crash and lose hedging power in the other states, and the correlation between large and small caps falls from about $0{,}82$ in the crash to about $0{,}50$ in the recovery — a time-varying structure that an IID Markowitz-style model [Markowitz1952] cannot capture and that echoes the evidence on asymmetric correlations [AngChen2002]. Inference about the current state proceeds with Hamilton's recursive filter [Hamilton1989], which updates the filtered probabilities
$$\Pr(s_{t}=j\mid \mathcal{F}_{t}) \propto f\!\big(r_{t}\mid s_{t}=j\big)\sum_{i} p_{ij}\, \Pr(s_{t-1}=i\mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}),$$
and, ex post, with the smoothed probabilities. Maximizing a power utility with risk aversion $\gamma=5$ over multiple horizons, the **optimal portfolio weights differ markedly by regime** — maximum equity exposure in the bull, defensive in the crash — and generate intertemporal **hedging demands** arising from uncertainty about future state transitions, decomposing the demand for risky assets into a myopic component plus a hedging one [GuidolinTimmermann2007], in the tradition of intertemporal hedging demand theory [Merton1973].

The same model shows the direct link to cross-sectional selection: the **size premium** (small minus large) is strongly regime-dependent. It is positive and maximal in the crash state — on the order of $+100$ basis points per month, where small caps ($-4{,}10\%$) lose less than large caps ($-5{,}10\%$) — and also positive in the bull state — on the order of $+71$ basis points per month — while it is negative in the slow-growth state — on the order of $-61$ basis points per month, where large caps outperform small caps — with a spread of about $161$ basis points between the maximum-premium regime and the minimum one [GuidolinTimmermann2007]. The same vector of state probabilities $\pi_t$ that governs allocation across asset classes can thus condition selection within them: the size premium is not constant but concentrates in the bull and crash states and reverses in slow growth, where the optimal demand for small caps vanishes.

The unifying reading is as follows. The same object — the latent Markov chain $s_t$ with its transition matrix — that in the chapter [[21 Regimi di mercato e formazione delle view]] serves to form allocation views, here provides the state variable that conditions selection: in the UP, low-volatility state, momentum is profitable and should be weighted fully; in the bear, high-volatility state it should be reduced or reversed to avoid its crashes; in the adverse, high-volatility state the value premium widens; in the crash and bull states the size premium is positive and large. Cross-sectional selection in the chapters [[09 Selezione dei titoli su fondamentali]], [[12 Dal segnale all'alpha - information coefficient e la legge fondamentale]], and [[13 Selezione e costruzione quantitativa]] thus inherits from the regime engine its missing premise: the information coefficient of the signals is not a constant, but a function of the market state. Factor timing and factor rotation in the chapter [[27 Factor timing e rotazione dei fattori]] are its natural operational continuation.
