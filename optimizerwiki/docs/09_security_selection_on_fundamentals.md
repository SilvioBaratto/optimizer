> [!abstract] Summary
> The chapter shows how to narrow an investable universe to a subset of securities starting from financial statement information: it builds a taxonomy of ratios — profitability, liquidity, leverage, operating efficiency, valuation — and reads the role of multiples (P/E, price-to-book, EV/EBITDA) as value signals, together with the limits of comparing non-comparable firms. It further establishes that size and value are characteristics that order securities in the cross-section, generating alpha relative to the CAPM, and that such strategies are organized into self-financing, well-diversified factor portfolios, culminating in the Fama-French-Carhart specification.

## From the Investable Universe to a Narrower Set: the Financial Statements as Raw Material

Security selection starts from the information that firms periodically communicate to the investor community. Financial statements are accounting reports that describe a firm's past performance and that public companies publish on a quarterly and annual basis; they are the tool through which investors, analysts, and other outside parties obtain information about the corporation [BerkDeMarzo2019]. Every public company is required to produce four documents: the balance sheet, the income statement, the statement of cash flows, and the statement of changes in stockholders' equity [BerkDeMarzo2019].

The balance sheet provides a snapshot of the financial position at an instant, listing assets and liabilities; the two sides are equal by construction according to the balance sheet identity
$$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Stockholders' Equity}.$$
The difference between assets and liabilities is the book value of equity, an accounting measure of the firm's net worth. The income statement reports revenues and expenses over an interval of time and arrives, as its last line, at net income or earnings; its per-share expression is earnings per share,
$$\text{EPS} = \frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Shares Outstanding}}.$$
The statement of cash flows, finally, reconciles income with the cash actually generated, broken down into operating, investing, and financing activities [BerkDeMarzo2019].

Accounting standards — Generally Accepted Accounting Principles in the United States, International Financial Reporting Standards elsewhere — provide a common set of rules and a standard format, which makes it easier to compare the results of different firms [BerkDeMarzo2019]. It is on this standardized basis that ratio analysis is built. Investors use financial statements in two ways: comparing the firm with itself, analyzing how it has changed over time, or comparing it with similar firms using a common set of financial ratios [BerkDeMarzo2019]. Narrowing the investable universe means systematically applying one or the other criterion: securities are ranked and selected based on ratios that measure profitability, liquidity, working capital, interest coverage, financial leverage, valuation, and operating return [BerkDeMarzo2019].

It is essential, however, to keep in mind the limits of this raw material. Many assets are recorded at historical cost rather than at current value, and some valuable assets — employee expertise, reputation, relationships with customers and suppliers, the value of future innovations, quality of management — do not appear on the balance sheet at all [BerkDeMarzo2019]. For these reasons the book value of equity, however accurate from an accounting standpoint, is an imprecise measure of the true value of equity. The market value of equity, or market capitalization, instead equals the number of shares times the market price,
$$\text{Market Value of Equity} = \text{Shares outstanding} \times \text{Market price per share},$$
and does not depend on the historical cost of assets, but on what investors expect those assets to produce in the future [BerkDeMarzo2019].

## The Taxonomy of Financial Statement Ratios

Financial ratios can be organized into families, each of which illuminates a different dimension of the firm.

**Profitability.** The income statement provides the measures of profitability. The gross margin is the ratio of gross profit to revenue, $\text{Gross Margin} = \text{Gross Profit}/\text{Sales}$, and reflects the ability to sell a product for more than its cost of production. The operating margin, $\text{Operating Margin} = \text{Operating Income}/\text{Sales}$, measures how much the firm earns before interest and taxes for each unit of revenue; the EBIT margin is calculated analogously. Comparing operating or EBIT margins across firms in an industry assesses the relative efficiency of operations, while bearing in mind that differences in margins can also stem from differences in business strategy [BerkDeMarzo2019]. The net profit margin, $\text{Net Profit Margin} = \text{Net Income}/\text{Sales}$, indicates the fraction of each unit of revenue that remains for shareholders after interest and taxes; caution is required in comparing it, since differences can also arise from financial leverage and different accounting assumptions [BerkDeMarzo2019].

**Liquidity.** From the balance sheet one assesses short-term solvency. The current ratio compares current assets and current liabilities,
$$\text{Current Ratio} = \frac{\text{Current Assets}}{\text{Current Liabilities}}.$$
A stricter test is the quick ratio, which in the numerator considers only cash and near-cash assets (short-term investments and accounts receivable), excluding inventory because it might not be liquid; the cash ratio, which places only cash in the numerator, is the most stringent [BerkDeMarzo2019]. A higher current or quick ratio implies lower risk of a cash shortfall in the near future, but these ratios consider only current assets: a firm can be highly liquid even with modest ratios if it generates cash quickly from its operations [BerkDeMarzo2019].

**Operating Efficiency and Working Capital.** Combined income statement and balance sheet information measures the efficiency of working capital use. Accounts receivable days express receivables in terms of days of sales,
$$\text{Accounts Receivable Days} = \frac{\text{Accounts Receivable}}{\text{Average Daily Sales}},$$
and analogous ratios apply to accounts payable and inventory. Alternatively, turnover ratios express annual revenues or costs as a multiple of the relevant working-capital item, such as inventory turnover $= \text{Annual Cost of Sales}/\text{Inventory}$; a higher turnover corresponds to fewer days and hence more efficient use of working capital [BerkDeMarzo2019]. Asset turnover, $\text{Sales}/\text{Total Assets}$, measures how efficiently the firm uses its assets to generate sales.

**Interest Coverage.** Creditors assess the ability to meet financial charges using interest coverage ratios, typically EBIT as a multiple of interest expense; a high ratio indicates that the firm earns much more than it needs to pay interest [BerkDeMarzo2019]. Since depreciation and amortization are deducted in EBIT even though they are not cash outlays, analysts often compute EBITDA,
$$\text{EBITDA} = \text{EBIT} + \text{Depreciation and Amortization},$$
as a measure of the cash the firm generates from operations to pay interest [BerkDeMarzo2019].

**Financial Leverage.** Leverage is the extent to which the firm relies on debt as a source of financing. The debt-equity ratio is $\text{Total Debt}/\text{Total Equity}$ and can be computed using either book or market values for equity; since the book value of equity is difficult to interpret — and can even be negative, rendering the ratio meaningless — it is more informative to compare debt with the market value of equity [BerkDeMarzo2019]. Also defined are the debt-to-capital ratio, $\text{Total Debt}/(\text{Total Equity} + \text{Total Debt})$, and net debt, that is, debt in excess of cash reserves,
$$\text{Net Debt} = \text{Total Debt} - \text{Excess Cash \& Short-term Investments};$$
a firm with more cash than debt could immediately retire its debt and therefore has no effective leverage [BerkDeMarzo2019]. The equity multiplier, measured in book values as $\text{Total Assets}/\text{Book Value of Equity}$, captures the amplification of accounting returns produced by leverage.

**Operating Return.** Return on investment is assessed by relating income to invested capital. Return on equity is $\text{ROE} = \text{Net Income}/\text{Book Value of Equity}$; return on assets, $\text{ROA} = (\text{Net Income} + \text{Interest Expense})/\text{Book Value of Assets}$, includes interest in the numerator because assets are financed by both debt and equity, and is less sensitive to leverage than ROE [BerkDeMarzo2019]. Return on invested capital,
$$\text{ROIC} = \frac{\text{EBIT}\,(1 - \text{tax rate})}{\text{Book Value of Equity} + \text{Net Debt}},$$
measures the after-tax profit generated by the business alone, and is the most useful of the three for assessing the performance of the underlying operations [BerkDeMarzo2019].

**The DuPont Decomposition.** ROE can be decomposed via the DuPont Identity, which expresses it as a function of profitability, asset efficiency, and leverage:
$$\text{ROE} = \underbrace{\frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Sales}}}_{\text{Net Profit Margin}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Sales}}{\text{Total Assets}}}_{\text{Asset Turnover}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Total Assets}}{\text{Book Value of Equity}}}_{\text{Equity Multiplier}}.$$
The first factor measures overall profitability, the second the efficiency with which assets generate sales; their product yields return on assets, and multiplying by the equity multiplier — a measure of leverage — leads to ROE [BerkDeMarzo2019]. The decomposition shows how two firms with similar margins can have different ROEs owing to different asset turnover and leverage.

## Multiples as Value Signals

Valuation ratios relate market value to measures of a firm's scale and are used to judge whether a security is expensive or cheap, as well as for comparisons within an industry.

**Market-to-Book (Price-to-Book).** The ratio of market capitalization to the book value of equity is the market-to-book ratio, also called price-to-book (P/B):
$$\text{Market-to-Book Ratio} = \frac{\text{Market Value of Equity}}{\text{Book Value of Equity}}.$$
For most successful firms this ratio well exceeds one, indicating that the value of the assets put to use exceeds their historical cost; its variations reflect differences in the firm's fundamental characteristics and the value added by management [BerkDeMarzo2019]. Analysts classify firms with low market-to-book as value stocks and those with high market-to-book as growth stocks [BerkDeMarzo2019].

**Price-Earnings.** The most common valuation multiple is the price-earnings ratio (P/E), the ratio of the value of equity to earnings, on a total or per-share basis:
$$\text{P/E Ratio} = \frac{\text{Market Capitalization}}{\text{Net Income}} = \frac{\text{Share Price}}{\text{Earnings per Share}}.$$
The underlying idea is that, by buying a share, one acquires the rights to the firm's future earnings: since differences in the scale of earnings tend to persist, one is willing to pay proportionally more for a security with higher current earnings [BerkDeMarzo2019]. The P/E has a foundation in the constant-growth dividend discount model, whose first formal derivation dates to Williams [Williams1938]. Starting from $P_0 = Div_1/(r_E - g)$ and dividing by expected earnings $EPS_1$ one obtains the forward P/E:
$$\text{Forward P/E} = \frac{P_0}{EPS_1} = \frac{Div_1/EPS_1}{r_E - g} = \frac{\text{Dividend Payout Rate}}{r_E - g}.$$
One distinguishes the forward P/E, computed on expected earnings over the next twelve months, from the trailing P/E, based on earnings over the previous twelve months; for valuation purposes the forward is preferred, since what matters is future earnings [BerkDeMarzo2019]. The formula shows that, if two securities have the same payout, the same EPS growth rate, and equivalent risk — and hence the same cost of capital — they should have the same P/E; and that high-growth firms and industries, able to generate cash well beyond investment needs and hence to maintain high payouts, should have high P/Es [BerkDeMarzo2019]. Indeed P/Es vary widely across industries and are higher where expected growth is high; other things equal, riskier firms have lower P/Es [BerkDeMarzo2019].

**Enterprise Value Multiples.** Since the P/E considers the value of equity, it is sensitive to the choice of leverage and is therefore of limited use in comparing firms with markedly different leverage [BerkDeMarzo2019]. This limitation can be overcome by valuing the underlying business through the enterprise value,
$$\text{Enterprise Value} = \text{Market Value of Equity} + \text{Debt} - \text{Cash},$$
which measures the value of the business net of cash and independently of debt. Since enterprise value represents the value of the entire firm before debt payments, an appropriate multiple divides it by a measure of earnings or flows before interest: enterprise value to EBIT, to EBITDA, and to free cash flow are typically considered. But since capital expenditures can vary widely from one period to the next, most practitioners favor the enterprise value to EBITDA multiple [BerkDeMarzo2019]. This multiple, too, has a foundation in the constant-growth model for flows: if the expected growth of free cash flow is constant,
$$\frac{V_0}{EBITDA_1} = \frac{FCF_1/EBITDA_1}{r_{wacc} - g_{FCF}},$$
and it is higher the higher the growth and the lower the capital requirements [BerkDeMarzo2019]. Other multiples are possible — enterprise value to revenue, when it is reasonable to assume similar future margins, or price to book value for firms with substantial tangible assets, or industry-specific multiples [BerkDeMarzo2019].

**Consistency of Aggregates.** When constructing a multiple, the numerator and denominator must both refer either to the entire firm or to shareholders alone. Price and market capitalization are equity quantities and should be related to EPS or net income; revenue, operating income, and EBITDA instead belong to the entire firm, and are correctly compared with enterprise value [BerkDeMarzo2019]. Finally, P/E and EBIT or EBITDA multiples are not meaningful when earnings are negative: in that case one often looks at enterprise value to revenue, though with the risk that earnings are negative because the business model is fundamentally flawed [BerkDeMarzo2019].

## The Limits of Comparing Non-Comparable Firms

The use of multiples is an application of the method of comparables: rather than discounting the firm's flows directly, its value is estimated from that of other comparable firms expected to generate very similar flows [BerkDeMarzo2019]. The multiple serves to adjust for differences in scale: just as one would value a building from the price per square meter of similar buildings recently sold, so a share is valued by substituting for square footage an appropriate measure of the firm's scale [BerkDeMarzo2019]. Concretely, the value of a security is estimated by multiplying its current earnings per share by the average P/E of comparable firms, under the assumption that the firm being valued has future risk, payout, and growth similar to those of the comparables [BerkDeMarzo2019].

The weak point of the method is that identical firms do not exist. If the comparables were identical, their multiples would coincide exactly; since they are not, the usefulness of a multiple depends on the nature of the differences between firms and on the sensitivity of the multiple to those differences [BerkDeMarzo2019]. Industry evidence shows this clearly. Comparing the multiples of a set of footwear companies, one observes for every multiple a significant dispersion around the mean: even though the enterprise value to EBITDA multiple is the least variable, not even it yields a precise estimate of value [BerkDeMarzo2019]. Differences among multiples reflect differences in expected growth rates, in profitability, in risk — and hence in the cost of capital — and sometimes in the accounting conventions adopted in different countries [BerkDeMarzo2019]. Investors understand that these differences exist and price securities accordingly; but when valuing with multiples there is no clear indication of how to adjust for them, other than narrowing the set of comparables [BerkDeMarzo2019].

This yields the underlying limitation: the comparables approach does not account for important differences between firms. Exceptional management, an efficient production process, a patent on a new technology are all factors ignored when a multiple is applied [BerkDeMarzo2019]. A second limitation is that multiples provide only relative information: they say how much a firm is worth relative to others in the comparison set, but do not help establish whether an entire industry is overvalued — a problem that became acute during the Internet boom of the late 1990s, when new multiples were created to justify the values of firms with no positive cash flows or earnings [BerkDeMarzo2019].

For these reasons, the use of multiples should be understood as a shortcut relative to discounted cash flow methods: rather than separately estimating the cost of capital and future flows, one relies on the valuation the market assigns to other firms with similar prospects [BerkDeMarzo2019]. In exchange for simplicity and grounding in actual prices, one gives up the ability — proper to discounted cash flow methods — to incorporate firm-specific information and to conduct sensitivity analysis. No single technique provides a definitive answer about the true value of a security; practitioners use a combination of approaches and draw comfort from their convergence [BerkDeMarzo2019].

## Prices, Information, and the Limits to Active Selection

Fundamentals-based security selection runs up against a fact: the market price of a publicly traded firm already incorporates very accurate information, aggregated from a multitude of investors, about the true value of the shares [BerkDeMarzo2019]. When a buyer wants to purchase a security, the willingness of others to sell it signals that they value it differently; the information that others are willing to trade induces buyers and sellers to revise their own estimates, until a consensus is reached. In this way markets aggregate the information and opinions of many investors [BerkDeMarzo2019]. It follows that, if our valuation model disagrees with the price, this is more likely a sign of an error in our assumptions than of a mispriced security [BerkDeMarzo2019].

The idea that competition among investors eliminates all positive-NPV trading opportunities is the efficient markets hypothesis: securities are correctly priced based on their future cash flows, given available information [BerkDeMarzo2019]. Competition is more intense, and the hypothesis holds better, when information is public and easy to interpret: the price reacts almost instantaneously, and few investors manage to trade before the adjustment is complete. When instead information is private or difficult to interpret, those who hold it can profit from it, and prices reflect it only gradually [BerkDeMarzo2019].

This framework sets the stakes of active selection. An investor can identify positive-NPV opportunities only in the presence of a barrier to free competition — expertise or access to information known to few, or lower transaction costs than others — and in any case the source of the advantage must be difficult to replicate, otherwise the gains would be eroded [BerkDeMarzo2019]. In an efficient market, those without a competitive advantage will not find positive-NPV opportunities, but the average investor can still invest with confidence because they receive flows that fairly compensate the risk taken [BerkDeMarzo2019]. Verifying efficiency, however, requires a theory of how risk determines expected returns: the efficient markets hypothesis, expressed in terms of returns, states that securities with equivalent risk have the same expected return, and is therefore incomplete until equivalent risk is defined [BerkDeMarzo2019]. It is precisely in the choice of this risk measure that the question of whether fundamental characteristics of securities generate genuine excess return is decided.

## Style-Based Techniques: Size and Value in the Cross-Section

Many managers distinguish their strategies based on the types of securities they tend to hold: small versus large stocks, value versus growth [BerkDeMarzo2019]. If the CAPM held, the market portfolio would be efficient and no strategy could beat the market without taking on additional risk; the difference between a security's expected return and the return required along the security market line is the security's alpha,
$$\alpha_s = E[R_s] - r_s, \qquad r_s = r_f + \beta_s\,(E[R_{Mkt}] - r_f),$$
and is zero for all securities when the market portfolio is efficient [BerkDeMarzo2019]. Some fundamental characteristics, however, order securities in the cross-section in a way that produces systematically nonzero alphas.

**The Size Effect.** Small-capitalization stocks have historically achieved average returns higher than those of the market portfolio; and, despite having high betas, their returns appear high even after accounting for the higher beta — an empirical finding known as the size effect [BerkDeMarzo2019]. Sorting securities each year by market capitalization and grouping them into ten decile portfolios, and recording their monthly excess returns, one observes that nearly all portfolios lie above the security market line, with the effect most pronounced in the smallest deciles; a joint test that all alphas are zero is statistically rejected [BerkDeMarzo2019]. The size effect was first identified in 1981 by Banz [Banz1981].

**The Value Effect.** Similar results are obtained using the book-to-market ratio, the ratio of book value to the market value of equity, to form portfolios. Value stocks — those with high book-to-market — tend to have positive alphas, while growth stocks — low book-to-market — have low or negative alphas; here too the joint test of zero alphas is rejected [BerkDeMarzo2019]. Systematic evidence on the relationship between risk-adjusted return and market value is documented by Fama and French [FamaFrench1992].

**A Theoretical Explanation.** After the publication of Banz's study, a theoretical reason emerged linking capitalization and expected returns: as long as beta is not a perfect measure of risk — due to estimation error or because the market portfolio is not efficient — one should expect to observe the size effect [Berk1997]. The argument is that a security with positive alpha has, other things equal, a higher expected return; the only way to offer a higher expected return is to buy the same stream of dividends at a lower price; a lower price means lower market capitalization (and higher book-to-market). Therefore a portfolio of low-capitalization, or high book-to-market, securities collects securities with higher expected returns and, if the market is not efficient, positive alphas [BerkDeMarzo2019]. Consider two firms with the same perpetual dividend stream of $1$ million: if the first has a cost of capital of $14\%$ and the second of $10\%$, their market values are $1/0{,}14 = \$7{,}143$ million and $1/0{,}10 = \$10$ million respectively. If both are assigned, by error or inefficiency, the same beta to which the CAPM attaches a required return of $12\%$, the lower-value firm has alpha $\alpha = 0{,}14 - 0{,}12 = 2\%$ and the higher-value firm $\alpha = 0{,}10 - 0{,}12 = -2\%$: the firm with lower capitalization has the higher alpha [BerkDeMarzo2019].

**Momentum.** Past returns also order securities. Ranking securities each month by their realized return over the previous $6$–$12$ months, the best-performing ones achieve positive alphas over the following $3$–$12$ months: a momentum strategy that buys securities with high past returns and shorts those with low past returns would have produced, over the period $1965$–$1989$, an alpha exceeding $12\%$ annually [JegadeeshTitman1993].

**The Caution of Data Snooping.** At the time of discovery, many researchers did not find the evidence convincing, because searching among a large set of characteristics it is always possible, by pure chance, to find one correlated with the estimation error of average returns: this is data snooping bias [BerkDeMarzo2019]. The topic is taken up in [[10 Validazione, data snooping e overfitting]]. The existence of positive-alpha strategies leaves only two conclusions: either investors systematically ignore positive-NPV opportunities, or such strategies contain a risk that investors do not want to bear and that the CAPM does not capture [BerkDeMarzo2019]. Since these strategies have long been known, the information to build them is readily available, and many funds already follow them, the first conclusion appears implausible; the second remains, namely that the market portfolio is not efficient and that beta with respect to the market does not adequately measure systematic risk [BerkDeMarzo2019]. Possible reasons include proxy error in the observed market portfolio, systematic behavioral biases of some investors, and exposure to non-tradable risks outside the portfolio, foremost among them human capital [BerkDeMarzo2019]. The limits of the CAPM as a risk model are also discussed in [[05 Modelli fattoriali]].

## Building Factor Portfolios

If the market portfolio is not efficient, computing the expected return requires an alternative method for identifying an efficient portfolio. Directly identifying efficient portfolios is difficult, because expected return and standard deviation cannot be measured precisely; two characteristics of the efficient portfolio are known, however: it is well diversified, and it can be constructed from other well-diversified portfolios [BerkDeMarzo2019]. This latter observation is crucial: it implies that it is not necessary to identify the efficient portfolio itself, but only to identify a collection of well-diversified portfolios — factor portfolios — from which an efficient portfolio could be constructed, in order to use the collection itself to measure risk [BerkDeMarzo2019].

With $N$ factor portfolios of returns $R_{F1}, \dots, R_{FN}$ the expected return of security $s$ is
$$E[R_s] = r_f + \sum_{n=1}^{N} \beta_s^{Fn}\,(E[R_{Fn}] - r_f),$$
where the factor betas have the same interpretation as beta in the CAPM: each measures the expected percentage change in the security's excess return for a $1\%$ change in the excess return of the factor portfolio, holding the other exposures constant [BerkDeMarzo2019]. A security's risk premium is thus written as the sum of each factor's premium multiplied by the security's sensitivities to those factors. When a single efficient portfolio is used the model is single-factor; with multiple portfolios it is a multifactor model, also known as Arbitrage Pricing Theory [BerkDeMarzo2019].

**Self-Financing Portfolios.** The expected premium of each factor, $E[R_{Fn}] - r_f$, is the expected return of a portfolio in which funds are borrowed at rate $r_f$ to invest in the factor portfolio. Since such a portfolio costs nothing to construct, it is called self-financing; one can also be obtained by going long some securities and short others of equal market value. In general, a self-financing portfolio is one whose weights sum to zero rather than to one [BerkDeMarzo2019]. Requiring that all factor portfolios be self-financing, the model is rewritten as
$$E[R_s] = r_f + \sum_{n=1}^{N} \beta_s^{Fn}\,E[R_{Fn}].$$
The cost of capital is thus computed without identifying the efficient portfolio, relying on the weaker condition that an efficient portfolio can be constructed from a collection of well-diversified portfolios [BerkDeMarzo2019].

**Choosing the Portfolios.** The first natural portfolio is the market itself: while not necessarily efficient, it has historically commanded a large premium over the risk-free rate and captures many components of systematic risk. A self-financing portfolio is therefore included, consisting of a long position in the market financed by a short position in the risk-free asset [BerkDeMarzo2019]. The other portfolios are built from the style strategies that appear to have positive alphas:

- *Size strategy.* Each year firms are split into two equally weighted portfolios based on the market value of equity — below ($S$) and above ($B$) the median of NYSE firms; a strategy that buys $S$ and finances the position by shorting $B$ is the small-minus-big (SMB) portfolio [FamaFrench1993].
- *Book-to-market strategy.* Each year firms with book-to-market below the 30th percentile form portfolio $L$ and those with book-to-market above the 70th percentile form portfolio $H$; a long position in $H$ financed by a short in $L$ is the high-minus-low (HML) portfolio, interpretable as long value and short growth [FamaFrench1993].
- *Past-returns strategy.* Each year securities are ranked by the previous year's return, going long the top 30% and short the bottom 30%; the resulting self-financing portfolio is the prior one-year momentum (PR1YR) portfolio [Carhart1997].

**The Fama-French-Carhart Specification.** The collection of four portfolios — the market's excess return $(Mkt - r_f)$, SMB, HML, and PR1YR — is the most widely used choice for the multifactor model, and the expected return of security $s$ is given by the Fama-French-Carhart specification:
$$E[R_s] = r_f + \beta_s^{Mkt}(E[R_{Mkt}] - r_f) + \beta_s^{SMB} E[R_{SMB}] + \beta_s^{HML} E[R_{HML}] + \beta_s^{PR1YR} E[R_{PR1YR}],$$
where the four factor betas measure the security's sensitivity to each portfolio [FamaFrench1993] [Carhart1997]. The portfolios were identified by Eugene Fama, Kenneth French, and Mark Carhart [BerkDeMarzo2019].

The advantage of the multifactor model is that it is far easier to identify a collection of portfolios that captures systematic risk than a single portfolio; the disadvantage is that the expected return of each portfolio must be estimated, and each added portfolio increases the difficulty of implementation [BerkDeMarzo2019]. Since it is not clear which economic risk each portfolio captures, one has no choice but to use historical average returns; and since the returns of the FFC portfolios are highly volatile, over eighty years of data are used to estimate their expected return, which remains imprecise nonetheless [BerkDeMarzo2019]. The link between self-financing portfolios and risk models is developed further in [[05 Modelli fattoriali]]; the role of estimation error in portfolio construction in [[08 Errore di stima, outlier e shrinkage]]. The one area where the FFC specification appears to improve markedly on the CAPM is measuring the risk of actively managed mutual funds: whereas under the CAPM funds with high past returns show positive alphas, repeating the test with the FFC specification reveals no evidence that such funds have positive future alphas [Carhart1997].
